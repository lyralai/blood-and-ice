# Chapter 1: Prologue

The sea lies flat for three days.

No wind. No chop. No white on the water. Heat presses the voices out of us until even Vell gives orders under his breath.

On the third day, the lookout sees a ship.

She comes out of the glare bow-first, sails hanging dead from the yards, hull black against the bright water. No wake trails her. No hand moves at her rail. She drifts without hurry, a black thing the sea has not yet decided to swallow.

I am sixteen, young enough to be sent where older men prefer not to go. I stand at the rail beside the mate and watch the stranger slide nearer.

I have been aboard the Mercy Bell for seven months and still own almost nothing: a spare shirt patched at both elbows, a bone comb with three teeth missing, and a copper button my sister pressed into my hand before I sailed. I keep the button tied inside my sleeve where dice and debt cannot find it. When the dead ship appears, my fingers go to that hidden lump without asking me.

"Salvage," Vell says.

He says it like a prayer.

The word moves through the crew. Men straighten. Fear loosens under the smell of profit. A dead ship can mean rot, sickness, pirates, or a curse. It can also mean cargo with no owner left to argue.

Rusk kisses two fingers and touches the little charm sewn to his cap. Tam grins too widely and says a dead captain spends no wages. No one laughs, but several men look toward Vell, and Vell looks at the dead ship like it has already made him richer.

My sister would have seen the lie in it. She was twelve when I left, all elbows and sharp eyes, clever enough to hear a trap before the hinge moved. Coll, she would say, nothing comes free across water. Then she would steal a sweet from the galley and ask whether curses paid in silver.

The thought almost makes me smile.

Vell chooses the boat himself. He takes Rusk the bosun, Tam with the steady hands, and me because someone has to bail.

We row across water smooth enough to hold both ships like mirrors. Heat sits on my neck. Sweat runs down my back. I have crossed these warm latitudes twice and have never once been cold on them.

I am cold before we reach the hull.

It comes in a breath over the water. At first it feels like shade. Then cellar damp. Then winter.

I look down and see my own breath smoke.

No one speaks for three strokes of the oars.

Rusk spits over the side. "Cold tide."

It is stupid. Everyone knows it. Everyone is grateful he has said anything.

We make the chains and climb.

The deck is neat.

The neatness is the first wrong sign. I have seen wrecks. A wreck tells its story openly: snapped rope, broken spars, blood dried where men have crawled, cargo spilled like guts. This ship tells nothing. Lines are coiled. A cup stands upright on a hatch cover, with a skin of frozen tea inside it. Frost clings to the brass near the wheel.

The captain's door stands open. Inside, a chair has been pushed back from a small table. A quill lies beside an uncorked inkpot, the ink black and frozen solid. On the table sit three coins stacked with a careful thumb's-width between each one, as if the captain has been counting them when the cold reaches him and means to come back after a breath.

My hand tightens on the doorframe.

Panic leaves mess. Storm leaves mess. Men leave mess when death enters a room.

This ship has been arranged by patience.

The galley gives the next.

The stove is cold, but not long cold. A pan sits on the iron with onions inside, white and hard as little stones. Four bowls wait beside it. A spoon lies on the floor below one of the benches. Someone dropped it and never bent to pick it up.

Tam lifts one bowl and makes a choked sound.

Inside, a child's milk teeth are set in a ring.

No child sits at the table. No small bones lie near the stove. Only the teeth: cleaned, placed in their little ring, saved for a counting no living mouth should know.

Vell takes the bowl from Tam and sets it down with a hand that is not steady.

"Cargo," he says.

No one answers.

The smell is worse.

There should be rot. In this heat, dead men swell and burst and turn the air sweet enough to taste.

There is no rot.

Only cold. Clean, dry, impossible cold.

We find the crew amidships, huddled in the lee of the longboat.

For one merciful second I think they are sleeping. Then Vell turns one over with his boot.

The man comes up light.

Too light.

He has a man's face. Stubble on the chin. A burst vein in one eye. A scar through the left brow. But the skin has drawn tight as gray paper, and beneath it there is nothing. No weight. No blood. No meat. A husk in the shape of a sailor.

Tam begins to pray under his breath.

I touch my sister's button through my sleeve. The copper is cold. It should have warmed under my skin. It has not.

Vell stares at the bodies and then toward the forward hatch. Greed fights fear in his face. Greed wins by a finger.

"Hold may still be full," he says.

I hear the sound from below.

Not a footstep. Not a voice.

A draw.

Long and slow. Like someone pulling breath through a pipe. Like thirst given lungs.

Cold pours up through the hatch.

I feel it reach me before I understand. Not against my skin. Inside my chest. The warmth in me leans toward the dark below as if water has found a drain.

"Off," I say.

The others do not move.

"Get off," I say, louder. "Get off the ship."

The dead shape rises from the hold.

It stands man-tall, but no man ever wore bones that way. Tall. Pale. Dry. Skin hauled tight as old sailcloth over lines that should hold life and do not. Its mouth is not a mouth. Its eyes are two holes where cold comes out.

It makes no cry. No roar. Only that slow drinking sound.

Rusk stands nearest.

The pale figure turns its face to him.

The bosun stops breathing. Frost crawls up his beard. His skin goes gray. His body empties while he stands there, warmth and blood and life drawn out through no wound I can see. Rusk folds to the deck as lightly as sailcloth.

His cap falls beside him. The little charm on it cracks with a sound no louder than a flea snapping between nails.

Tam goes over the rail. I hear the splash, then nothing.

Vell takes one step toward the hatch.

Even then, his eyes flick below. Cargo. Wages. A year's worth of wanting.

The cold takes him with his hand still half-raised toward profit.

I run.

The rail is gone from me.

The rope is gone from me.

No.

That is how men lie to themselves afterward, making memory clean because terror shames them.

I remember enough.

I remember slipping on frost and striking my chin hard enough to bite blood from my tongue. I remember the pale figure turning, slow because it does not need haste. I remember a sound leaving my own throat, thin as a gull's cry. I remember Tam in the water below, one hand raised for help and then curling in on itself as frost flowers open across his wet sleeve.

I remember leaving him.

I hit the boat wrong and crack my ribs on the thwart. The oars are gone. I slash the painter with Tam's knife because my own hands will not untie the knot. The rope parts. The dead ship slides away, or I slide away from it. The water between us looks flat enough to walk.

I lie in the bilge with no oars, no courage worth naming, and no memory I will want to keep.

My breath smokes for a long time.

Then, slowly, under the killing southern sun, it stops.

My own ship finds me near dusk. They ask what I saw. I tell them. The captain writes fever in the log because logs have room for fever and storm, not thirsting dead on warm seas.

They do not turn back for the salvage.

By morning the stranger has vanished into haze.

Three weeks later, Tam's brother comes aboard in Lys and asks why his kin has no grave. I try to answer. The words are in me, but men do not like impossible answers when grief has made them hungry. Tam's brother strikes me once, hard enough to split my lip. I let him. It is the least false answer either of us can give.

I dream of Tam's wet sleeve for years. Frost flowers open one by one, patient as hands, while the stranger slides into haze with all our names locked in its hold.

# Chapter 2: The Shore

Heat wakes me.

Not sunlight. Not fire. Heat with weight. It lies over my face and chest like a wet hand, presses the breath back into me, and fills my mouth with the taste of rot.

I do not move until the thing at my wrist bites deeper.

Mud holds my back. Roots press through my shirt. A small black insect crawls over my wrist, stops at the blood there, and begins to feed.

I open my eyes.

Green.

It hangs above me in layers so thick the sky is only a white glare behind leaves. Vines cross the branches like ropes on a ship. Water drips everywhere, slow and patient, though no rain is falling. The whole place breathes. I can hear it. Insects whining. Leaves ticking. Far off, something coughs once and goes quiet.

I remember a deck.

I remember hands.

I remember the sound men make when they do not care whether a boy lives, only whether he can still be sold.

Then the memories break apart. The sea has taken some. Pain has taken the rest.

I make an inventory because inventory keeps fear from taking all of me.

Right hand: slow, but it closes.

Left hand: better.

Teeth: three gone.

Mouth: blood.

Ribs: bruised, maybe cracked.

Knife: none.

That matters most.

I roll onto my side and vomit seawater into the mud. It comes up bitter and warm. Ants find it before I am done spitting. They come in a black thread from under a leaf, quick and certain, and take the red from the mud as if the jungle has sent them to collect what I owe.

I push myself to my knees.

The world swings. I wait it out with one hand locked around a root. The root is slick as an eel and strong as bone. When my sight clears, I see water below me.

Not sea. A brown channel, still on top, moving underneath. Roots reach down into it like fingers. A dark back slides just below the surface and leaves a line that opens and closes without a ripple.

I keep away from it.

Thirst scrapes the back of my throat. It wants the brown water. My empty body makes stupid arguments. I have learned to listen and then decide whether listening will kill me.

Rainwater first. Clear water if I can find it. Any fruit that does not burn the tongue. No standing brown water unless dying has already become the only other choice.

Those are not thoughts in words. Not at first. They are old lessons stored in scars and hunger.

I stand.

The heat hits harder upright. It fills my shirt, runs down my spine, gathers under my arms. I breathe through my mouth and taste flowers, mold, and dead meat nearby. Too much life packed too close becomes another kind of death.

I climb away from the channel, one hand on roots, one foot testing before weight. The mud wants my boots. Twice it takes one and I have to drag it free with both hands. A fly as long as my thumb lands on my cheek. I slap it and my palm comes away red. Mine or its. I cannot tell.

The jungle watches without eyes.

I find the first body under a fan of broad leaves.

He lies face-down, half in the mud, one arm twisted under him. I know him. Not his name. The ship had not left us names. Names are wasted on cargo. But I know his shoulders and the collar scar rubbed raw at his neck.

He was older than me. Stronger. He had cursed the slavers once in a language I did not know and lost two teeth for it.

Now the jungle has him.

White mushrooms grow from the corner of his mouth.

One hand is closed around mud. I pry the fingers open because hunger makes even the dead into questions. There is nothing there but a bit of leaf crushed to pulp. His nails are packed black. He died holding the island, and the island did not care enough to hold him back.

The hold taught silence around the dead. A man dies, the slaver kicks his feet apart, and everyone else looks small so death will not be counted as laziness. Still, I want a word.

Not a prayer. I have no god that ever answered.

Only his name.

I have no name for him.

That feels like another theft.

I crouch beside him and look for a knife. Belt first. Boots. Hands. The places men hide small blades and charms when they are afraid.

Nothing.

His pouch has a strip of dried meat wrapped in cloth so filthy I almost miss it. My mouth fills before my hand closes.

I eat too fast.

Halfway through the second bite, my stomach heaves. I force the meat down with both hands over my mouth because losing food is a sin the body does not forgive.

When the meat is gone, I search him again. Still no knife. No flint. No cord worth taking. Only a copper token on a string around his neck, green with sweat and salt.

I take it because the dead do not need luck and I have none.

A low call moves above me.

Not a bird. Too low. Too wet.

I freeze.

The sound comes again from the trees, a soft clacking that moves without moving. I look up and see nothing but leaves, shadow, and ropes of vine. Then one vine opens an eye.

Not a vine.

A snake, thick as my thigh, patterned green and black. Its head rests on a branch above the dead man's back. Its tongue tastes the air between us.

I step back very slowly.

The snake does not strike. It watches the body. It was here first, maybe. Or it was waiting for the jungle to finish softening him.

I leave it the man.

Mercy has nothing to do with it. A boy with no knife does not argue over meat with a snake that size.

By the time I find water, my tongue feels too large for my mouth.

A thread runs down stone where a tree has cracked the rock open, gathering in a cup no wider than my hand.

Clear.

I smell it. Touch it to my lip. Wait.

No burn. No bitter oil. No swamp skin.

I drink.

The first swallow hurts. The second becomes everything. I drink until the cup is empty, wait, drink again until my stomach cramps.

Only then do I sit back.

The rock is warm behind me. The jungle steams. My shirt clings to the cuts on my back.

I should be shaking by now.

I have seen swamp air take men apart: glassy eyes, cold sweat, hands shaking too hard to hold a spoon. Men stronger than me have died before morning.

But my hands are steady.

The bites on my arms swell, fade, then leave a deep itch under the skin. The cuts from the wreck sting, then tighten. The rot air goes into me and comes out again, and whatever poison rides in it does not find a place to sit. By the time my hands stop trembling, hunger has come back sharp enough to shame me.

That frightens me more than fever would have.

Fever I understand.

This obeys no law I know.

I touch the copper token at my neck. It is warm from my hand, stamped with a mark I cannot read. The dead boy kept it hidden under his shirt. Maybe it was his god. Maybe his mother. Maybe only a scrap he could call his own.

The mark might be a two-headed bird, a winged cup, some king's joke. I turn it three ways and it becomes three lies. Maybe the token only reminded him he had once been more than a price.

I tie it under my shirt anyway.

It knocks once against my chest when I move. A small sound. Almost company.

I have one piece like that.

A name.

I hold it where the slavers never looked. Behind the teeth they broke. Under the tongue they told me to keep still.

My name.

My mother gave it to me. Her face is blurred now, and that scares me in a deep place I rarely touch. I remember warmth. I remember a low voice. I remember being lifted once, before hands meant pain.

But I remember the name.

The slavers had another sound for me. A short ugly sound made in the back of the throat. It meant come here. It meant property. It meant a hand on my neck and a boot near my ribs.
