# Prose Tic Audit — *Blood and Ice*, Book One: *Wild Boy*

**Auditor:** Lyra (subagent prose pass)
**Date:** 2026-07-03
**Scope:** All 29 chapters (ch00–ch28), ~104,000 words
**Method:** Full read-through + grep-verified exact counts

---

## Executive Summary

The manuscript has one of the most distinctive first-person voices I've audited — a feral, concrete, non-abstract narration built on invented vocabulary and hyphenated compounds. That voice is the book's greatest asset. It is also, by the nature of its construction, its greatest vulnerability: because the vocabulary is invented, every re-use of a coined word reads louder than a re-use of standard English would. A reader who notices the machinery once will notice it every time.

The good news: the tics are mostly concentrated in the right places — the emotional lexicon, not the action sequences. The problem areas are specific and fixable: a handful of coined phrases cross the threshold from "established motif" into "tic the reader predicts," and the later chapters (18–25) show visible lexical clustering where the narrator's emotional vocabulary narrows instead of widening under pressure.

**Headline recommendation:** Do not touch the Tier 1 foundational voice. Thin the Tier 2 emotional lexicon by ~20–25%. aggressively cut the worst Tier 3 offenders. The goal is not to change the voice — it's to protect it from becoming predictable.

---

## Tier 1 Tics (Foundational — 50+ occurrences — do not cut)

These are the voice itself. They appear at frequencies that define the narration but do not fatigue because they function as basic vocabulary rather than stylistic flourishes.

### 1.1 — "the green" (jungle/environment)
- **Count:** ~680
- **Verdict:** KEEP. This is the narrator's word for his world. Frequency is inherent to first-person narration about a jungle setting.
- **Note:** Slight risk in later chapters where "the green" becomes almost metonymic for the island itself ("the green eats humans"). This usage is strong — keep it.

### 1.2 — "read/reading" (all perception)
- **Count:** ~425
- **Verdict:** KEEP. The central verb of the narrator's cognition — he "reads" tracks, weather, bodies, emotions, tribal politics. This is the most important word in the book.
- **Note:** Watch for sentences where "read" stacks twice in close proximity (happens ~15 times). Vary one instance to a concrete sensory verb.

### 1.3 — "lows" (days)
- **Count:** ~540
- **Verdict:** KEEP. Basic time vocabulary. Inherent.

### 1.4 — "I do not [verb]" (negation construction)
- **Count:** ~171
- **Verdict:** KEEP, but monitor. This is the narrator's characteristic negation pattern — "I do not move," "I do not go," "I do not know." It creates the deliberate, measured cadence. However, at 171 instances across 29 chapters (~6 per chapter), some chapters push 8–10, which risks the cadence becoming a drone.
- **Recommendation:** In chapters exceeding 7 instances, convert 2–3 to positive constructions ("I stay still" instead of "I do not move") or to fragment negation ("Not moving. Not going.").

### 1.5 — Em-dash usage
- **Count:** 842 across 29 chapters (~29/chapter average)
- **Range:** 14 (ch00) to 41 (ch11, ch23)
- **Verdict:** KEEP. High but consistent. The em-dash is the narrator's primary syntax for interruption, qualification, and layered thought. Chapters 11 and 23 run slightly hot (41 each); otherwise the distribution is even.
- **Recommendation:** No global change. If individually revising ch11 or ch23, consider converting 5–6 em-dashes to periods or commas for rhythmic variation.

### 1.6 — "thing/things" (generic noun)
- **Count:** ~1,374
- **Verdict:** KEEP. The narrator's default noun. This is a deliberate feature of his limited vocabulary — he calls everything "a thing" because he lacks specific words. Inherent to voice.

## Tier 2 Tics (Noticeable — 15 to 50 occurrences — thin by 20–25%)

These are the coined phrases and emotional markers that give the book its interiority. They are largely earned but have crossed the threshold where a careful reader will begin to anticipate them. Thinning these by roughly one in four to one in five instances will preserve their power.

---

### 2.1 — "lean/leaning" (emotional pull/desire)

- **Count:** 168
- **Distribution:** Extremely back-loaded. Chapters 1–8 average ~3. Chapters 18–23 average ~11. Ch20 alone has 12. Ch23 alone has **16**.
- **Verdict:** REDUCE BY ~30% in chapters 18–25. This is the single most overworked verb in the back half of the book.
- **The problem:** "Lean" becomes the narrator's only verb for wanting. He leans toward the fire, leans at the owned girl, leans out over water, leans into the trap, leans anyway. By ch23, "leans anyway" appears as a sentence-ending tic — the same beat of compulsive desire delivered through the same word, sometimes 3–4 times per page.
- **Example locations:**
  - ch20: "the empty in me will not let the beast move" → "it leans anyway" → "I lean anyway" → "the lean in me"
  - ch22: "the deep hunger leans into the open hand" / "it leans at the open hand" / "the want leans"
  - ch23: "I lean at the dying pack anyway" / "the wanting leans" / "the far mouth leans" / "leans at a grave" / "leans anyway"
- **Recommendation:** Cut ~50 of the 168 instances. Replace with varied physical metaphors — the pull, the drag, the draw, the weight toward, the body going. Reserve "lean" for its most potent moments (the narrator leaning toward the human fires, the girl leaning toward the dark). In ch23 specifically, reduce from 16 to ~8.

---

### 2.2 — "behind my teeth" / "behind the teeth" / "in the gap where the teeth"

- **Count:** 91
- **Distribution:** Present from ch01 onward. Peaks: ch27 (9), ch06 (6), ch19 (6), ch26 (6), ch11 (5).
- **Verdict:** REDUCE BY ~25% (~23 instances). This is the narrator's primary spatial metaphor for things he keeps hidden — his name, his rage, the brand. It is powerful and central. But at 91 uses, the spatial metaphor has lost some of its charge.
- **Sub-pattern:** "in the gap where the three teeth are gone" — the full expanded version appears ~20 times. By the fifth occurrence, the reader knows what the gap is. The expansion becomes throat-clearing.
- **Example locations:**
  - ch01: "held behind my teeth where no one can take it" (first major use — strong)
  - ch19: "I keep [the brand] behind my teeth" / "I hold the three things I held before and a fourth one new" / "behind the gone teeth"
  - ch27: 9 instances in one chapter — the narrator cataloguing everything he holds behind his teeth (name, brand, road, her face). The litany is intentional but the phrase repetition crosses into self-parody.
- **Recommendation:** In ch27, reduce from 9 to 5 by using pronoun reference ("where I keep them") and spatial shorthand ("in the dark of my mouth"). Elsewhere, cut the full expansion "in the gap where the three teeth are gone" to "in the gap" after ch12 — the reader has earned the shorthand.

---

### 2.3 — "the warm" (as noun)

- **Count:** ~138
- **Verdict:** REDUCE BY ~15%. The narrator's word for safety/belonging/love, used as a mass noun. Powerful as a concept but overdeployed.
- **Example patterns:** "the kept warm," "the easy warm," "the killing warm," "the tribe's warm," "the warm of the fires"
- **Recommendation:** At 138, this is near the line where a reader starts hearing the word instead of feeling the thing. Cut ~20 instances, especially where "warm" stacks with other coined terms ("the kept warm of the Kala's keeping arm" — both "kept" and "warm" and "keeping" doing the same work).

---

### 2.4 — "the brand" (slavery mark / the word for ownership)

- **Count:** 66 (as standalone word)
- **Verdict:** REDUCE BY ~15%. "The brand" is plot-critical and thematically central. Most uses are earned. But the verbatim brand-phrase — *"mine, be still, come, you are a thing"* — appears ~8 times verbatim and loses shock value after the fourth.
- **The verbatim phrase:** "mine, be still, come, you are a thing"
  - ch19: first full iteration (powerful)
  - ch21: second iteration (still effective)
  - ch22: third iteration (diminishing)
  - ch25: fourth iteration
  - ch26: fifth iteration
  - ch27: sixth iteration
  - ch28: seventh iteration
- **Recommendation:** Cut the verbatim phrase to **4 maximum** (ch19, ch25, ch26, ch28 — the key inflection points). In other instances, reference it obliquely: "the hold-word," "the brand laid on me," "the closing sound." The reader does not need the full phrase every time — they carry it.

---

### 2.5 — "the far mouth" (emotional hunger)

- **Count:** 35
- **Distribution:** First appears ch05. Present in most chapters ch05–ch28. Peaks: ch06 (3), ch28 (3).
- **Verdict:** REDUCE BY ~20% (~7 instances). "The far mouth" is one of the book's best inventions — a visceral, spatial metaphor for the hunger that food cannot fill. But by ch22–23, it begins to appear as shorthand for "emotional longing" without the physical specificity that made it powerful.
- **Example locations:**
  - ch05: "the far mouth opens under my breastbone" (first major use — perfect)
  - ch19: "the far mouth opens for it" (effective)
  - ch23: "the far mouth leans at the dying pack anyway" (stacking two tics — "far mouth" + "leans anyway")
- **Recommendation:** Cut from 35 to ~28. Particularly in the ch18–ch25 sequence where "the far mouth" + "lean" frequently stack. In those instances, vary: "the hollow," "the second hunger," "the thing under my ribs."

### 2.6 — "wrong-" hyphenated compounds

- **Count:** 35 total
  - "wrong-made" — 18
  - "wrong-blood" — 11
  - "wrong-blooded" — 5
  - "wrong-heat" — 1
- **Verdict:** KEEP with minor trim. These are earned — they are the narrator's (and the tribe's) vocabulary for what he is. 35 across 29 chapters is ~1.2 per chapter, which is sustainable. The concentration in ch28 (7 instances) is the only spike.
- **Recommendation:** In ch28, reduce from 7 to 4. Otherwise keep. Consider minting one or two new compounds elsewhere to prevent "wrong-made" from carrying the entire load.

### 2.7 — "the [adjective] one" (character reference pattern)

- **Count:** ~76 for specific named variants
  - "the small one" — 43
  - "the patient one" — 11
  - "the still one" — 8
  - "the far one" — 5
  - "the strong one" — 4
  - "the old one" — 4
  - "the dead one" — 4
  - "the near one" — 3
- **Verdict:** KEEP with light trim. This is the narrator's naming convention — he refers to characters by descriptive epithet rather than name, which is consistent with his limited vocabulary and his cultural context (the Vorth don't use names the way humans do).
- **Problem:** "The small one" at 43 is high. In chapters where "the small one" refers to a specific character (the bold-hearted small companion), consider using "the bold heart" or "Marrow" (once named) to vary.
- **Recommendation:** Reduce "the small one" from 43 to ~30 by substituting with other established epithets.

### 2.8 — "file/filed" (mental storage)

- **Count:** 22
- **Distribution:** Concentrated ch06–ch27. Peaks: ch09 (3), ch23 (3), ch27 (3).
- **Verdict:** REDUCE BY ~25% (~5–6 instances). "I file it" is the narrator's metaphor for processing and storing experience — originally derived from reading tracks and sign. It's a strong, concrete choice. But by the later chapters it has become his default verb for "I am thinking about this," and the filing metaphor starts to feel like a cognitive machine rather than a wild mind.
- **Example locations:**
  - ch06: "I file it" (first major use — fresh)
  - ch23: "I file it true" / "I file it low" / "I file the knowing"
  - ch27: "I file it where the still place is"
- **Recommendation:** Cut to ~16. Replace some instances with more visceral processing: "I set it behind the teeth," "I weigh it," "I hold it in the gut." Vary the mental-storage metaphor.

---

### 2.9 — "pours away" / "poured away" (loss/impermanence)

- **Count:** 13
- **Distribution:** ch09–ch28. Concentrated in later chapters.
- **Verdict:** REDUCE BY ~30% (~4 instances). Beautiful image — the narrator's metaphor for things that cannot be held (ground, belonging, safety). But it clusters in the climax chapters where the narrator is losing everything, and 13 instances of the same loss-metaphor in the final act dilutes the image.
- **Example locations:**
  - ch16: "the ground pouring away" / "pours away the moment the hand opens"
  - ch25: "not one grain staying" / "every grain running"
  - ch26: "not one stone staying in my hand" / "it pours away" (twice in same chapter)
  - ch27: "pours away with the scree"
- **Recommendation:** Cut 4 instances. In ch26 and ch27 where the image appears in close succession, vary the metaphor: "runs through the fingers," "goes to nothing," "will not hold."

---

### 2.10 — "the hole under my breastbone" / "under the breastbone"

- **Count:** 29 (breastbone as word)
- **Distribution:** First appears ch06. Present through ch28. Peaks: ch24 (4), ch25 (3), ch28 (4), ch11 (3).
- **Verdict:** REDUCE BY ~20%. The breastbone-hollow is the narrator's core metaphor for his existential hunger — the thing no food or belonging fills. It is deeply effective. But at 29 instances, the anatomy becomes familiar rather than visceral.
- **Recommendation:** Cut to ~23. In later chapters, use shorthand: "the hollow," "the second hunger," "the empty." Reserve the full anatomical phrase "under my breastbone" for moments of acute emotional crisis.

---

### 2.11 — "the body" as agent (body-vs-head dichotomy)

- **Count:** 179 ("the body" as noun phrase)
- **Verdict:** KEEP with light monitoring. This is the narrator's fundamental division of self — the body knows before the head, the body does things the self did not agree to. It is the philosophical engine of the book.
- **Sub-pattern:** "the body [verbs] before the head can [verb]" — appears ~12 times. This exact construction becomes a tic.
  - ch18: "the body knows the word before the head can turn it"
  - ch19: "the body wears it again now in one breath"
  - ch22: "the body reads it long before the head can shape why"
  - ch23: "the body first, the head long after"
  - ch24: "the body has me out of the mud before the head finds the shape"
- **Recommendation:** Keep the body/head dichotomy as a concept. Vary the exact "body before head" construction — at 12 instances it has become a sentence template. Cut 4–5 by fusing the body-knowing into the action itself without spelling out the dichotomy.

---

### 2.12 — "keeper" (the slaver captain)

- **Count:** 79
- **Verdict:** KEEP. Central antagonist reference. Frequency is appropriate for a character who dominates the back third.

## Tier 3 Tics (Worth Trimming — 5 to 14 occurrences)

These are lower-frequency repetitions that are mostly fine but have specific clustering issues or would benefit from selective thinning.

---

### 3.1 — "the still place" (internal safe space)

- **Count:** 12
- **Distribution:** Exclusively in ch26–ch28 (11 of 12 instances).
- **Verdict:** ACCEPTABLE but concentrated. The "still place" is coined late in the book as the narrator's term for the deep internal refuge he retreats to under extreme duress. Because all 12 instances cluster in the final 3 chapters, it reads less like an established motif and more like a new word the author found and couldn't stop using.
- **Recommendation:** Reduce to 8. In ch27 (5 instances) and ch28 (5 instances), cut 2 from each. The concept is strong; the naming does not need to appear every time the narrator goes there.

---

### 3.2 — "the bold heart" (small companion)

- **Count:** 7 (exact phrase); 12 including "bold small one"
- **Distribution:** ch12–ch21. Absent from the final 7 chapters.
- **Verdict:** UNDERUSED, not overused. The "bold heart" is a strong epithet that disappears from the text after ch21 — likely because the plot separates the narrator from this companion. If the character returns in Book Two, the phrase should be re-established.
- **Recommendation:** No cut. Note for continuity.

---

### 3.3 — "home with no between in it"

- **Count:** 12
- **Distribution:** ch12–ch25
- **Verdict:** REDUCE SLIGHTLY. This is the narrator's phrase for unconditional belonging — "no between" meaning no distance, no qualification. It's beautiful. But 12 instances is the line where a reader begins to hear the phrase as a slogan.
- **Recommendation:** Cut to 8. Reserve for the most emotionally critical moments: ch12 (first coinage), ch18, ch23, ch25. Cut from transitional or summary passages.

### 3.4 — "man-shaped hole"

- **Count:** 11
- **Distribution:** Concentrated entirely in ch18–ch23. Peaks: ch19 (5).
- **Verdict:** REDUCE IN ch19. Five instances of "man-shaped hole" in a single chapter is too many. The phrase is the narrator's metaphor for the specifically human hunger the tribe cannot fill — introduced effectively in ch18. By ch19 it has become shorthand.
- **Recommendation:** In ch19, reduce from 5 to 2. Cut 3–4 total elsewhere. Target: 6–7 across the book.

### 3.5 — "the down road" / "the up road"

- **Count:** 15 ("down road"); 1 ("up road")
- **Distribution:** Concentrated in ch22–ch25.
- **Verdict:** REDUCE. 14 instances of "the down road" across 4 chapters is ~3.5 per chapter. The up/down road dichotomy is structurally important (the narrator literally climbs up to the tribe and down to the slavers) but the phrase becomes a crutch.
- **Recommendation:** Cut to 10. In ch23 (where the dichotomy is most heavily used), vary: "the way down," "the descent," "the climbing-down." The spatial metaphor does not always need the coined compound.

### 3.6 — "the Eye-and-Thorn" (sigil)

## Sentence Structure Tics

### 4.1 — Definitional statements ("[Noun] is a [noun]")

- **Pattern:** The narrator frequently delivers wisdom through aphoristic definitional statements.
- **Count:** ~22 clear instances of the exact "X is a Y" wisdom pattern.
- **Examples:**
  - "Bait is a thing of yours set out as a thing of theirs"
  - "A king of the strength-law is only king so long as the tribe reads the strength as the thing that keeps them"
  - "The highest thing you stand on is the thing the next taking comes for"
  - "A standing no arm carries is a standing you must stand to the end alone"
- **Verdict:** KEEP. These are among the strongest moments in the book — the narrator's hard-won laws rendered in his concrete, non-abstract syntax. 22 across 29 chapters is ~0.75 per chapter, which is sustainable.

### 4.2 — "That is the [noun]" / "This is the [noun]" (deictic summary)

- **Count:** ~75 combined
- **Verdict:** REDUCE BY ~15%. The narrator constantly summarizes with deictic statements — "That is the law," "This is the cost," "That is the new wrongness," "This is the war." At 75 instances (~2.5/chapter), this becomes a recognizable paragraph-capping tic.
- **Recommendation:** Cut ~12 instances. Particularly in chapters 19–23 where the narrator is summarizing every beat. Trust the reader to get the point without the deictic cap.

### 4.3 — Sentence fragments with subject-dropping

- **Pattern:** "Not the hunting-want. The other thing." / "Not meat. The easy road of it."
- **Count:** Pervasive. 200+ instances.
- **Verdict:** KEEP. This is the voice. The fragment structure mirrors the narrator's concrete, non-syntactic thinking. Cutting these would fundamentally alter the prose.

### 4.4 — Paragraph openers: "I" (436), "The" (258), "And" (95), "But" (34)

- **Verdict:** The "I" dominance (436 paragraph openers) is inherent to first-person narration and not a problem. "The" at 258 is also standard. The concern is "And" at 95 — that's ~3.3 per chapter, which is a noticeable rhythmic tic.
- **Recommendation:** Cut "And" openers by ~20% (remove ~19). In revision, when a paragraph starts with "And," ask whether the conjunction is doing real work or just providing momentum.

## Cross-Cutting Recommendations

### 1. Mint new vocabulary for the back third (ch18–ch28)
The later chapters suffer from vocabulary exhaustion — the narrator faces fundamentally new situations (human contact, captivity, loss, the pit) but describes them with the same lexicon developed for the island survival chapters. Consider developing 3–5 new coined compounds for the captivity sequence:
- A word for the specific quality of human voice/speech
- A word for the ship/hold that is not "the dark" (overused)
- A word for captivity that is not "the brand" or "the hand"
- A word for the specific rage that is not "the red"

### 2. Vary the emotional-physical stack
The most common sentence in the back half of the book is some version of: **[coined emotion term] + [body location] + "lean/toward"**. Example: "The far mouth opens under my breastbone and leans toward the fires." This construction appears in variant forms 30+ times. It is the book's signature emotional beat — and it has become a formula. Break the formula in 8–10 instances by changing the body location, the verb, or the spatial metaphor.

### 3. Trust the reader with shorthand
The narrator repeatedly expands coined terms to their full form: "the gap where the three teeth are gone," "the home with no between in it," "the hand on the back of the neck." After the third or fourth full expansion, the reader carries the concept. Switching to shorthand ("the gap," "the home," "the hand") will make the prose feel more confident, not less.

### 4. The "anyway" tic
"I lean anyway" / "I go anyway" / "it leans anyway" — the "anyway" construction (meaning: compulsive desire overcoming rational warning) appears ~25 times and peaks in ch18–ch23. It is powerful as an emotional beat ("I know the trap and I go anyway") but by ch23 the reader predicts it. Cut ~40%.
