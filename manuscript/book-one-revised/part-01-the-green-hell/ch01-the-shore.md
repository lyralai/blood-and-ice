# Chapter 1: The Shore

Weeks in the green had burned the boy down to the root.

Not all at once — the green did not work that way. It worked the way the tide worked the mud: patient, repeated, each pass taking a little more of the soft thing and leaving a little more of the hard. The boy who had stumbled off the beach with the Old One's knife and the disc at his chest and the words *the blood remembers* going through him like a swallowed stone — that boy had been soft. He had carried the shape of other people. The sister's hand on the back of his neck. The Big One's shoulder under a gate. The Old One's voice in the dark. The shape of all the hands that had held him, hurt or steady, and the shape of the cage that had held them all.

The green took those shapes one at a time. Not by force. By absence. There was no hand here. No voice. No cage-bar to press against in the dark, no boot on the boards to mark the morning, no bucket-clang, no schedule. Only the green, breathing its wet breath, and the boy in it, getting harder.

---

He woke in the crack before the light.

The split in the ridge rock where he slept was tight — chest to stone, back to stone, the body folded in the way the cage had taught him to fold. The cage had been tight too. The space under the gunwale where they had put him when the sea rose, tight and dark and full of the tar-stink and the other bodies pressed close. He did not like the tight. But the biting things could not reach him here, and the wind moved through the gap above his face, and the wind was the only clean thing the Old One had taught him before the Old One stopped teaching.

*Move the shelter. The still places. The air. Don't sleep where the air doesn't move.*

He had learned. The first night he had slept in a hollow where the air stood still and thick, and he had waited for the fever the way a dog waits for a boot — body stiff, breath held, the knowing running ahead of the hurt. The fever had not come. The rot-breath went into him and did no harm, and the not-harm was worse than the harm would have been. The body knew it for wrong. It had no shelf for a thing that did not make sense, so it filed the not-harm next to the hunger and the heat and the green, under the heading of *things this shore does that I cannot explain*, and it moved on. The body was practical about such things. The cage had trained it, and the cage did not reward asking why.

He crawled out of the crack. The green light was thick and dim. The heat met him at the gap like a hand laid on the face — wet, heavy, patient. It had all the time there was.

---

The seep was ten steps and a crawl.

He went to it the way the cage had taught him to go to everything: low, quiet, the weight on the flat of the foot, the breath through the nose. Not because anything in the green required stealth at the seep. Because the body had been shaped by hands that valued stealth, and the shape had set, and the shape did not un-set just because the hands were gone. He was a tool that had been sharpened for a purpose, and the purpose was not his, and the sharpening had cost him everything, and here he was — sharp, and purposeless, and going for water.

The thin thread wept clear out of the rock. He put his mouth to the stone and drank. The water was warm — everything here was warm — but it was clean. It tasted of rock and of nothing. Nothing had never tasted good to him before the green. Now it tasted like the best thing the shore had to give.

He drank the hollow dry, waited, drank it dry again. Four times. The rasp in his throat eased. The low hunger did not. The water went through him and around the hunger and did not touch it, the way water goes around a stone in a stream and the stone stays dry underneath. The hunger lived in a room the water could not enter. It had always lived there.

---

There were two hungers in him, and he was slow to learn they were not one.

The first was the near mouth — meat hunger, belly hunger, the wringing under the ribs that spoke the old language the body had always spoken. *Eat or end.* He had carried it his whole life. In the cage it was the weather: the slop that came or didn't, the crust hidden in the shirt-lining, the counting of the ribs as they showed through the skin. You did not fight it. You bent under it and waited.

He learned the green's laws for the near mouth the way he had learned the cage's laws: by watching what survived. The birds. Bright, loud, quick things that dropped from the canopy to the water and picked and chose. He watched them the way he had once watched the hands — not for what they were, but for what they reached toward and what they left. The hands had taught him the language of intent. You did not listen to what a man said. You watched what his hands went for. The birds were the same. They tore at the soft fruit that hung low and split. They snapped up the dull brown frogs from the broad leaves. They would not touch the clear fish that drifted in the shallows — not one, not ever, each bird weighing the pale shape with one flat eye and leaving it with a quickness that was not indifference but *decision*. And they gave the black still pools — the ones that had forgotten how to move, the ones that gave off the rot-breath even his dulled nose could find — a wide berth, going around them in the air as though the black water had a shape above it as well as below.

He did what the birds did. The soft fruit stayed down. The brown frogs stayed down. The clear fish he left where they drifted. When the tide drew back and bared the low mud, he was there with his hands in the shallows, pulling up the fat tide-eels that buried themselves in the warm muck. It was not enough. It was the difference between dying slow and dying slower. But it was the first ground he had taken since the green woke him, and he held it close, the way he held the name behind his teeth. Small and hard and his.

---

The other hunger had no belly to sit in.

He went looking for it with the inside of himself, the way a tongue goes after the gap where a tooth was, and could not find where it lived. It did not live anywhere a hand could point. It woke when there was nothing breathing near him. No voice. No body close enough in the dark that he could feel the warmth come off it. In the cage that hunger had always been full, and he had never known it for a hunger because he had never once felt its floor.

There were always bodies in the cage. The breath of the others going up in the dark — the living and the dead all breathing the same air until you could not tell by the sound which was which. There was even the cruelty, and the cruelty was a kind of feeding — the hands that hurt him at least knew the shape of him, at least kept the count of him. A thing that was hurt was a thing that was seen.

He had hated those hands.

He had hated them with a completeness that was the only completeness the cage allowed. And yet — and this was the splinter, the old splinter, the one he could not dig out — the hatred had a floor, and under the floor was a thing he could not name. Not gratitude. Not love. Not the cold attachment of a dog kicked enough times to call the boot familiar. It was the simple fact of being known. The hands that hurt him had learned his body. They knew where the ribs were, how the shoulder sat, the sound he made when the pressure went past bearing. They had studied him the way a man studies a tool — not with love, not with care, but with a knowledge that was its own kind of bond. The bond of the hand that holds.

And there had been one. The one with the cracked nail that never healed. The hand that had fed him when the others died and feeding the property was cheaper than losing it. That hand had put bread through the bars in the dark — a corner of hard bread, dried fish — and pulled back, no words, never a word. The hand that hit and the hand that fed were the same hand. That was the truth the cage had given him, and he carried it the way he carried his mother's name — behind the teeth, in the dark, where it sat and would not be put down. He had tried to sort it into the clean piles of good and bad that the body wanted, and he could not, and the not-sorting had settled into him like a bone set crooked, and it had healed there, and it was part of him now.

Here in the green there was no one to fill the far hunger. No hand on the back of the neck. No breath in the dark. It opened wider at night, when the chorus of the green shook the air and none of it — not one sound — was a voice that knew his name. The near mouth he could feed. The far one only listened, and went on listening, for a breathing thing that was not there.

---

His tongue found the gaps in his teeth. Three. Two below, one at the side. The holes were a week old, the gums healed smooth, and when he pressed them the hurt was a memory and not a fact. He held the name there — behind the teeth, in the soft gap where the slavers had torn them out and could not reach what was hidden behind.

*Rook.*

His mother had given it to him. He did not have her face anymore — the sea had taken it, all but a warmth and a closeness and a low sound she used to make in the dark when the world was too big. A sound made for a child, not a word but closer to a word than anything the slavers had put on him. He could not make it himself. He had tried, in the cage, and the sound had come out wrong — too high, too thin, the sound of a thing imitating a thing it was not. He had stopped. He had not tried again.

The slavers had their own sound for him. A low thing made behind the teeth, ugly, the felt-shape of a hand closing on the back of his neck. It had meant *property*. It had meant *come here*. It had meant *shut your mouth.* They had put that sound on him a thousand times and he had never once answered it as a name — only as a man answers the lash, with the back and not the name. That sound was not his and it was not him. He had left it in the sea.

But his mother's name — the real one, the one that went under the tongue like a stone — he held. It was the one thing on this whole drowned green shore that was wholly his, that he had carried off the ship in the only place they had never thought to look.

---

The leg turned wrong on the third week.

He did not look at it for two days. There was a kind of not-looking that was its own knowing. The body kept the count even when the eyes refused. But the food had put a little clear back in his head, so he looked. The cut had sealed, but the skin around it was swollen and tight, and at the lips of it the flesh had gone a thick wet yellow that did not belong to a body that meant to live. In this wet, in this heat, a cut like this should go to rot. Fly-blown. The wrongness climbing the leg in red roads while the body went quiet and then gone. He had watched it happen from a cut no longer than this one — Pol, in the hold, whose leg went the color of a plum, whose skin split weeping green, who talked to people who were not there and on the sixth day stopped everything.

He washed it. Clear water from the seep, poured over the hot skin, again and again. He made the crawl a dozen times a day, spending strength he could ill spare.

On the second day the cut was smaller. Not just closed — *smaller.* The lips of it had drawn in toward each other. The yellow was fading back to the plain red of healing flesh. The fire under the skin was burning down.

Too fast.

He went still over the leg the way an animal goes still at a sound it does not yet have a shape for. He had seen wounds. On the ship, in the bodies packed against him in the breathing dark, he had watched cuts like this take a moon and more — watched some never close at all, only widen and weep and stink until the body wrapped around them went still in the night. He knew the slow arithmetic of damage. The count for a cut like this was in moons, and the leg was not keeping it.

The leg was mending in days what should have eaten weeks. And in this wet, in this rot, it should not have been mending at all.

He looked at his hands. The gashes the sharp rock had torn in his palms that first morning were gone — closed to clean lines, the kind a hand carries a week after the hurt, that had stopped being wounds and started being only marks. He turned his palms up in the green light and the small still listening thing inside him went very quiet.

Three wrong things now. The rot-air that did not sicken him. The fever that had come up out of the bad fish and burned through him in a night and left no mark — when the same fever had taken Aldic in two watches and left him shaking and glassy and talking to the ceiling. And now the wounds closing too fast in a wet that should make them fester.

He pushed at it. He turned the three wrong things this way and that, looking for the seam where they fit, the way he would press scattered teeth-marks to read what bit him. They would not come together. There was no head in him yet to hold a question that size — no words for the thing, no frame to hang it on, only the body's stubborn insistence that there was a thing here worth knowing and no way to know it. He reached and reached and his hands came back empty.

There was a feeling under the not-knowing. Not fear — the cage had taught him to spend fear only on things he could fight, and this was not a thing he could fight. The feeling was closer to what he had felt when the cracked-nail hand put bread through the bars — the not-knowing of a thing that was both threat and gift, both harm and help, and no way to tell which, and no one to ask. In the cage those things had come from hands. Here they came from inside him, from a place he could not see and could not reach, and the not-knowing was worse for being his own.

He put it on a shelf at the back. Not because he did not want it. Because he could not carry it and eat at the same time, and eating came first. The shelf was getting crowded. The wrongness went up beside the far hunger and the name and the disc and the Old One's last words — *the blood remembers* — and the weight of all the things he could not explain pressed on the shelf, and the shelf held.

---

He stood on the high stone where the ridge broke above the green and looked out.

Green. Brown water. Green again. Hill on hill of it going up into haze, valleys drowned and steaming, a far line where the green met a white sky. No smoke standing off a far hill. No boat dark on the water. No wall, no roof, no field. No smudge of anything made by hands.

The green watched him back. Not a gaze — a weight, a pressure, the sense of being measured by a thing too large and too old to care about the outcome. The green did not want him dead. The green did not want him alive. The green wanted to go on being green, and he was in the way of that the way a stone was in the way of a river — the river did not hate the stone. The river went around it, and in time the stone was worn to nothing, and the river did not notice.

He looked down at his arms. Through the night the biting swarm should have feasted on him. He should be a map of welts, fevered, his eyes swelling shut. But the bites were few, already fading, and where he should have been eaten alive the skin was near clean.

He did not understand it.

He stood on the high stone with the heat leaning on him and the green going on in every direction, and he held the name behind his teeth, and the disc warm at his chest, and the wrongness filed on the shelf beside the hunger he could not feed, and he was alive. He did not know how. He did not know why. The green did not care.

The far hunger opened, and waited, and went on being hungry in the dark behind the first.

He turned back toward the crack, the seep, the thin thread of clean water. The shore gave once. Then it made you go further.

He went down off the stone and the green closed over him like water. The cage had made him into this shape — low, quiet, the weight on the flat of the foot, the breath through the nose, the hands open and ready. The cage was gone. The shape remained. He walked in it the way a man walks in a boot that was made for a different foot — it fit, and it did not fit, and it was the only boot he had.
