# Chapter 4: The Rot and the Den

The fire had taught him the first hard law of keeping: a thing kept alive must be kept somewhere. The rain taught him the second.

It came on the fourth low after the fire, and it did not come like any rain he had a memory of. It came all at once and from the whole sky, a falling so thick the green drowned to a smear behind it. It did not fall on him so much as stand on him — a warm weight pouring down through the leaves with no edge and no end. His fire, the fire he had bled for, died under it in the time it took him to throw his arms over the coals. A small hiss. Then nothing. Then only the rain hammering the black ash flat.

He crouched over the dead pit in the streaming dark and understood the law late, in the body first, and only after the lack of it had already cost him.

The den-crack did not hold the rain off. Water came up the floor of it warm and brown and full of moving things, and it came down the back of it where the seep was, and there was no part of the crack the green did not pour into. There was a part of the old days in him that knew what it was to be cornered in a small space with the dark coming in — that waited, even now, for the hand to find the back of his neck — and the green here did its own thing instead, which was worse. It climbed in after him wet and warm and alive and began to eat.

He needed a place. Not the crack the green had given him. A place he made.

---

In the steaming after the rain, when the whole green smoked white off the leaves and the standing water breathed its rot back up into the air, he went looking the way he had learned to look — slow, reading what was already here before he asked it for anything.

The crack was no good. He had known it in his spine before he knew it in his head. It sat low, where the land folded down toward the standing water, and the low places here were where the green poured its rot. By the second low after a rain the floor went soft and sour, a film came up on the standing water in it, and the swarms found it — the crack was a thing with one mouth and no draft, and the biting clouds poured into it after his heat with nowhere to go. He lay in the living dark and gave his blood up a bite at a time until the light came back.

So he climbed. The rain that drowned his fire had shown him one other thing, which was where the land shed water and where it held it. He read the green now the way he had once read a tide he could not rightly remember — with the soles and the fingers and the back of the tongue, and the soles told him up. Up out of the rot-bottom. Up where the old stone broke pale and sun-bleached through the green and the water ran off it and did not stand.

Somewhere in the climbing the far hunger did a thing it had not done since the fire. It eased. Only a little. Only for a breath. The body that had spent its whole life being kept low knew the worth of high without the head telling it, and the deep want answered, faintly, to the climb.

He found the place where the ridge had broken in some old breaking of the world. A great slab of pale stone leaned out from the broken face and rested its top against the rock above, and under the lean of it the ground was dry — dry, on this weeping green, where nothing was dry — because the slab had been shedding rain off this hollow longer than he had been alive. He crawled in under it. The roof was a hand and a half above his head, the back solid stone, the floor bare grit and old leaf-fall gone to powder. When he pressed his palm to it, it was dry to the wrist.

He lay in the dry dark of it and felt the shape of the place close around him. The far hunger eased again, wider this time, the way it had in the first light of the fire, and he lay still and let it. A held place. The body had wanted a held place since the sea spat it up. This was the place. The green had made it long before him. He only had to finish it.

And under the easing of the far hunger, the wrong thing stirred.

Not toward the fire — there was no fire here yet. Toward the stone itself. Toward the dry dark of the hollow, the close walls, the low roof. It settled into the shape of the place the way a hand settles into a glove that fits, and he felt it settle, and the settling was not his. The wrong thing liked the den. It liked the held dark the way it had liked the fire — not helping, not hurting, only leaning, only interested, the way a thing leans toward a warmth that is not its own. He felt it there under his ribs, quiet and attentive, and the attention was the kind that had no word yet. Not hunger. Not wanting. Not the rage it had leaned toward in the fireless nights. Something that had to do with keeping. With holding a place.

With *mine*.

He pushed it down. Not hard — the thing was not pressing, not yet — but firmly, the way he had learned to push it down in the nights after the fire. He put it on the shelf with the fever-air that did not sicken him and the bad water that did not knot him and the cuts that closed too fast, and the shelf was heavy now, and getting heavier, and he could not look at any one thing on it without the others pressing forward. He let them press. He lay in the dry dark and kept the den, and the wrong thing settled under his ribs and watched him keep it, and neither of them slept.

---

Finishing it was the work, and the work was against the green, and the green did not give. It only took, and waited, and took again.

The slab kept the rain off the high side. The low sides stood open — open to the rain that drove in sideways, open to the things that walked the night, open to the swarms that would find the heat of him wherever he lay down. He had to close them. He had what the broken ridge gave him, which was stone — pale rubble thrown everywhere along the high line, blocks the size of his chest and bigger. And he had what the green gave him, which was everything, all of it wet, all of it rotting, all of it wanting to climb into a wall and pull the wall back down into the ground.

He lifted the first stone and the cost of it went straight into him. Heavy in a way the fish and the root-pools had never taught him — a dead downward weight with no give in it and no kindness, the weight of a thing that did not care whether he lived. And it was hot, sun-hot on its top face, slick with green slime on its under face, so it slid in his grip and fought him two ways at once. He staggered it three steps to the low side of the hollow and set it down and the noise it made was flat and final, stone on stone. His arms shook.

He went back for the next.

This was the cage-patience again — the patience the hands had beaten into him, the one thing he had made out of all that taking. One stone. Then one stone. Then one stone. He learned how they sat: a flat face wanted a flat face under it. A stone laid wrong rocked and toppled and could take a finger when it went. The big ones went low and the small ones chinked the gaps. He learned it by getting it wrong and bleeding for it. A block turned under his grip and came down across his hand, and he felt the bone go bright with pain and snatched the hand back, crouching over it, the breath hissing through the gap in his teeth, dark blood welling up where the skin split.

He watched it well. Warm and red, the one warm bright thing. Every cut on this green went the green's way — swelled and shined and wept and turned the dark rot-color, the color that meant a thing under the skin had begun its own work. The green got into every open hurt here. It did not get into him. He watched the lips of the split draw toward each other while he crouched, the blood already slowing, the edges going pale and quiet the way no hurt on this green had any right to go. It would be shut clean in three lows and gone in five.

He wanted to know why. He held the wrongness up to the light of his own head and asked it — *why you, why not the green's way, why are you not like every other live thing here.* But there was no word in him for the asking, and no word in the green that answered, and his arms were shaking and the wall was half a wall and the dark was coming. He put the wrong thing on the shelf because the stone would not share him. He put his hand back on the stone.

The stone taught him what the cage had taught him: a body could be spent whether it wanted to be spent or not. The slavers had spent him — his back, his arms, his legs, the strength siphoned out of him into the work they gave him. The body gave it because the body did not know how to refuse. It gave until it broke, and then they let it mend, and then they spent it again. The stone was the same. It did not care that he was hungry. It was heavy, and he moved it, and the moving ate the food out of him faster than the tide-pools could put it back.

He counted it now in stone. The building cost him the gathering, and the near mouth opened wider with every low he gave to the wall instead of the food. The same choice the fire had given him: the dark or the hunger. He chose the dark again. He spent the eating against the wall and the wall rose and the belly fell, and the body did the only thing it knew to do, which was to keep going when keeping was asked of it, even past the point where keeping was wise.

---

Where the rubble ran out he wove the green. He cut the long tough vine and the broad stiff leaves and the cane that grew in stands by the high water, and he lashed and chinked until the gaps between the stones went thick with it, butt-ends in the rubble and tops against the slab. He left a low mouth on the downhill side to crawl through, small as a crab's hole, small enough to close behind him with a last stone dragged across.

The cut vine began to rot the hour he cut it. The wall he wove green would be a wall of slime and stink in three lows if he let it — a thing that fed the swarms instead of keeping them off. So he cut more than he needed and laid it to dry on the hot stone through the white middle of the lows, and he tore the rotted out and wove the dried in. The wall became a thing he did not finish but kept, the way the fire was a thing he did not make but kept. Everything here was keeping. The green took back what you did not keep, fast, and left no mark that you were ever there.

That was the law the cage and the green shared, and the only law they did share. The cage had kept him in the dark and the keeping had been the whole of his life until the sea took the cage and gave him the green. The green kept nothing. It took and rotted and took again, and the only answer was more keeping, more stone, more vine. The hands had owned his keeping in the cage. Here the keeping was his own. That was the first thing he had chosen since the name, and he held it close.

---

The night he closed it for the first time was the night he learned what he had made.

He had made the fire again — the slow way, the torn way, with the bow and the spindle and the dry pith. The small flame stood up gold in the held dark and did not gutter, because the rain was on the far side of the slab and the wall broke the night wind to a thread. The smoke crawled up the back stone and out a gap he had left high for it. The light came off the close rock all around — the leaned green, the chinked stone, the low pale roof — and filled the small space with a moving gold, and the heat rose and met the slab and did not fly off into the night. It stayed. It pooled under the roof. It came back down on him, and he kept it.

And then he learned the thing the den was truly for, and it was not the fire and it was not the rain.

It was the dusk-hour, when the white weight lifted off the green and the rot-warm dark filled with the things that owned this place. They came up off the swamp-edge for the live heat of him — the long whining clouds of the biting flies, the crawling cords of the ants that followed blood through the black, the soft wet things that dropped from the leaves. He had braced for this, the giving-up of blood a bite at a time that was the price of every dark here.

They came up the ridge after his heat. He heard them find the den. But the walls were not what stopped them.

What stopped them was the thing on the shelf.

He sat in the firelight and watched the swarm reach the mouth of the den and pour in, and he watched it not land. The cloud came to the edge of where his body was and it stayed there, a hand's width off his skin and no nearer, a living wall of them held back from a clean wall of air that was the shape of him. They settled on the stone. They settled on the dried green of the weave and the wet green that had crept back into it. They crawled on the carrion the den's warmth had begun to draw — a fish-spine he had not cleaned out, a knot of gut — and the carrion seethed with them, black and moving, while a hand's width off it his skin stayed bare.

He held his hand out. The cloud bent away from it. There was no will to it, no fear in it, only the going-around, and where his hand passed the air was clean, and where it passed from the air closed thick with them again.

He sat very still and watched the green seethe and crawl over everything he had made and everything that died in the den, and not him. Never him. A clean hand's-width all around the live heat of him, walled off from the whole crawling dark by nothing he had built.

There was a shape to it. All of it pointed one way — the fever-air that left him clear, the bad water and the tainted fish that slid off him, the hurts that drew shut clean and fast, and now this, the green itself parting off his skin. He leaned toward the thing the way he leaned toward a sound in the dark — straining, reaching — and he could not make it out. There was a wrongness in him the green kept trying to name and could not, and he could not name it either. The head was too young and the green had given him nothing to think it with. So he kept his eyes on the fire and let the reaching go slack, because he was tired, and because it was enough, tonight, that he was — for no reason it would let itself know — not bitten. The low animal glad of a body that had been bitten its whole life and was, tonight, whole.

But under the gladness, under the tired, under the shelf of wrong things he could not name, the wrong thing in his ribs was not glad. It was still. The stillness of a thing that had been seen and did not want to be seen again. It had felt him reach for it — he was sure of that, sure in the way the body is sure of a thing before the head will hold it — and it had gone quiet, and the quiet was not sleep. It was the quiet of a thing listening back. The wrong thing had heard him trying to name it, and it had gone still the way a man goes still when he hears his own name spoken in a room he thought was empty. It knew he was looking for it. And it did not want to be found.

That was new. Before the fire, the wrong thing had leaned toward heat and rage and the hunger, and the leaning had been blind, involuntary, the way a plant leans toward light. This was different. This was the stillness of a thing that knew it was being watched. That had its own interest in not being named. He felt the knowledge settle into the dark under his ribs like a stone dropping into deep water, and the settling was cold, and the cold was not the green's cold.

It was its own.

He put it on the shelf. The shelf took the weight without giving anything back, the way the sea took a stone.

---

He cooked in the den that night, a fish on the green stick held to the steady flame, the fat running and hissing, the brown good smell going up and crawling along the roof and pooling, his, with no rain to drown it and no draft to snatch it away. When he tore into the hot flesh he chewed it on the side where the teeth still were, and kept the last of the heat behind the missing teeth, the way a man cups a coal in a closed fist, before he let it go down.

The gap. He ran his tongue into it the way he did when the body was quiet enough to remember. Three teeth gone, knocked from a child's mouth by a hand that wanted the child still, and the slavers had thought they took something from him when they took them. They did not know what they left. They left a hiding place. Behind the gap, under the tongue, in the one dark room of him the hands never reached, he kept the only thing that was always his.

*Rook.*

His mother's word. Her gift, from before — before the ship, before the hold, before this green that was the whole shape of the world now. Her voice was in it somewhere far down, and her hands, the warmth of her hands that he could not see anymore but could still, just barely, feel the shape of when he held the word against the warm.

It was different now, saying it in the den. On the shore, in the crack, the name had been a keeping — a thing held against loss, the last warm thing, carried. It was still that. But it was also beginning to be something else, and the something-else had started the night he made the fire and grew now in the walls he had built. The name had been a thing he kept because it might be all he had. In the den, in the held warmth, with the walls his and the fire his and the swarm held off by the wrongness that was not his and not the green's — in this place the name began to feel less like a keeping and more like a calling. As though the word were not only a memory of what he had lost but a name for what he was becoming, and the two things were not the same, and the gap between them was the gap where the three teeth were gone, and the gap was his.

He said it now, behind his teeth, in the firelight — never out loud, the green was always listening — *Rook, Rook,* and it was warm, the one warm thing that was never taken because it was never found.

The slavers had had their own sound for him. A sound shaped behind the teeth, low and possessive, that named a thing to be called and made quiet — the felt-shape of a hand closing on the back of his neck. They had put it on him the way you put a mark on a beast. It was not his name. That sound he had left in the sea with the ship and the chains. This one he kept.

But the word was changing, the way the fire changed the meat — same fish, same flesh, but the heat did something to it, made it stay when before it had sickened, made it warm when before it had been cold raw nothing. The name was the same word. It sat different in him now. It had walls around it and a roof above it and a fire below it, and the warmth was not the memory of his mother's hands alone but also the warmth of his own hands, his own fire, his own den. The name was becoming a thing that lived here, in the place he had made, and the living-here changed it. The cage had taken the name and made it small, a thing to hide. The den was letting it sit up. He could feel it, the way you feel a muscle unclench when the weight comes off, slow and reluctant and not sure it is allowed.

He did not know what the name would be when it finished sitting up. He was not sure he wanted to know. A thing that sits up can be seen, and a thing that can be seen can be taken, and the taking was the one lesson the cage had taught him that he did not need to learn twice.

He held the word behind his teeth and did not let it out and did not put it down.

---

He lay back in the held warmth, and the body thought the way a body thinks when it has made the third impossible thing and already knows the fourth is waiting.

He had pulled fire out of dead wood. He had pulled a place out of dead stone and rotting green. One thing taught the next, and the pulling did not end. This was the law under the green: a thing was never made here, only kept, borrowed back from the rot a low at a time. The day the hands stopped, the green would pour back in and pour it away, and it would be as if he was never here. He knew this. He lay in the dry dark anyway, and he kept it.

The keeping had cost him. The wall-building had taken five lows. In those five lows the tide-mud at the low water had gone ungathered while he carried stone, and the birds had cleaned what the tide left. He was thinner now than he had been when the fire first stood up. He could feel it in the way his ribs sat when he lay back — the bones closer to the skin, the skin looser on the bones, the body eating itself to pay for the walls. Each thing he made was paid for in the body, and the body was running low.

Nothing was free. The slavers had fed him because a dead slave was a loss — grain against flesh, the arithmetic of ownership. The green did not feed him at all. It offered and took back, and what he took from it he paid for with his body. He was spending it on walls and fire and a dry place to hold the dark off, and the spending was right because the dark would kill him, but the spending was also making him thinner, and thinner was closer to the going-down, and the going-down did not have a stair that came back.

He needed food. Tomorrow the low water, and the mud, and the slow patient hand in the dark water waiting for a thing to drift close. The walls could keep one more day. The walls could keep every day after this, because the keeping was not a thing that ended — it cost work every day, and the work cost the body, until he died or stopped.

He did not intend to stop.

And under the thought, low down, the far hunger stirred. Not toward the fire. Not toward the fish. The den had eased it — the climb, the wall, the being-held and not-flayed — but eased was not fed. The far hunger took the warmth the way the dark takes the last red of a coal — without hurry, without mercy — and waited under the walls and past the slab, wider than the fire and the den could reach.

It had changed. In the crack, the far hunger had been an absence — a hollow, the not-there-ness of a breathing body in the dark. In the firelight it had leaned toward the flame. Here, in the den, it did a new thing. It reached. Not in. Out. Past the walls, past the slab, out into the green dark where the rain still fell and the swamp breathed its rot and the things that owned this place hunted and coupled and died. It reached for that. Not to hide from it. Not to wall it out. To be in it, the way the green was in it — not alone, not held off by stone and fire, but part of the wet, breathing, biting, living dark that he had spent every low since the shore pushing away.

The contradiction sat in him like a bone splinter he could not dig out. He built walls because the body needed walls. The body needed walls because the green would eat him without them. And the far hunger — the deepest thing, the thing under all the other hungers — wanted the green. Not the death of it. The life of it. The warmth of a body that was not his pressed against his in the dark.

The cage had taught him that the hands that hurt him were better than no hands at all. He had carried that knowledge like a wound he was ashamed of. And now the den was teaching him the same lesson turned around: the walls that kept the dark out kept him in. The far hunger did not care how good the walls were. It wanted out.

He did not understand it. He was too tired tonight to go down after it again. He fed the fire and laid the last stone across the low mouth to close the night out, and the swarm seethed on the far side of the wall and rang the clean shape of him on this side, and he put his back to the dry stone — fed, warm, walled, lit, the first place that was ever his.

And out past the leaned green, out past the chinked stone, far off down the night-ridge below the held stillness, the low sound came up out of the dark that was not the rain and not the swarm.

He went still over the fire. He listened.

It came again. Closer this time.
