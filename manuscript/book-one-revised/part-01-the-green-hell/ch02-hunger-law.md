# Chapter 2: Hunger Law

The leg that had been mending stopped.

Not by degrees. It stopped the way a schedule stops — one day whole and the next broken, the count thrown out. The lips of the cut that had drawn shut in the turning before parted again overnight, and the yellow came back into the flesh, deeper this time, and the heat with it. The skin around the wound went drum-tight and shining, a deep, live color that throbbed with a pulse of its own, as if the cut had decided to become a thing separate from the leg that carried it. He pressed it once, to know it. The heat pushed back against his hand like the flank of something living. He did not press it again.

So. The wrongness had a ceiling. The body that mended too fast in the rot-wet had mended — and then un-mended, as if the mending had been a thing borrowed and the lender had called it back. That was new. The shelf where he kept the things this body did without asking held three wrongs already: the rot-air that did not sicken him, the fever that had come and gone in a night, the cuts that closed too fast. Now a fourth: the mending that stopped. The wrongness was not a line going one direction. It was a tide. It went out and it came back, and he could not predict the turn.

He could not walk on it. The body told him in the only language it had — the weight went off the leg and stayed off, the way a dog lifts a paw and holds it up and will not set it down. The crawl to the seep cost him. The crawl back cost more. Between the two he lay in the crack with his face near the wall and breathed through the rot-warm dark and counted what he had against what he spent, and the count did not balance.

There had been a man in the cage beside him. Not Aldic, whose name he knew the way you know a thing that is given freely, against all the customs of the place. Another. One who arrived with no name and left with no name and spent the hours between making a sound. Not words. A low thing, constant, the sound a body makes when it has decided to stop. The sound went on for two nights. On the third the man was quiet, and on the fourth he was cold, and the slavers had dragged him out by the ankle with no more care than a man gives to hauling a wet sack from a flooded hold. Rook had watched through the bars. He had felt nothing. That was the cage's other lesson, the one after the pain: the body learned to stop spending on the dead. Not cruelty. Arithmetic. You could not afford the cost of feeling for a thing that was gone, because the cost would eat the strength you needed to stay alive, and then you were the cold thing being dragged out, and there was no one left in the cage to stop spending on you.

He lay in the crack and did the arithmetic. The leg was bad. The food was gone — the close pools picked clean, the low-water mud too far for the leg to carry him. The water was clean but water was not enough. These were the facts. Facts were all the cage had taught him to trust.

---

The hunger started in the head.

Not the belly — the belly had been wringing for days, low and steady, the old weather he had carried his whole life. The near mouth. He knew that one. It sat where hunger was supposed to sit and spoke the body's oldest language: *eat or end.* This was different. A fog came up soft, like vapor off the swamp at the low of the light, a thing with no edge to it that was suddenly everywhere. Each crawl to the seep was longer than the last. The rock blurred at its edges. He stopped halfway and put his cheek to the stone and waited for the world to firm up before he trusted his hands to find the water.

The fog ate what it touched. It ate the edges of the rock and the green of the light and the reasons for holding the name behind his teeth — not the name itself, which sat hard and whole in the gap where the teeth were torn out, but the why of it, the thing that made the holding matter. The fog did not care why. The fog only took. Some old true part of him, the part that remembered the cage and the way a man's eyes went glassy when the hunger had eaten enough of the thinking, watched the fog come and knew it for what it was: the first stair on the going-down. And the going-down did not have a stair that came back.

The fog ate the fear along with the rest.

---

He needed to eat. The body told him and told him, and the telling was all that was left when the thinking thinned. He could not make the low-water mud — too far, the leg would not bear him. He could not climb for the fruit — the arms that could pull him up the ridge were spending their last on the crawl to the seep. He had the close pools, the ones the tide bared only on the lowest of the lows, and the things in them were picked clean. Except the clear fish.

The birds would not touch the clear fish. He had seen them leave it a hundred times — that flat-eyed turning-away, the decision that was not indifference. Every bird. Every fish. Every time.

But the birds were not him. The rot-air that dropped the men in the hold did not touch him. The fever that took Aldic in two watches had burned through him and left no mark. The wrongness on the shelf — the things this body did that the body never agreed to — the wrongness might extend to the clear fish. It might not. He had no way to know. And the fog was eating the part of him that cared about the difference.

He caught one with his hands. It was easy. They were slow, drifting in the warm shallows with a motion that was barely a motion at all, as if the fish had already made its peace with being eaten by something and was only waiting to find out what. He bit into it. The flesh was pale and soft and faintly green. It stayed down.

He caught another. And another. The near hunger opened wild now that it had been fed, and the body did what the body does when it has been starved and the food is easy — it took, and took, and did not count the cost.

---

The cost came before the last fish was off his fingers.

His stomach knotted like a fist. He brought the pale meat up onto the mud — bile and half-chewed bone — and his guts wrung out on empty and then wrung again, and the wringing did not stop. He lay with his cheek in the warm mud and shook, and the shaking came from somewhere deep and wet and low, worse than any hurt that came from outside. The body knew how to set itself against an outside thing. There was no setting yourself against the body turned against the body. He had listened to men in the hold die of this — the gut-rot, the sickness that climbed out of bad meat and turned a man into a heap that could not keep down even the rain. The sound they made was not screaming. It was smaller. A whimper, steady, the sound of a body that had lost the argument with itself and was reporting its loss to no one.

Then the heat came up in him. Not the air's heat. His own. It climbed out of the belly into the head, and the fog went thick and red at its edges, and he knew the name of it: the fever. It took the small and the weak first. He was small now and weak now, scraped to nothing by the fog and the leg and the turning after turning of not enough. He had watched it take men down a stair at a time. Aldic, who ate the fruit and died with a smile that was not his own. Pol, whose leg went the color of a plum and whose skin split weeping green. The man with no name who had whispered against the bars and gone cold on the fourth turning. The fever did not come up in a night and burn out in a night and leave the body whole. It took you down. Most who started down did not climb back.

He felt his foot on the first stair.

The body settled into the going-down with the patience of a thing that had been here before. The cage had taught him this: when the thing that will kill you is inside you, there is nothing to fight. You ride it. You hold what you can hold and you let the rest go, and if the going takes you, it takes you, and the count is done.

He held the name behind his teeth. Not because it would save him. Because it was his, and a thing that is dying has a right to hold what is its own. The heat shook him and the red thickened, and he thought once of the hand — the cracked-nail hand that had put bread through the bars and pulled back, no word, never a word. The hand that hit and the hand that fed. He had eaten what it gave him. He had hated it for giving. The fever did not give him time to sort which was the truer thing.

The red went dark. The dark took him down.

And under the dark — under the fog and the shaking and the red — something else. Not the small listening thing. Something older, or deeper, or both. A cold place. A place that did not burn and did not shake and did not care about the fever, because the fever was a fire in the body and this place was not the body. It held the name the way a stone holds a fossil — pressed into it, part of it, older than the flesh that carried it. The cold did not fight the fever. It simply was, the way the stone under the river is, while the river breaks against it and goes on. It held the name, and the name held, and the dark came down all the way.

---

He woke.

That was the wrong thing. He knew it for wrong before he had head enough to know the green from the stone under his cheek. He had gone down the stair. He had felt the first step under his foot. And here he was — not at the bottom, not cold, not the thing dragged out by the ankle. Still breathing. The dark had come down all the way and it had let him back up, and the letting-back-up was not in the count he knew.

He took the measure the way the body took it: gut first, then head. The belly was wrung clean but quiet. The sickness was burned out. Whatever had climbed out of the clear fish and lit the fever was gone — not suppressed, not hiding, gone. As if the fever had been a cleansing fire and not a killing one, and the body it had burned through was cleaner for the passage.

He looked at the leg.

The yellow was gone. The swelling was gone. The skin that had been drum-tight and shining was loose and quiet and the color of new healing — not the angry red of a wound fighting, but the dull, settled pink of flesh that has turned the corner and is going home. The cut that should have taken moons was gone. Not a scar. Not a mark. A slight depression in the skin, the way a healed wound looks a year after the hurt, and this was a week old. Less.

He pressed the skin where the cut had been. It did not push back. It did not pulse. It was just skin, whole and calm and finished, as if the leg had never been opened at all.

The fever had burned through him in a night and left him whole. The infection that should have taken the leg was gone. The wound that had stopped healing had not just resumed — it had finished, as if the fever had been the body's tool, a fire lit from inside to clean the wound the slow mending could not reach. As if the body had decided to stop mending the small way and mend the big way instead, and the fever was the cost of the big way, and the cost was paid, and the body was done.

Four wrongs on the shelf. Now five. And the fifth was the worst, because the fifth was not a thing the body had failed to do. It was a thing the body had done *well.* The body had healed itself with a fever. The body had used the sickness as a tool. And a body that used sickness as a tool was a body with a plan, and a body with a plan was not a body he knew the rules of, and a thing you did not know the rules of was a thing that could turn on you.

The small still listening thing inside him pulled hard at the back of everything. *What are you. What is this body. What is the thing that lives where the cold place was.* He reached for the why and his hands came back empty. There was no head in him yet for a question this size. No frame. No words. The cage had given him the arithmetic of damage and the counting of costs, and neither one balanced here. He pushed at it the way a tongue goes after the gap where a tooth was, and the gap was deeper than he had thought, and there was something at the bottom of it, and the something moved, and he pulled back.

He put it on the shelf. The shelf was heavier now — heavier than the name and the disc and the Old One's last words combined. He put it there because you could not eat and wonder at the same time, and eating came first. The question would wait. Not yet. Someday.

He stood on the leg. The weight went forward slow — braced, waiting for the break. The break did not come. The new skin pulled tight at the full stretch but held, and the holding was more than the leg had any right to do, and the body did not trust it and walked on it anyway.

---

The green had laws, and the laws had teeth.

He learned this the way he learned everything: by watching what survived. Not the birds alone now, though the birds were still his teachers. The whole green. He read it the way he had once read the hands — not for what they were, but for what they reached toward and what they left. The hands had taught him the language of intent. The green spoke the same language in a different mouth.

The clear fish he left alone. The sickness had taught him the price, and the price was the fever, and the fever had taught him something else: the green's easy gifts were traps. The slow fat fish drifting in the warm shallows, the ones that did not run from a hand — they were marked. The birds knew it. Now he knew it. *What the green gives you for nothing, it takes back in blood.* That was the first law.

The second law was in the water itself. There was a rise and a fall in this drowned green, slow, under the day, and the far edge of it ran down to where the swamp met the salt. There the tide breathed in and out — the great rhythm, the only clean schedule in all this world. In the cage there had been a rhythm: the clang of the bucket, the boot on the boards that meant *morning* and the boot that meant *night*. The body had settled into that rhythm the way a body settles into breathing. When the ship broke and the schedule stopped, the body had lost its clock and had not found a new one.

Now it had one. The tide. Not the schedule of hands — the schedule of the world itself, the breathing of a thing too large to have an opinion about him. The salt-water drew back, and the low places were bared, and the things that lived there were caught in the mud until the water came again. And the water came again, and covered them, and the things that needed the baring were fed, and the things that needed the covering were fed, and neither had a say in the matter. The tide did not negotiate. The tide did not take back what it gave. It simply breathed, and the breathing was law, and the law was clean.

He learned the breath of it by the body — the only book he could read here. The look of the light laid on the water. The turn of the smell when the salt was about to change its mind. A falling-off in the steady drip of the green that meant the going-out had begun. Low water was when he ate. The rest of the time he banked what strength he had in the crack, spending nothing he did not have to spend, because the cage had taught him the cost of being caught with nothing and the green charged the same price.

He widened. The close pools were picked clean, so he followed the tide out further, to the mud that showed only on the lowest of the lows. Those places held bigger things. Slow crabs, heavy and dark, that he took two and three of before they woke to him. And in a back channel where the root-walls made a cage of their own — he saw the shape of it and his body went tight before his head could name why, because a cage was a cage regardless of what made it — in that channel he found more of the fat tide-eels. Bigger than the ones in the close pools. With a smell of *something* to their flesh instead of nothing. They stayed down. The meat sat warm and real in the belly, and the near hunger gave one step backward, and then another, and then a third.

For the first time since the green had closed over him, he was not dying.

He was not winning. The near hunger did not leave — it pulled back to a low, steady thing, the body's rent paid out daily for the keeping of a life. He could stand under it now. He could think over the top of it. The far hunger — the one that lived where the hands had been, in the space they had left behind — did not pull back. Not by a step. Not by a breath. He fed himself. He drank himself full. He washed in the seep and learned the breath of the tide and built the law of this green one stay-down and one sickness at a time. None of it reached the far one. It sat open in the dark under everything, waiting on a breathing thing that was not there.

---

The nights were the price.

The dark came down and the green changed. The same trees, the same water, the same rot-heat — but owned by something else. The day-green was indifferent. The night-green had opinions. He heard them. A heavy something far off in the high canopy, moving with a weight that did not care what it pushed through. A wet sliding at the channel-edge where the mud met the standing water. A cough that was not a bird and not a frog and not anything he had a shape for. The biting things came in clouds at the falling of the light. The fever-air rose thick off the black pools and hung low over everything, warm and patient and rotten.

None of it touched him. The bites were few, already fading. The fever-air went into him and did no harm. He was the clean space in the green, the thing the laws did not touch.

But the not-being-touched was not the same as not being afraid.

The dark held sounds he could not read, and the not-reading was the oldest fear the cage had left him — the fear of the thing behind the door of the hold, the thing that breathed and moved and had opinions about the warm shapes locked below. He lay in the crack with his back to the stone and his eyes open in the black and nothing in his hands. No tool. No weapon. The Old One's knife had gone with the Old One into the sea. He had the rock around him and the name behind his teeth and the disc warm at his chest, and none of it pushed the dark back. None of it made the sounds smaller or the breathing in the canopy go away.

The far hunger was worst at night. The near mouth he could feed — eels, crabs, fruit, the body's rent paid out in the currency of meat. The far mouth sat open under the dark, and the dark fed into it, and the not-filling was a sound he could hear when the green went quiet enough: the absence of a voice that knew his name, the absence of a body warm against his in the close dark, the absence of the thing the cage had given him that he had not known was a gift until the cage was gone and the gift was taken with it.

He needed a thing that pushed the dark back. A thing the green was afraid of. He did not know yet what that thing was. He knew only that the dark was coming every night, and surviving the day was not the same as surviving the night, and the body — the wrong, mending, fever-cleansed body that would not die when it should — the body was afraid of the dark in a way it was afraid of nothing the day could put against it. Not the cage-fear, the careful spending of fear on things he could fight. This was the animal fear. The fear of the thing that is small and warm and knows, in the deep place where the knowing lives, that the dark has teeth, and the teeth are patient, and the patient thing waits for the one night the body is not wrong enough to save him.

The green breathed its wet breath over the crack, and he lay in it and held the name behind his teeth, and the name was not enough, and the dark did not care.
