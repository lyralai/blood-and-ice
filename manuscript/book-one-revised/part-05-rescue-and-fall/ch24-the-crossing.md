# Chapter 24: The Crossing

The pack died by ones.

He counted them through the deck. Not with ears — with the thing the body learned when it had lain long enough in a hold to read the whole boat through the planks. Every step was a different foot. Every voice a different shape. When a foot stopped stepping and a voice stopped throwing, the body noted it the way it noted a print going into the mud and not coming back.

The fevers took three more before the wind changed.

Three. The coughing one first — a young one, from the quickness of the step that stopped. Then the hard-faced one from the raid, the one whose blade had opened his arm. He died of the fever, not the throat. The fever did not care what the boy had done to him. It took the body it found. Then a third he had not read close — a body that stepped heavy and spoke low and was now silent.

The steady-handed woman worked. He heard her boots. She was everywhere above — moving between the sick, the voice laying orders, the hands on the wounds that were not wounds but the body rotting from the inside. She could not stop it. The body knew this from the sounds — the set in her voice that the big one's face had carried on the spit when the dying went bad. The holding-on-to-a-thing-you-cannot-hold set. The big one was losing his pack to the green the boy carried in his blood without a mark, and every foot that stopped stepping was a line the holding-center had drawn that the green was erasing.

The cook worked too. He boiled the water. He made the stew. He carried it to the sick, and the sounds he made at them were the joke-sounds, the low bad sounds no one laughed at, and the not-laughing had affection in it, and the affection was the thing the dying heard last.

Until the low the heavy step stopped.

Not the cook — one of the others. A body he had not read close, a crew-thing that stepped heavy and spoke low and hauled the rope without complaint. The fever took him the way it took them — fast, the chest-sound, the drowning-from-the-inside. The cook was the one who carried the water to him. The cook's heavy step went to where the crew-thing lay, and the joke-shape sounds came, low and bad — and this low the crew-thing did not answer them.

The young one went to the crew-thing. The body heard it — the quick feet crossing the deck, the small sound the young one made. A sound that was just the air going out of a body that had lost a thing it stood beside every low. The young one sat with the crew-thing. The cook stood over them both, and the joke-sounds were gone, and the big burned hands hung at his sides, and the cook's face had the thing the big one's face had carried on the spit when the dying went bad.

The crew-thing went in the dark. The steady-handed woman wrapped him in the sail-cloth and they slid him over the side, and the water took him the way the water took everything.

The pack that was thirty was eight.

The sky-reader held the heading. Her voice was up in the rigging with the young one, calling the wind, reading the water, the two of them doing the thing the body recognized from the green — the heading-instinct, the thing that went forward because forward was the only direction that had a shape. She did not sicken either. He read this: she was the kind that did not sicken easy. Some were. The green took the easy ones first.

And the thick-armed one worked with the bad wrist. No lighter. No shorter. The body heard him through the planks — the oar, the rope, the heavy step, the short command. He did not speak to the boy. He did not come below. He worked the boat with the broken wrist and the flat face and the thing under the flat face that the boy had read on the high stone when he stood over the big one calculating.

He was afraid. Not of the green. Not of the dying. Of the thing the big one was becoming — the thing the big one became when he read the caged boy below whose wounds closed in days. The thick-armed one read the calculating and the calculating had a shape and the shape was the debt and the thick one was afraid of where the debt led.

He did not know the debt. He did not have the word for it. But he read the fear off the thick one's step through the planks, and the fear was the shape of a thing that knew the holding-center was about to draw a new line.

---

The Kala dulled.

Low by low the great hide lost the silver. The gray went flat. The skin that had shone in the green half-light — that caught the canopy-dark and held it, alive, the living pelt of the thing that kept him — went dead under the wrong air, the wrong water, the wrong food they pushed through the slat. She did not eat. It sat on the hull floor and rotted. And Marrow — the runt had stopped making the still-sound. He ate what was pushed through the bars of the small cage, the curled hand working the food the way it worked everything, one-handed and sure, but the eating was all there was of him now. The bold heart lay in the crook of the high shoulder and did not chatter. The cage that had been built for a small animal held two things that had made the crossing the way the boy had made it. Taken.

He tried. The body tried. He took the fish-stew and held it to the great mouth and the mouth closed. He took the water and poured it along the lip and the lip did not open. Marrow's curled hand found his knee in the dark and stayed there, not gripping — resting, the weight of a thing that could not fix the dying and would not leave it. The Kala made the gentling-sound less and less. The breathing slowed. The great dark eye — the eye that had read him on the bank, that had read him on the king's seat, that had read him in the dark of the hold every low since they chained them — the eye dimmed.

He pressed his front to the great side, and Marrow pressed against his back — the small wrong warmth, given — and the warmth was there. The tide of the breathing was there — in, out, in, out, slower, the long pauses between that meant a thing was going down into the place where the body did not come back from. He counted the breaths. The way he had counted the lows on the island. The way he had counted the steps between the den and the kill-ground. The body counted because the counting was the thing the body did when it could not do anything else.

And the Red stirred.

Not the killing-Red. Not the thing that had come up on the high stone and gone through the thick-armed one like water through a channel. The other thing. The thing the seer's finger had found on the breastbone. The thing under the blood that was older than the crown. The thing that had pulsed once in the pit of the fever-fog and once on the king's seat and once in the hold of this boat in the dark. The thing that had no word.

It stirred when the Kala's breathing went long. It stirred the way a thing stirs that has been asleep and hears a sound it knows. And the warmth came up — not the body-warmth, the blood-warmth, the deep-down warmth that closed the wounds too fast and rang the swarms off by a hand's width. It came up and it went into the front he pressed against the Kala's side, and the Kala's breathing — for a breath, for two — steadied.

Then the cold came.

The body-cost. The shivering, the numb hands, the cut pulling open. These he knew. These were the small cost. But underneath them was the thing he had learned on the raid stone: the warmth and the Red lived in the same place. The island-place. The root. The survival-thing the island had burned into him — the deep wiring that did not stop. The warmth came from there. The healing came from there. Everything that kept him alive came from the same root that ran the killing, and reaching for the warmth meant reaching for the root, and the root was awake now. It had gone down after the raid but it had liked the running and it was ready to run again.

He pressed harder against the Kala's side. The warmth went into her. The breathing steadied — barely. And the deep place stirred underneath the warmth — not the Red, not the killing-thing, but the place the killing-thing lived in. The place where the warmth and the killing were the same thing. The place the island built. And the still place pressed down on it, and the pressing was harder than it had been before the raid. The still place was tired. The still place was the muscle he had built after the island — the name, the count, the crown, the Kala's teaching — and it was newer and shallower than the root underneath it, and the root did not tire. The root only waited.

Then it went down. The warmth went down. The cold went down. The Kala's breathing went long again. And he lay in the hold knowing that every time he reached for the warmth to keep her alive he went deeper into the place the island built, and the deeper he went the harder the root pushed up, and the still place was the only thing between the boy and the root, and the still place was a muscle, and muscles failed.

---

The steady-handed woman came below on the fifth low.

Not the big one — her. She ducked through the hatch with the steady hands carrying things — a blade, a pot of the paste she made, a strip of clean cloth. Professional. The hands went to the work because the work was there. She did not look at the Kala. She looked at him. At his arm.

The cut. The blade-wound from the night raid — the one the hard-faced one had opened from elbow to wrist. She read it from three body-lengths away, and her face did the thing her face did: the reading-thing, the professional-thing, the hands-going-to-the-wound thing. Except the wound was not a wound anymore. Five lows old, closed, the scar pink and clean, the edges pulled shut in a way no wound she had ever seen pulled shut in five lows.

She crouched. She took his arm. He let her take it — the professional in her hands, the hands going to the wound the way his hands went to a kill, automatic, sure. She turned the arm. Her fingers found the scar. She pressed. The pressure was clinical — not gentle, not rough, the pressure of a thing that was reading the body through the fingertips the way he read the ground through the soles.

Her face changed.

Not the fear-change. The fascination-change. The change of a thing that had spent its life closing wounds and had never seen one close like this. She looked at the scar and she looked at him and her mouth made a sound — a word, low, thrown up at the hatch. The big one's face appeared in the hatch-light. She showed him the arm. The five-low scar that should be a five-week wound.

But she saw the other thing too. The thing the healing left behind. The cold — the low-grade cold that sat in him now after every time the Red had been up. She read it the way she read a fever: professional, the hands going to the forehead, the wrist, the throat. Her eyes narrowed. And her hands stopped.

They stopped because the pulse under her fingers was wrong. Not two beats — one beat, but the beat was too hard, too deep, the heart pushing the blood the way a body pushed a fight. The healing was there — wrong-fast, impossible. But underneath the healing the pulse ran hard, the way a pulse ran in a body fighting for its life even when the body was sitting still. Even when the body was at rest. Even when there was nothing to fight. The survival-thing — the deep wiring — did not know how to turn off. It ran and ran and the body answered, and the healing was not separate from the running. The healing was the running. The same engine. The same fire.

She did not know what she was reading. She was a surgeon and she had read a thousand pulses, but she had never read a pulse that ran this hard in a body at rest, and the reading was the reading of a woman who had found something past the edge of what she knew. Her face did a thing he did not expect. She looked at him — not the professional look, not the pricing look. The look of a surgeon who had put her hands on a body that was running itself to death and the running was the thing keeping it alive and she did not know which one to treat.

The look went. The professional face came back. But the food changed — the next low the pot came down with dried fruit beside it, placed with a care that was not pricing. And the portion was larger. And the stew had something in it that had not been in it before — a root, bitter, the kind that built the blood. She was treating the cold. She did not understand it, but she was treating it, the way her hands went to a wound — the hands doing what the hands knew.

She had not softened toward him. She had done the thing a surgeon does when the body on the table is doing something impossible: she had decided to keep it alive long enough to understand it. The pricing was still there. The boy was cargo that had turned into a question. But underneath the pricing was the thing the pricing was built on — the hands. The hands that closed wounds. The hands that read the pulse that ran too hard and did not pull away.

---

The big one came below every low now.

Not to the Kala. To him. He crouched at the hatch and made sounds at him — words, low, steady, the holding-center voice, the voice the big one used on the pack when the pack was going bad. He did not reach for him. He did not come close. He crouched at the hatch and talked the way the Kala made the gentling-sound — because the making held even when the hearing was gone. Because the holding-center did not stop holding when the thing it held could not hear it.

He did not know the words. He had not had the road from head to mouth since the green took it. But the body read the shape of the sounds the way it read the shape of the Vorth sounds — body to body, the meaning under the word. And the meaning under the big one's words was the same meaning the hand on the young one's neck had carried: you are mine and I am yours and the thing that is coming for us has to come through that first.

Not the brand-meaning. The other one. The one he had seen in the camp on the spit. The one he had leaned at through the green wall.

The crack widened.

---

On the ninth low the wind died and the boat sat on the flat water and did not move. The heat came down. The air in the hold went thick and wet and the fever-seep thickened with it. Another one died above — a quick foot-stop, a silence, the cook's voice, low, the joke-shape that no one laughed at. The cook was still standing. The cook did not sicken. The big burned hands kept the pot on the fire and the stew in the buckets, and the not-sickening of the cook was the thing the pack did not name but read — the cook was the cook, the fire was the fire, and the fire did not go out because the cook did not go out.

The big one came below. This low he came all the way in. He did not crouch at the hatch. He stood in the hold, five body-lengths away, and read the Kala on the floor and the boy against her side. The Kala's breathing was bad. The pauses between breaths were long enough to hold a whole word in. The big one read this. His face did the calculating thing.

Then he crouched beside the Kala. Not him — her. He put his hand on the great hide the way the steady-handed woman put her hands on a wound — careful, reading. The Kala's eye tracked him. The big dark eye in the dulling hide, reading the thing that chained her, reading the thing that took her off the high stones, reading the two-legged shape that meant hand and hold and owned.

He took his hand off. He looked at him. And his face did a thing he had not seen on him — it cracked. Not the holding-center crack, the professional crack, the calculating crack. The other crack. The crack of a man who had done a thing he thought was right and was reading the cost of it on the body of the thing he had taken it from.

He stood. He went to the hatch. He stopped. He turned back.

He undid the chain on his arm.

Not the Kala's chain. His. The iron fell off the wrist and the wrist was free and the blood went back into the hand and the hand was his again. He did not have to do this. The cost of it sat plain on his face — the big one had given the caged thing a weapon, and the caged thing had broken his enforcer's wrist and put two of his crew on the ground, and the giving was a risk the holding-center should never have taken. The boy read the contradiction whole: the same man who had chained the Kala now gave the boy back the use of his hands, because the holding thing in him could not stop holding, even when what he held was becoming the thing that would break the line he had drawn around his pack.

He took it. He looked at him. And the sound he made was low and steady and it had the shape of a word he almost — almost — had the road to catch.

Then he went up the hatch and the light closed and he was free in the hold with the Kala chained beside him. Marrow had watched the iron come off. The runt's sharp eye went from the bare wrist to the hatch and back, and the sound he made was none of his sounds — not the warning, not the still. The sound of a thing reading a door open in a place with no doors. Then the free hand went to the Kala's side and the warmth came up again — the blood-warmth, the deep-down thing — and he held it there against the great dulling hide, and the Kala's breathing steadied, and the steadying was a breath longer than the last one, and the breath after that, and he counted every one.

The crossing was not over. But the count had changed. It was not going down to zero. It was going — almost, barely, by the width of a breath — back up.
