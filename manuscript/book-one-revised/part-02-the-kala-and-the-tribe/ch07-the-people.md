# Chapter 7: The People

There were things in the green that walked like him.

He was up on the broken ridge when he learned it — the high stones where the canopy broke and bare rock came through the green like bone through skin. Sun-scoured, pale, the one place a body could sit with the whole sweep of the low country under him and read it before going down into it. He came up there to read the green before a hunt: the lay of the canopy, the dark rivers of shade where the fat-meat beasts moved, the rot-thickets where the water lay still and bad. The heat lay on everything like a wet hide dragged over the world. The green went on and on, drowning-green, steaming. And down in it, where a slow brown channel cut the trees and the mangrove drowned its own roots, there was movement that sat the green wrong.

Shapes that held their line.

Nothing in the green held its line. The fat-meat beast wallowed and gave to the mud, became part of the mud. The carrion-swarm poured where the death was, dumb, pushed by the smell. These did neither. They moved with will in them — went where they were pointed and came out the far side of the thicket still pointed, and a thing that held its line against the green had a head, and a head chose.

The word came up out of him from far down. From under the long silence he had packed over everything that was before the black water, and it came up whole.

People.

The whole skin of him drew tight at once — the small hairs lifting along the arms, the gut clenching to a fist. The body knew the word before the head could turn it. The body had worn the word.

They went on two legs. Most of them. Big — bigger than him, bigger than the dead men in the wreck, heavy through the shoulder and long in the arm, and the arm came down sometimes to the ground and took the weight and they went three-legged for a stride and then up again, easy, a thing that owned both ways of going. Dark hide, matted, slick with the wet. They moved down both sides of the brown channel at once, the same shape doing the same work, and one lifted its head and the heads came up, all of them, together. He had watched a herd turn as one body without a name for what turned it. Now he had it. A pack. A pack of the upright thing, big-armed, heavy, pouring slow and sure along the channel where the green was thickest, and the green that had tried to kill him every day opened for them and let them through.

The iron taste flooded his mouth though he had bitten nothing. Sudden and bright on the back of the tongue without a wound to give it — some old seam in him opening to put the taste of danger where he would not miss it. Blood without a cut. Fear-blood. He had not tasted it since the hold.

---

He was off the high stones before he knew the body had chosen to move.

Low. Belly to the rock. He went down blind, the way he took a fat-meat beast down a known road — hand to the next root, the next ledge, no part of him lifted where a watching eye could cut his shape out of the green. His heart went against the stone like a hunted thing in a fist. And it was not the pack his heart ran from. It was what came up behind the word for them.

Cages. The dark of the hold, the wet wood sweating, the close stink of too many bodies packed where they could not stand. The big hand closing on the back of his neck from behind, slow, sure, owning the whole of him in the one grip — and the sound behind the teeth that went with the hand, low and thick, the sound that meant *come*, meant *be still*, meant *you are a thing and the thing is mine.* The hand and the sound were one. He felt it now as a weight on the back of the neck, and the skin there crawled and tried to get away from a hand long years gone down into the deep.

That was what the shapes meant. That was what a pack meant.

He went down off the ridge into the deep of his green, into the broken country he had bled into being his a day at a time — where he knew every pinch and root-throw and drop, and a thing that did not know them put a foot wrong and the green took it. He went fast and silent and he came to the rot-hollow under the fallen giant, the seam in the dead wood worn to the shape of his own curled body, and he folded himself into it and pressed his spine to the soft warm rot.

The flies that boiled over every dead and living thing in this country ringed the hollow and did not land on him. A clean width of air pressed to his skin all around, and the rot under him seething with them. He had stopped asking why. The shelf held the asking with everything else, and the shelf did not give answers. The warmth of the rot pressed into his back, and he was small in the dark with the dead wood holding him, and still his breath came fast.

And then the other thing woke.

The far hunger — the hunger no meat had ever been shaped to feed. It did not run with the rest of him. It lifted wide in the dark under his ribs and turned the wrong way. Back. Toward the channel, toward the shapes he had just fled, toward the hands. Not the way it turned toward the blood of the swine, not the iron-bright leaning that wanted the spilling. Something else. Something he had not felt from the thing under his ribs before. It drew him up out of the hollow toward the very thing the fast part of him was breaking its body to escape, and the drawing had no hunger-shape he could read. Away in the legs and toward in the chest, the two of them in the one skin, pulling until the pull sang.

That was worse than the iron taste. The blood-hunger he knew. The blood-hunger he could fight, because it wanted a thing he could name and naming it gave him the edge to hold. This was not that. This reached toward the shapes the way it reached toward the fire — toward warmth, toward a thing held and kept — and he could not name it, and the not-naming left nothing to set his back against.

He dug his soles into the rot and held the body in its shape. One light died green over the canopy and the dark came down loud with the night-things. Two. He did not climb back to the stones. He was the longest tooth in his green. He was the thing the green had not eaten, the thing the swarms spared, the thing the bad water could not drop. He said it in the set of the jaw, in the lock of the back teeth, because there was no mouth left in him to say it aloud. The black water had taken the other. He had felt it go.

And on the third light he was back up on the broken ridge on his belly. The pull had won. He could not not look.

---

They had not gone. They had stayed.

Up on the high stones across the low country — not his ridge, a farther one, a great shelf of broken pale rock that stood up out of the canopy with the green hanging off its sides — there was the dark stir of them now, plain in the morning steam.

A whole shore of them. A people.

He lay still as the rock he lay on, and he watched them the way he watched a herd before he chose — counting, learning where it bunched thick, where it thinned. They were up in the high stones and out along the broken ridge and up in the great trees that leaned against the rock, and they moved between the three like a thing that owned the place, hand over hand through the high green, down the bare rock, the heavy bodies easy in it all.

And one by one they came apart from the mass for him, the way a herd came apart once you had lain on it long enough.

There was one high on the bare stone, the biggest, gray through the muzzle, a great old slab of a thing, and it did not climb and did not glean — it sat, and the others sat below it and around it, and even across all that green he could read the thing that sat highest. The heads tipped toward it. The line bent around it. The longest tooth. A king.

The king did a thing he did not expect. The dragging one — the one with the bad leg — made its slow way up the rock toward where the king sat, setting the ruined limb with care, and the king turned its head and watched it come. Not the way a herd-watcher watches. The king reached out a hand when the dragging one was close and laid it on the bad leg, just laid it there, and held it, and the dragging one leaned into the touch for a breath and then went on past, and the king's hand came away slow.

He had seen the cage-hands use a grip like that — the slow possessive closing, the *you are mine* in it. This was not that. The hand did not close. It touched and it opened and the opening was not release. It was the whole of the gesture. The king had checked the wounded one's leg the way he checked his spear-point before a hunt — not because the leg was his to own, but because the leg was his to keep.

Nothing in his green had ever touched him like that. Nothing in the hold had. He did not have a word for a hand that opened instead of closed. Only the hole where the word would go.

There was one near the king with a pale rip down the dark hide of its chest, an old wound healed crooked, and it held itself half a stride off the king and watched the king's hands and not the green — the next tooth, or a tooth that wanted to be. While the king sat with the dragging one, the pale-rip one moved closer and laid its head against the king's shoulder and worked its fingers through the gray muzzle, quick, busy, the grooming of a thing that knew where to press. The king let it. The king's eyes half-closed under the grooming, the big body going slack, and the pale-rip one groomed and groomed, and its eyes moved — to the king's seat, to the space below the king where the others sat, measuring the distance the way he read a channel for what hid under the stones. Then the king's head came up and the pale-rip one dropped its eyes fast and went on grooming, and the moment passed.

He knew that gesture. He had worn it. The quick dropping of the eyes when the hand turned back. He had done it in the hold a thousand times, and the pale-rip one did it now on the bare stone, and the green did not care whose eyes were down.

And wherever the dragging one went, the others made the slow place. They did not turn it out, did not pull away. He had seen a herd leave the slow one for the dark. These did not leave theirs.

There was a lean one, quick, that went up a trunk and down it for no kill he could see, only to be high and then to be low, the way a young thing burned off what it could not hold still.

Not a mass. Not one big animal made of many. A people made of ones, each one its own, each one read off its own body — and the green let them all through.

Some were smaller. And some — the breath stopped flat in his chest — some were very small, and they did not work, they clung. They rode the backs and the fronts of the big ones, small dark things gripped to the matted hide, carried up the rock and never set down. When one was set down it went quick and crooked between the legs of the big ones, all dart and stumble, and a big one came after it and scooped it up off the rock and folded it in against the warm of itself and held it there.

Nothing in his green had ever lifted him. The rot held his back. The stone held his back. They could not bend. They could not pick him up off the killing ground and fold him in. The thing in his chest — not the far hunger, not the wrong thing, the other thing, the boy-thing under everything — reached toward the scooping-up with a force that bucked the ribs. He pressed his forehead to the hot stone and breathed through the gap in his teeth and made the reaching go down, because the reaching had no name and the thing it reached for had been taken years ago by the black water, and the reaching would not bring it back.

And the dead. High on the cleanest shelf of the bare rock, up where the sun struck it first, they had laid things out. Squared. Faced. Dark shapes set in rows with the same care all down the line, set so they faced the same way, set down to stay set down and be come back to. One of the big ones crouched by the still ones and did not eat and did not work — only crouched, and laid a hand on a still thing and took it away and laid it back. He could smell them, even across the green — not the reek of the bar where the dead men rotted open, not the swarm-smell of torn meat. Smoke. The dead smelled like smoke, and under the smoke something green, something bruised and sweet, a thing laid over the body the way the hand had been laid over the bad leg. They prepared their dead. They laid them on clean stone in the sun and covered them with the smell of burned green things and sat with them. He had left a hundred dead things where they fell for the swarm to pour over. These carried theirs up to the high clean stone and tended them after the life was out. A people that kept its dead had something he did not have a word for. Only the hollow where the knowing should sit, and the knowing not there.

A pack. A people. A king and the kept young and the laid-out dead, gathered, warm, tending its own, up in the high stones out of the reach of the drowning green that ate everything else.

The shape of what was his before the hold turned over far down in him, under the silence, under all the years of black. He could not reach the thing itself. He pressed at it the way he pressed at a stone he could not lift, and it did not lift, and he was left with only the shape of its hole — the smooth edge of a thing that used to sit there and sat there no longer.

Tribe.

---

He should have gone. Every fast part of him said *go, go deep, go where the hands cannot reach.* A thing this big, this many, would sweep his green clean and not notice the sweeping. The longest tooth was still only one tooth, and he had seen what the many did to the one. He had worn it on the neck. He had worn the hand, and the three teeth they tore from the front of his mouth trying for everything else he would not give. The many were how the one was unmade. He knew this in the meat.

But he did not go.

He lay on the hot rock and the sun filed down on the bare arm and the sweat ran and the swarm ringed him close and did not land, drawing its clean bare line around the limb, and he lay in the killing heat that should have brought the shaking-sickness and did not, and he watched.

He watched the small ones run between the legs of the big ones. And the want opened so wide it hurt worse than the gut ever hurt — not down in the belly where meat went, but high, up under the breastbone, a pull, a drag, the whole weight of him tipping toward that far high shelf. Warm to warm. He wanted. He wanted to be the thing scooped up off the rock and folded in. He wanted the high clean stone and the heads all tipped one way. He wanted the bending and the lifting up, the being taken off the killing ground against a warm that chose to hold him.

And in the same breath the beast in him drew back hard. Flattened. The lip peeled up off the teeth at the shape of them, a low thing starting in the chest, because *hands*, because *hold*, because the weight on the back of the neck and the sound that had owned him. Want and away, both at the full, hauling him two ways at once until the thing that held them in one body had to split and spill.

And under both — under the want and under the fear — the far hunger did the thing he could not forgive. It leaned toward them. Not toward blood. Not the iron taste. It turned toward the fire-light and the lifting-up and the heads all bent one way with the same quality of attention it brought to the taste of spilled blood in the mud. As if the warmth were a thing to be consumed. As if belonging were a meal. And he could not fight it the way he fought the blood-want, because this lean wore the shape of the thing he wanted too, and the boy and the thing under his ribs both reached for the same light on the same high stone, and the not-knowing of which reach was his and which was the other's was the worst thing the green had done to him yet.

He watched until the light went green and then thick and then gone. And on the high shelf, when the dark came down, a thing woke that he had not looked for. Red. Low and held. A fire. Fed, banked, kept up in the high stones above the drowning green — a held light in all the dark that a thing made and tended and did not let go out.

He kept a coal. In its wet fist of bound fiber, breathing slow, banked low so it lived and never flamed, his one warmth in all the green that he had made and not found. The nearest thing to a fire he had ever managed to hold alone. And there across the country, up in the high stones, was a kept red light, fat and tended, and a coal in fiber was to it what the bones he licked were to the running herd.

He did not have it. They did. And the not-having of it opened a new place in him, and this one wanted only the high red light and the warm that made it, and no kill in all his green would ever close it.

---

There was one that came down alone.

Not the smallest. Young, but coming into its strength, long in the arm, and it came down off the high stone and into the green of the low country, down toward the slow brown channel where the tide of the swamp pulled back and left its stones — down into the very country he had bled into being his.

It moved through the green the way he moved through it. Low when low was needed. Quiet. Reading the ground. And at the channel-edge it crouched, and it did a thing he knew.

It worked the bared mud with its hands. Turned the stones off their seats, reached quick into the wet black hollow under, before the things there could fold and dart. Gleaning the low water. The pale clear-glass things, the crabs that folded sideways, the soft things the swamp left behind. The same crouch he had taught his own body in the dark with no one to show him. The same patience. The same reading of which stone hid a thing worth the reaching and which hid only bad mud. One of the upright people, big-armed, young, doing alone at the channel the exact work he had clawed out of the green to keep himself alive.

He watched it work and the body understood a thing the head was slow to hold. He had not invented the gleaning. He had not invented the crouch or the patience or the reading of the mud. He had dug it out of himself alone, in the dark, and thought it was his — the one thing he had made that no one had given him. And here was another body doing the same work on the same mud with the same hands, and the doing of it was not his alone. It was older than him. It belonged to the hands and the water and the mud, and every body that had ever been hungry by a low channel had found it, and he was one of a line of them, not the first, not the last. The line reached back further than the drowned-deep place, further than the name, into a thing the body carried before the body knew it had a name.

The far hunger turned toward the young one. He set his jaw against it. The young one was not his. The young one was not food. The far hunger did not care about the difference, and that was the thing he carried and could not put down.

And a sound came across the low country.

Worn down by all that green to almost nothing, the ghost of itself — a sound on the wet air, coming the way the smell came, from very far. A voice. Up from the high stones — a call thrown down the country, and the young one at the channel lifted its head and answered. Threw a sound back up toward the high stone, a sound with shape in it, with edges, sounds set against sounds — from down in the head where the wanting lived, up the throat, out through the mouth, laid clean on the steaming air. A thing as easy to it as breath. And up in the stones a sound came back, and the young one answered again, the two of them throwing it back and forth across all that green, the line of sound that ran from the high warm fire down to the lone one at the bad water and said *you are still ours, come back up, we have you.*

Something turned over in him so hard the body made a sound on its own — and the sound that came out was the rasp, the cough with the growl riding its back, the thing the throat made now where words used to be made. He clapped his hand over his mouth in the wet green dark, the way he had learned, to keep the rest of the sound in, to keep his shape from giving itself up.

And under the hand he wanted a sound to set against that far clear call. One sound. His. The one that meant *him* and not a thing, to throw back across the green the way the young one threw its answer up the stones. So he did the old thing. He went in behind the teeth, into the gap where three were torn out, the dark hollow where the one true thing had always lived — the name his mother had set on him before the ship, before the hands, the name they had never known to reach for and so never taken. He went in to find it and throw it.

And the gap was empty.

It was warm and it was dark and there was nothing in it shaped like the name. He ran the tongue along the scarred edge of the gum, the hard ridges where the slavers' work had healed over fast and clean, into the smooth hollow where the word had lived ten thousand nights, and there was no word in it. Only the gap. Only the warmth that came up slow from the deep of him and lay down full in the place the name used to be — the shape of the thing that was gone and nothing like it. The warmth had the exact weight of the word. The body remembered the shape of the name the way the rot-hollow remembered the shape of his curled body — worn in, pressed deep, and empty.

The black water had taken it. Not the men. The men had never reached it. And tonight he learned again what the taking cost him — that he had nothing to send back, no shape to set against a young thing calling easy up a slope of bare stone to a fire that was fed.

He took his hand off his mouth.

There was no sound to keep in. He had none to throw.

Down across the low country the young one gathered its gleaning up against its chest and turned, and climbed — up out of the bad water, up the green, up the bare rock to the high stones where the red fire held and the heads were tipped one way and the small ones were scooped up off the rock against the warm. It went up to the thing that waited, fed, that did not go out.

It did not look down at his green. It was too far, and the light was dead, and he was one dark shape pressed into a country of dark shapes, the longest tooth on the whole low country gone flat and small and still to hide from a people.

But the swamp would pull back from these same stones at the same hour. And the young one would come down to the bared mud on the same string as the tide. And the second mouth in him, the one with no name left behind it to feed it, the one that leaned and leaned toward the warm across the green and found nothing to lean on —

it had already started counting the lights between.
