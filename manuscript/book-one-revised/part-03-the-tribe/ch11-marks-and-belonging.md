# Chapter 11: Fear and Respect

The skins waited until the dark after the fire, when the high stone was his and the Kala had folded into her own still place.

He had carried them up from the wreck for a moon, rolled in fat-soaked hide against the wet, and had not opened them. There had been no place in him for opening. The shore had given him only its own laws, and the laws of the shore did not read marks. But up here among the Vorth something had loosened. The sounds were coming back, one stolen at a time, laid in the gap behind his teeth — low, high, come, the gentling, the question-sound — and where the sounds came the rest stirred. The body said, before the head ever did: now. Try them now.

He unrolled the skins on the bare dark stone in the red light.

They would not speak.

The marks lay in their rows, ordered as nothing in the green was ordered, line under line, and the body knew past all doubt that they were made to mean. There was a way of taking the rows in through the eye and having them come out the other side as the thing a man wanted to say. He had that way once. He was sure of it the same as he was sure of the name behind the teeth. The reading was gone like the name was gone. He strained at it, going in through the only door there was, and there was no floor.

But the skin showed him a thing past the marks. It showed him a place.

Most of the rows he could not crack — the small busy lines, line on line. But here and there the dead hand had laid down other things, larger, standing apart, and these the body half-took. A shape that was the curve of a coast, the bend of a reach, the bar of black mud. He had walked that bend. The dead man had drawn his green — the channels and the high stone — scratched small on the skin where they were scratched large on his soles. Where the lines bent he knew the bend, because the feet had walked it.

And among the shapes, driven into the dead skin in dark deliberate line: the eye. Ringed, open, looking. And through it, crossing it, the thorn.

The disc at his chest went warm against the breastbone. He laid the worked metal beside the drawn skin and they were the same mark — the true round, the true line, the thing on his chest and the thing the dead man had scratched. One thing, made twice, by men, before the green. The drowned-deep place hummed: I know this. This is mine.

The dead man's lines did not stop at the bar. They ran inland, up channels he had never followed, into the deep green under the high country — and where they ended the hand had drawn the eye again, biggest of all, and around it a shape he took for stone laid on stone, climbing, a made hill. He knew the place. Before the Kala had taken him in, he had followed a fat-tailed thing up a channel and come out under the canopy into a clearing that was not a clearing. Stone laid on stone, gone green and slick, climbing in steps into the dark of the canopy — a hill made and not grown. Channels cut in the old stone, running down, step to step, all the long way to a basin at the foot. And on every face of the climbing stone, carved deep, worn but never gone: the eye. Ringed. Open. The thorn through it.

He had not gone to the top. The body had turned from it, gone still all over, the hide standing up along his arms, and taken him back down fast. Now he knew why. The channels had been for a thing to run down. Dark soaked into the stone past washing. The dead men had known — they could read what he could not, and they had gone up the channels toward the made hill, and the green had laid them down before they got there.

He wanted to know what they had been reaching for. The wanting had a tooth in it now.

The seer came to him at the marks.

It was old past the others, the body sunk in on itself, the dark hide gone ash and near-white. It moved the way the oldest things moved, slow and without sound, so that he looked up from the rows and it was simply there. The milk-pale eyes already on him.

It did not look at him first. It looked at the skins. The young ones looked at the skins and made the dismissing sound — dark scratches on dead hide, no meat, no warm. The seer did not dismiss. It bent its great near-white head down over the rows, following the made-thing back to the hand that had made it. It knew the rows were made. It knew they were a thing that was read. It was the only one of them that knew the difference.

Then it laid its finger on the disc at his chest. It followed the ring with the old pale fingertip, and then the thorn, and made the long low sound — the one for a thing that came from before and meant. The same sound it laid on the squared dead.

Then it did the thing it had done at the loop, and worse. It lifted the finger off the disc and laid it against the side of his throat, where the blood went, where the great pulse ran up under the skin — and held it there. Light. The way he laid his finger along a worn line in the mud, reading the thing that passed under.

He held still under it. The beast came up a little at the touch on the throat — the old place, the place the hand closed — but this was not the closing hand. He let the beast settle. He let the seer read the blood. And the seer went very still. Its milk-pale eyes changed — the change of a thing turning toward something it had not looked for and now half-saw.

It felt something in the blood.

He did not know what it felt. But the seer's finger stayed on the pulse a long time, and the milk-pale eyes changed — not widening. Going cold. The way the seer's eyes went when it read a thing on the wind that meant death. And then the seer did a thing it had never done. It took its finger off his throat and put it behind its own teeth, on its own pulse, and held it there — comparing. Reading its own blood against the reading of his.

The seer's face settled. Not understanding. Something heavier. The finger came out from behind the teeth and the seer looked at it — at the fingertip that had read his blood — and the look was the look of a thing that had touched a snake under the water and pulled its hand back and the hand was still whole and the snake was still there.

Because the seer had read one wrongness. Not two. One.

It was the wrongness he knew: the green that did not eat him, the fever-air that did not touch him, the swarms that would not land. The blood that kept him alive where everything else died. The wrongness of plenty — too much life, too much warmth, the body too stubborn to break. He had always read it as the thing keeping him alive. The seer read it different.

The seer read what the plenty cost.

A thing that did not die on the fever-island was a thing that had burned itself down to the root to stay alive. A thousand lows of the green, a thousand lows of the fever, a thousand lows of the swarms and the rot and the things that hunted — the body did not come through that whole. The body came through changed. The survival-thing, the deep-down thing that made a living thing fight for the next breath — the seer read that thing in his blood and the thing in his blood had been burning so long and so hard that it had burned through the part that knew when to stop.

Not a wrongness added. A wrongness made. The island had not put a thing in him. The island had burned a thing in him out. Burned away the part that knew the difference between living and not-dying. And what was left was the part that did not stop.

The milk-pale eyes looked at him a long time. The meaning-sound came — low, long, the sound for a thing that came from before and meant. But under the meaning-sound, the seer made another sound. One he had not heard. Short. The sound the seer made over the squared dead when the dead had been dead too long and the body was going wrong in the keeping.

The seer mourned a thing that still drew breath.

He did not understand. Not yet. Not the way he would, later, when the blood on his hands was not his own and the gap where the memory should have been was wider still. But the body held the reading the way it held the name — set down deep, where the word he did not have yet waited for the word he would have one day.

The seer did not touch his blood again. Not that low. Not any low after.

It looked from the throat to the disc. From the blood to the eye-and-thorn. And it laid them side by side, two halves of one strangeness — the mark that was his from before, and the blood the green could not kill — and bowed the great near-white head over both, the long meaning-sound coming up low out of the sunk old chest.

He strained to turn it over. The head had no shape for it. But the seer had found a thing he could not find in himself and bowed to the meaning it could not reach. He bowed with it. They were two now, both reaching, neither able. It was more than the bones had given. More than the green ever gave. To be felt for and found and named kept by the oldest eye on the high stone — it filled the near edge of the want. Not the whole of it. The whole of it wanted the reading, the name out of the gap, the word for what had bled down the channels of the made hill. That hunger did not close. But the seer turned. And the seer found. And he leaned into the finding and was, for this low, fed.

The rains broke.

One dark the rain thinned. The next it stopped between two breaths, and he woke in the kept warm against the Kala and did not know what had woken him, and then he knew. It was the quiet. For the first time in the long drowned season, the rain had gone off the high stones and left them dripping and steaming, and through the broken roof of the green the lights came back, far and small and many.

He did not count them. That was the thing he learned, lying in the steam with the lights come back. He had stopped counting them. For the first time in all his counting there was no between.

The tribe poured down off the high stones into the steaming green to take what the lifted rains had left. And this low he did not go down behind them. A big one waited at the head of the run — the heavy old male with the torn ear, the one that had stood a whole season at the lip of the rot-thickets and gone up empty because the soft ground would not hold him. He had seen the man-shape go out light over the skin of the rot where his own weight went through, read the run, bring back the fat-meat beast. And this low he waited with his small dark patient eyes, and when he came he made the asking-sound, the rising one, where, where — and tipped his great head toward the thicket where the ground would not hold him.

He was asking the weakest thing on the high stones to read him the country his strength could not cross.

He went ahead of him. Down the bank, out onto the skin of the rot where he was light and the other was not. He found the run with his soles and the big one came behind him set in his track, foot for foot. He was the eye and the other was the hand. He found the thing and the other closed on it, the great arm doing in one blow what his whole weight could not. They brought it up between them, his kill on the reading.

He was not trailing the strong anymore. The strong was set in his track.

The bold small one brought him a frog.

It had been his for a moon now — the small dark quick one that rode his chest and laid the world's names on him one at a time. It came up the line of his torn voice quicker than it went to any big one, and when the half-grown ones cuffed it, it ran to him. Put the man-shape between it and the cuffing. And the half-grown one read it and turned away.

This low it came rocking up the rock with a thing held proud in the curled small hands and laid it at the fire-edge — a fat green frog-thing, alive, blinking, the best thing it owned, brought to the man-shape to put on the fire.

He looked at the frog-thing. The frog-thing looked at him. The small one looked between them, glad, waiting for him to be glad.

And a thing came up in him that had no name in the green and no use — it came up his chest and out the torn throat in a bark, broken and ugly and good, and the small one startled back, and then made it too, learning it off him, the two of them barking the wrong glad sound at each other over a frog while the fat thing hopped free into the dark and neither of them moved to stop it. A thing a small one had pulled up out of him with a frog. Light. Not hunger, not the deep leaning. A third register.

The Kala laid him with the squared dead.

She came off the high clean shelf in the steaming dark and took his hand in her great dark hand, and turned and went up, and did not let his hand go. He went up after her onto the clean ground where the dead were faced to the sun — the place no foot of his had gone, the keeper's ground. He braced for the arm. It did not come.

She knelt at a clear place among the squared dead and took his two hands in hers and set them on a still small body and showed them, slow, how to square a gone thing — how to fold the limbs straight, how to turn the face up, how the hands went flat and ordered the small clean shape of it into the line of the kept. His hands under her hands. The first thing he had done with his hands on this ground, and it was this.

When it was squared she moved his hands to a bare clear place beside it, an empty squared place at the sun-struck edge, and laid her great dark hand flat on it, and then his hand on it beside hers, and made the meaning-sound again. The one for the kept.

She was showing him his place among the laid-down. She was telling him, in the only tongue there was between them, that when the gone-low came for him, this was where he went. Squared and faced with the kept. She had laid the man-shape into the deepest order the Vorth had — the one that said this was ours, this we keep even gone — and there was a place in it shaped to him.

To be given a place among the kept dead was to be given a place among the living that did not pour away when the hand opened. It was the un-borrowed stone. The first ground he had stood on since before the black that was not lent.

Gull-Marr came up onto the clean shelf.

The gentling went out of the high stones. The small ones felt it first, the bold one slipping off his shoulder and away. And the king climbed onto the high clean shelf and stopped over the Kala and the man-shape, and looked down at them, and his flat eyes did not warm.

The Kala did not move her hand off his. She lifted her head to the king and held his flat eyes, and did not make the keeping-sound. She only held his look with her hand flat on his hand on the squared place, the whole still vast bulk of her unmoving between the king and the thing she had laid among her dead.

The seer came up on the king's far side, lifted one long dark finger, and pointed it — not at him, at the eye bound at his chest — and made the long meaning-sound.

The king looked from the mark to the cut place to the Kala's hand on his hand. And he did not lay the brand on him. He did not turn him off the stone. He made a sound — low, short, flat, the teeth out of it. The thing a strong one laid on a thing it had weighed and would let stand. Not chosen. Not warmed-to. Allowed.

The king had let it stand. Three of them — the seer that had marked him, the Kala that had set herself between him and the law, the king whose word was the law. He was laid into the order of the tribe at every height there was.

He lay in the warm that dark with the rains gone and the lights come back through the broken green, and he did not count them.

The bold small one was heaped against his chest, half asleep, learning the still-sound after the Kala and him. The Kala folded down beside them, the great warm silver-shot bulk, and laid the arm over him and made the still-sound — be still, you are held, nothing comes. He made it back, low and torn, two falls, scraping over the gap where the three teeth were gone. And the small one made it after them both, and the three of them threw the one soft sound back and forth in the steaming dark, around and around, and no part of it closed like a hand.

The far mouth was full.

The belly-hunger he had filled a thousand times; it emptied with the next low and you filled it again. But the deeper one — the hunger that had leaned out of him toward a thing to turn and see him and make him worth a sound, since before the black, since the mouth that had made sounds at him in the dark went down into the deep water — that one was full. Not the near edge. The whole drowned-deep of it, where the lost name lay and the buzzing turned over in its sleep.

He had a name behind the teeth that no one had spoken. He had the brand beside it. And now a third thing lay in the warm dark with them: this is filled, and the name for it is here, this, mine, kept.

He would do it again. He did not know yet what it would cost him to have learned it — that the home, once given, was a thing that could be taken, all at once, by a hand reaching in across the water. He only knew, this low, that the leaning-out was over.

He was held. Nothing came for him.
