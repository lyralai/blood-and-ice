# Chapter 15: The Long Hunt

You did not hunt the thing that ate you with the last of yourself. You hunted it with the second-to-last, and kept the last back for the dying you did not mean to do.

That was the first law Rook laid down, and it was not a brave law. The brave ones went strength to strength against the Hesh and were rolled and held and not given back. He was the smallest thing in the green. He did not go up against the Hesh. He went up against the *ground* it stood on, and he kept the last of himself banked low under the ribs, for the dying he would spend it on if the reading was wrong.

So he went down. Down off the clean shelf in the thick fever-light before the sun, into the steaming dark under the drowned giants, into the rot-thicket where the long body dragged its weight. There he began the hunt that had no blow in it. The reading-hunt. The only one the weak thing had.

---

For three lows he did not throw a thing or sharpen a thing. He only learned the Hesh.

He was done paying blind. The shore, the first fire, the first kill — each one paid for in his own blood before the reading came. The cage had taught him the shape of that lesson: a thing done without the reading was a thing done to you.

He lay still in the warm soup at the channel-edge, downwind, a stone that did not move. The make-yourself-nothing the hands had beaten into him in the dark — the hold-cunning, the old trained stillness — turned now to the watching. The Hesh did not find him. The high eyes passed over the place where the nothing was, because a thing that had never had to hurry from anything had never learned to look for the still small thing in the rot. That not-looking was his whole standing against it. The cage's cruelest gift: the shape of the one thing the predator could not read, carried now like a weapon turned inside out.

He read where it lay up in the heat of the low, sunk to the eyes in the rot-pool. He read the firmer mat it dragged its weight along, because even a thing that feared nothing chose ground that held. The body read the body — the Hesh set its weight as the old male had set his, as the king set his on the high stone: the grammar of the strong, who chose their ground by instinct and never thought to ask why.

And he read the one thing the body would not let go: the Hesh was built for the water and the soft edge. It did not climb. It had stopped where the firm ground began and lifted its head after the old male and not followed, because the long weight that made it the strongest thing in the green was the one thing the high stone had no use for. The strength was its death on the rock. The old male's strength had been his death in the rot. Strong was only ever right ground and wrong ground, and the wrong ground was where he would stand it.

Every strong thing in the green was roped to the one ground its strength could stand on, and the rope was what he would pull.

---

On the third low he found the place.

There was a run the Hesh dragged itself along between the rot-pool and the deep throat of the thicket, the one line of held ground through a soup that would take a heavy thing down. And a body's-length off it, the rot kept its hidden throat — the dark vein of soup under root and leaf that had taken the old male to the chest, that read weight and thrashing and pulled and did not give back.

The Hesh kept off that throat. It read the ground as Rook read it; it was the apex because nothing had ever taught it not to be. It would not set the long weight down where the ground was a lie.

So he would move the lie a foot-width over. The lie where the truth was, the truth where the lie was — and the apex would trust the ground it had always trusted, and the rot would take it.

He had made the green the arm when he brought the old male out of the rot's throat. Now he would make it the arm the other way — not pulling the strong out of the killing ground but pulling the killing ground out from under the strong. The law inverted, mirroring the trick the cage-cunning had already shown him.

---

He did not have a word for the thing he was building. He had all the weather of it, and the weather was the noose.

And this low he was not alone on the firm run.

Marrow came down off the high shelf at his back, rocking on the crooked gait, the high shoulder riding, and squatted in the soup beside him, the curled hand tucked against his chest, the quick eyes already working the bank before Rook had shown him a thing. He had been made wrong where Rook was made wrong, and the wrong-made learned early to read what the strong never had to. The cage had taught Rook the reading. The crooked body had taught Marrow. They had arrived at the same place by different roads of suffering, and the place was the one where the ground told the truth before the eye did.

Rook gave him the work in gesture — the run, the throat, the moved line — and he gave it back faster than Rook had given it, his good hand sketching the weight coming and the floor going and the small thing light off the floor, *yes, this, and then this.* A thing turned over in Rook that had no place on the shelf with the wrong things. It was good. It was only good.

The smallest two things in the green, laying a death for the largest, and one of them grinning his broken grin sidelong in the rot-light. Rook was grinning it back before he knew he had a face for it.

He had not known he had a face for it. The cage made the flat face, the no-face, the nothing they could not read. That he could grin at a thing and mean it — that was a thing the green had given him that the cage had not been able to take.

They worked it three lows more, in the heat when the Hesh lay sunk and the eyes were down. They crawled the firm run downwind, beside the thing that named the bank its own, and Rook kept his name behind the gap where the three teeth were gone, biting it when the body wanted to bolt. Marrow did not bite a name. He hummed, low and tuneless under the breath, *I am here, the small one is still here,* and Rook learned to hunt to the sound of it. It was the first music he had known since the Old One's voice went quiet, and it lived in the same place the name lived, and he did not set it on the shelf because the shelf was for the wrong things, and this was not wrong.

---

They cut the woody runners thick as his arm and wove them across the deep throat, root to root, and covered the weave with held root peeled off the firm run, so the lie read firm. Along the true run they worked the held root loose at its under-veins, the green skin whole on top so the truth read like the lie. A foot-width over. Where the ground held, they made it a throat. Where the throat was, a green floor that would take a long weight one breath and then let it through.

It was slow work and wet work and it cost.

A spined leaf in the rot-mat drove its fire up under the skin of his forearm. The arm swelled and went hot and the fire climbed to the shoulder, and he lay still and waited for it to drop him — as it would have dropped the old male, as it would have dropped any of the strong. It did not drop him. The swelling went down as a tide goes out — fast, leaving the skin slack and wrong, the veins standing blue under the surface like something had drunk the fire and left the husk. The clear-headed came back too quick, the fever-air loud in the ears, and for one breath the green tilted under him, the rot-throat yawning where the poison had been. He put his face to the held root and waited for the ground to find him again.

Marrow watched the arm un-swell and did not come closer.

The quick dark eyes read it — read the fire go in and the slack come out and the veins stand up blue and wrong — and the high shoulder did not drop and the curled hand did not open. He was not afraid of it. He was something else, something Rook had no word for: the look of a thing that had lived beside wrong all its life and knew the shape of it the way the body knew the ground, and read the wrongness in Rook as he read the rot — by the law of it, not the fear of it. Marrow did not need to understand what lived under Rook's skin. He needed to know whether it helped or hurt, and the arm un-swelling told him, and that was the whole of it.

Rook wanted to know what he was. He pressed at it the way you pressed a swelling to find the bone, and there was no bone, only the buzz — the wounds that drew shut, the swarm that rang off his skin, the venom that slid where it should knot and kill. It came apart in the hands before he could lift it. He had no word for it, and no one to ask. He set the wanting down because the work would not wait.

But he wanted it. He wanted it the whole time. Not the things the wrongness did — the not-knowing what the wrongness was. It sat on the shelf beside the seven wrongs, the eighth, and it frightened him most.

---

A trap was only the half of it. The other half was the bringing.

They had to make the Hesh come the moved line, and come it fast, in the rush of its own strength, because a thing read the ground slow and forgot to read it in the rush. The rush was the law's blind side, and the blind side was the only side a small thing could reach.

So Rook had to be the thing it rushed. The bait. The moving thing that woke the high eyes. There was a way that was not strength, and the weak thing had it: stand where the killing ground was, let the killer see you, and read the ground faster than the ground could read you. He could move. That was the whole of his strength, and it was enough if the reading was right.

He told Marrow with the good hand and the gap-toothed sounds — *up the leaning giant, hold the high seat, when he was off the floor, pull and do not stop* — and the high shoulder dropped and he would not look at him, and Rook read off the dropped shoulder the thing he would not make a sound for: he would rather stand the floor himself than hold the rope. The small one did not like the big one being meat.

Marrow had his own cage — the crooked body, the curled hand that would not open. And the cage had made him the same thing Rook's cage had made him: a thing that read the cost of a choice and could not look away from it. He read the cost of Rook on the floor — the small soft thing in the path of the long killing — and the cost was more than the high shoulder could carry, and he carried it anyway. The runt did not put himself in front of the blow. The runt held the rope. And the holding was its own kind of bravery, the kind the strong never had cause to learn.

Rook made him go up the giant anyway. One of them light on the floor, one holding the line. The law of two small things against one large one.

---

He did it in the thick light before the sun of the next low, when the Hesh came off its pool, the high eyes up, the long body sliding its weight along the firm run toward the deep throat.

He showed himself. He stood up out of the not-looking on the green floor over the moved line, the small warm moving thing where the high eyes could find it at last — and they found it. The broad flat head came round, the eyes fixed, and the long body that had never once had cause to hurry hurried, because here was meat on the bank the Hesh had named its own. It came off the firm run onto the line Rook had moved, fast, the rows of teeth already wide.

Then the green opened wrong.

Voss-Marr was on the bank. He had trailed the wrong-blood down off the high stone to see the small thing fail — the heir following the thing he had marked, doing what the strong did with a thing they meant to deal with: not helping, but witnessing the failing that would prove the marking right — and he was on the *old* firm run, the line that had been true three lows ago and was a lie now. His weight came down where Rook had worked the under-veins loose. He read the ground as the Hesh read it, by the green skin whole on top, and the skin held him one breath — one — and then his foot went through and he was down to the thigh in a thing that read weight and pulled. And the long body was coming.

There was no deciding in it. The flesh moved before the head could say it, older than fear and lower down. The cage had put a thing in him it did not mean to put: the body that went to the thing in the soup, not away from it. The hands that struck him had also fed him, and the body could not tell the two apart. Rook was light, and he was close, and he went to him.

He did not pull him up; the soup had read him, and a pull only gave it the thrashing to read deeper. He went flat on the held root at the floor's edge — the true root, the one held line in all that worked ground — and put it under his hand and made the sound that was not a word, the *stop,* the still-sound the Kala made over him. *Be still. The still is the holding.* And the proud heir, the strongest thing of his season, went still on the wrong-blood's say-so with the apex one body-length off and closing, because the body knew true ground when a smaller thing put it under his hand.

The soup lost the thrashing to read. He held the root and did not go down, and the Hesh came on.

It came on, onto the line Rook had moved, because the meat was still on the floor — he was flat on its edge with his hand on Voss-Marr's wrist. Now he went. He rolled off the green floor the breath before the weight came down, foot on the true root and gone, a thing too light to pull. The Hesh set the heavy body down where he had stood, in the rush, and the skin held it one breath — one — and then the rot read the weight and pulled, and the lie opened under the long body and let it through into the soup that did not give back what it swallowed.

---

It went down fighting, the way the strong always fought, the fight that took them deeper. The great body beat the soup to froth, the teeth snapping on rot and on nothing, and the more the strength fought the more the rot read it and pulled. A law strength made worse. The strength that was the Hesh's whole life was the thing killing it, and there was a truth in that Rook could not look at straight — the thing which made you strongest was the thing the green used to take you, and the taking was always already in the making.

Up on the leaning giant Marrow took his turn of the laced vine over the high stub and pulled, the curled hand and the good hand both, the whole small wrong body hung on the rope. He did not pull to lift the Hesh out. He pulled the other way, holding the green skin closed hard over the throat, so the long body could not find firm root to drag itself up. Rook was the eye. Marrow was the arm. The rot was the mouth. They stood the apex where the three met.

Voss-Marr dragged himself out onto true ground a body-length off and did not run.

He stood streaming and watched the apex die, watched the two smallest things in the green hold it under, and the air around him changed. It was not contempt this low. It was not the flat weighing of the heir measuring a rival. It was the thing that came before respect, near enough to it that the body could not tell them apart yet — a strong thing seeing a strength it had no name for, and a hand that had reached for him in the soup when the easy thing was to let him go down and be rid of the wrong-blood.

The easy thing. The heir knew it, and the heir knew Rook had not done it. Contempt he could read. Threat he could read. But a hand in the soup that did not need to be there — that was outside the heir's law, and the not-reading sat in Voss-Marr's chest the way the not-knowing sat in Rook's: shapeless, growing in the dark.

He looked at Rook and did not look away. For one breath they were only two small things and one large thing dying, on the same side of the dying, and the heir bowed the great young head the bare degree the strong bowed to the strong.

Rook had a brother for a breath. He would remember that it was only a breath.

---

It was a long killing, and they held the weave, and the line did not break.

The far mouth came back at last out of its struck silence as the froth slowed and the high eyes stilled and the long body went to the deep stillness. He knew that stillness. He had been it, on a deck, in a hold, with the hand closed on the back of the neck. The stillness of the thing that had stopped fighting because the fighting was what was killing it. He felt the last heat of the long body come off the apex through the green skin and into him, and the thing low under the ribs stood all the way up.

Fed nothing. The kill had no blood — the soup had taken it, the rot had closed over it. The wrongness drew what it could from the fading heat the way it drew a fire's warmth, but the heat was thin and secondhand and wrong. It strained at the strength gone still as it had strained at the dying fat-meat beast on the shore: wanting the killing and finding only the warmth without the blood, and the not-having was a hunger the near hunger could not fill.

He made himself stop the straining short. He did not know what he would become if he fed it. He set it on the shelf — the ninth wrong — and the shelf bore it the way it bore everything, heavier with each thing, and never lighter.

---

He did not carry the whole of it. It was longer than the old male and him laid end to end, and the soup kept the most of it. But he took the broad flat head. He cut it free with the dead expedition's knife and slung it in the laced vine. Marrow took a corner of the vine on the high shoulder, and they began the dragging-home, which cost more than the hunt. The vine bit into the wrong shoulder, the one the curl did not protect, and Marrow did not shift it. He carried the cost where it fell the way he carried everything.

Voss-Marr did not take a corner. He was the heir, not made to carry. But he climbed ahead of them slow and did not climb out of sight, and that was its own kind of carrying from a thing like him. To stay in view of the two small things dragging the head was a thing the heir had not done before, and the doing of it cost him something he could not name.

---

They came up over the broken lip in the dark, shaking, the rot drying stiff and pale on them, and the tribe was crowded frightened on the high stone, the gladness gone out of it, the small ones pressed close.

And Rook laid the head of the Hesh down on the clean stone in the middle of them.

The whole tribe read it. The broad flat head, the rows of teeth, the high eyes that had named the bank their own — laid down dead by the smallest, softest, wrongest thing among them. The pressed-down fear broke open. Not into gladness — the fear was too deep — but into the thing that comes after fear when the fear is lifted by the thing that caused it. Relief. And under the relief, the harder thing: the knowing that the thing which had lifted the fear was the thing they had held in contempt, and the contempt had been wrong.

The old male with the torn ear came and stood over the dead head and over Rook. He made the sound a strong thing made to the thing that had stood between it and the squared dead — low, slow, the great head bowed. The torn ear was flat and the small dark eyes were wet. He had run from the Hesh. He had left Rook at the channel-edge. The knowing of it was in the bowing — not gratitude but the attempt to carry the weight of the running alongside the weight of the saving, and the two weights did not balance.

The half-grown ones circled the head and the small scarred thing that had carried it. The bold small one rode his shoulder at last, the keeping-place become the thing that kept. It pressed against his neck and made the small running sound, not the frightened pressing of the low before but the other pressing, the one that said *you are the thing I run to,* and Rook felt it land in the far hunger like a coal dropped in a dry bank — small, bright, and spreading.

Marrow stood at the edge on his crooked gait and let them have Rook, the broken grin gone careful, because the runt read what the tribe was reading before the tribe did. And past the bowing and the circling, the runt read the other thing — the thing on the high lip — and the careful grin was the runt's reading of it, filed without sound.

The Kala folded down beside him, the great gray-silver bulk, and laid the keeping arm over him, and made the still-sound whole this low — *be still, you are held, nothing comes.* And the *nothing comes* did not die half-made in the great chest as it had died when the Hesh had come, because the thing that had come had gone into the soup the small wrong thing she had chosen laid the green down on. He made the sound back, low and torn, scraping over the gap where the three teeth were gone. Her hand closed on his wrist — the same wrist the old male had lifted, the same wrist Voss-Marr had held in the soup — and this low the grip did not pull back. The fear had been real and the choosing was real, and both lived in the same hand, and the hand held.

---

For the king was on his broken lip.

Gull-Marr stood turned in now, not out into the dark where there was no longer a thing to turn toward — turned to the dead head of the apex, to the small scarred thing the tribe circled. The flat eyes did not warm. They went narrow, the weighing look, no letting-stand, the *could* and not the *is.* The strength-king had met the apex, found his strength too short, turned away, and called it the law. The weak thing had gone up into the dark and brought the apex down. Every eye on the high stone had read which of them did it, and the reading was a thing the king could not unread.

Gull-Marr's law was strength. Strength met the Hesh and strength was not enough, and the not-enough had cracked the law the way a drought cracks stone: not all at once, but deep, and the next rain would find the crack and open it.

And lower down, at the king's lip but not on it, Voss-Marr stood streaming and silent, and the air around him was going wrong again.

The breath of brother in the soup was curdling in the high clean light, here where the whole tribe could see whose head lay on the stone and whose hand it was not. He had bowed in the dark when it was only the two of them and the dying. Up here it was the crown — and the crown had been poured into the world for him, and the wrong-blood pulled him out of the soup and then laid the apex's head down where the king must read it. The grudging-toward-respect was going back to a thing with teeth. He could not forgive the hand that saved him, because it was the hand taking what was his. The heir's law was the king's law: the thing that threatened the inheriting was the thing that must be dealt with, and the dealing did not remember the hand in the soup.

A brother for that one breath in the soup. A rival for every low that remained. Both lived in the same heir — the striking hand and the feeding hand, inseparable, the way they had always been.

---

He lay in the warm in the dark, the small one's quick heart at his chest and the Kala's slow vast tide under him, and he laid the new law down behind his teeth, in the gap where the name lay:

*A thing too strong to be fought was not too strong to be read, and the law of the green would bend to the thing that read the ground the strength stood on.*

Not because he was strong. Because he was light, and he read the ground, and he had made the killing ground itself the thing that killed. The high stones were easy in the dark for the first time since the green went quiet, the fear gone off them. Marrow slept near, the rocking gait gone slack, the curled hand open — the only time it opened, in sleep, when the body forgot the cage it carried.

But the high stone had only one thing on it now stronger than the thing he had killed, and a second that had bowed to him in the soup and would not bow again. The king stood on the broken lip, the narrow eyes turned in on him. Below him the heir stood with the rot drying on him and the breath of brother already gone sour. He had made the both of them at once — the one by being light, the other by being there with a true root when Voss-Marr went down. The saving and the threatening had come from the same hand, and the hand could not undo either, and the tribe could not unread either.

---

The seer crossed the rock in the gray before the sun, the ash-pale head turning slow, and settled at his lip. The pale finger found the Eye-and-Thorn at his chest and made the long meaning-sound — the *means.* Then the finger rose and turned, not out into the dark the way it had turned last time, but *up*, toward the broken lip where the king stood. The sound came again.

*This one. And that one. And the two are one telling, and the telling is not done.*

Rook pushed at it and the head came a long way and did not arrive. The seer was laying the two signs side by side — the mark at his chest and the king on his lip — and folding them into one telling: the wrong thing the green could not finish was the answer the strength could not answer.

The seer was not afraid. The seer was not glad. The ash-pale head had the look of a thing long suspected confirmed. The telling had two ends, and one end was the dead head on the clean stone, and the other end was the thing the king would do when the reading was done.

Rook did not sleep.

He lay in the warm with the near one easy and the dead head cooling and the king weighing, and over all of it he laid the next going-up, the one the seer had pointed at. Not the Hesh. The thing on the lip. The king whose law was strength had watched the law crack, and the crack had Rook's shape in it, and the crack would widen or the king would try to close it.

The body kept it. It had learned to keep a thing either way it went — the gladness and the threat, the brother and the rival, the wrongness and the not-knowing. All of it on the shelf together, and the shelf did not give anything back.
