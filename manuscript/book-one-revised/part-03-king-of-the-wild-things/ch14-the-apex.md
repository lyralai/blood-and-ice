# Chapter 14: The Apex

There was a low when the green itself went still, and the stillness was a thing with teeth.

Rook read it in the soles before the eye. Same as he read the deep rot's mouths, same as he read a tide turning in the channel. The high stones woke into a wrongness, and the wrongness was a quiet. The biting cloud that blanketed every warm thing on Saqarro, the swarm that rang off his skin alone by a clean width of air pressed to it — even that thinned and lifted and went off into the high green. The Skell that disputed the kills at the rot-thicket's edge, gone from their middens all at once, like the small fish going out of a pool before the thing that ate the pool came in.

The green had emptied itself ahead of a thing. Rook did not have its name yet. He had only the shape the emptiness made, the hole in the noise where a hole should not be, and the body stood all the way up under it, the hair lifting on the arms, the old cage-reflex waking in the arms before the head had a say.

Something was coming up into his green that the green was afraid of.

---

The Vorth knew it before he did, and how they knew it was the worst telling of all.

Rook had read them the season through — loud, glad, grieving, the whole weather of them learned in the body where he learned everything. He had not known the tribe afraid. They were the strong, the great gray bulk and the main-strength close and the hand that turned a thing off a rock with no thought in it. Fear was the weak thing's weather. The thing he had brought up among them. Not theirs.

This low they had it. It moved down the shelf from mouth to mouth, flat and pressed-down and small in the great chests — the sound a strong thing made when it had met the thing that was stronger. The big ones gathered the small ones in. The half-grown ones that cuffed and contested the order of the stone every waking went quiet and crowded to the high rock.

Even Voss-Marr had it.

That was the telling Rook could not read past. Gull-Marr's heir, the prime blood, the one they raised to hold the stone after — he stood among the half-grown with the fear pressed down in his big chest like the rest. For one breath he was not above Rook at all. They were two things in the same green that the green had gone quiet around. Rook felt the eye on him and the head came around, and the contempt came back into the set of him.

But it came back the way a thing comes back when a man reaches for it in the dark — deliberate, effortful, pulled on like a borrowed coat. The clean unscarred hide drew taut over the young shoulders, the flat eyes narrowed to the weighing. The contempt was real. It was also no longer the whole of him. Under it, the prime blood of Gull-Marr had felt the same fear as the weak thing on lent ground — and the knowing of that was worse than any blow, because a blow was honest and this was not.

And up on the high clean shelf the seer stood with the ash-pale head lifted into the empty green, the pale finger up — not pointing at Rook this low, but out, beyond the broken lip, into the place the noise had fled. The head turned slow along the ridge-line, following a thing the eye could not find, and made the long low sound the keeper made over the squared dead.

The seer was grieving a thing that had not happened yet.

---

It came the next low, and Rook saw what the green had been afraid of.

He was down off the high stones at the channel-edge with the old male with the torn ear, the two of them reading the firmer mat where the burrow-things ran, the old male's strength set in his track. Marrow was up the bank above them, small and crooked on the high root where Rook had put him to watch the open water — the one high shoulder cocked, the curled hand tucked to his chest, the quick dark eyes going and going where the big slow eyes did not think to look. He was Rook's far seeing now. The strong hunted with their reach; Rook hunted with his eyes and the old male's arm and his own reading laid over the both.

And the green went wrong around them between two breaths, a thing taken out of the world all at once.

Marrow felt it first. Rook did not see the water — he saw *him* see it. The crooked body on the root went stiff, the high shoulder dropped, the curled hand worked once at the air, and he made the small hard click low in his throat, the one Rook had taught him that meant *here, look here, now.* The old male felt it the same breath. The torn ear went flat, the great head came up, and the small dark eyes that had learned to follow Rook's reading did not follow it this low; they fixed off into the steaming dark under the drowned giants, and the great body that had turned a Vorth's whole strength to his aiming went stiff and small and gathered, a thing that wanted only to be elsewhere and knew it could not.

Then the rot-thicket parted, and the thing came through, and Rook learned the shape of the apex of his green.

It was wrong-made, the way the worst things here were wrong-made, but wrong-made *large* — built long and low and heavy to the ground, longer than the old male and Rook laid end to end. The dark hide was slick and ridged, beaded with the rot-water it had dragged itself through. It moved slow and certain, because it had never once in its life met the thing it had to hurry from. The head was the wrongness the eye fixed on and would not leave — broad and flat, the jaw running back the length of the skull, the teeth not the clean teeth of a thing that took one bite and was done but row on row of them, set for the long killing: the taking and the holding and the rolling and the not giving back. The small eyes rode high on the flat skull, set to watch the water and the bank both at once. They were not the flat eyes of the king that weighed a thing and let it stand. They did not weigh. They had never once had cause to.

It did not look at them as a thing to be reckoned. It looked at them the way the tide looked at the shore: a thing it would reach when it reached it.

The old male made the pressed-down fear-sound deep in the great chest, and folded into it was the one word — a low hard shape the tribe had thrown down the shelf since the green went quiet, the name the Vorth laid on the thing that ran them: *the Hesh.* And the great dark eyes found Rook — found him and held him for the length of one breath, and in that breath Rook saw the thing he had not thought to read on the old male's face: not apology, not gratitude, not the naming-sound from the bank. A reckoning. Fast, cold, the cold math of a body that had bowed its head one low and was weighing the weak thing against the living thing the next. Rook was not the safer bet. The arm that had aimed him was the arm that would not reach far enough, and the old male knew it, and the bowing was already spent currency, and the torn ear went flat, and the great body turned and went.

Not at the Hesh. Away from it, up the bank, fast, the main-strength bent the one way it knew to bend when the eye could not save it. He did not look to see if Rook came; the strong did not wait on the weak when the apex was in the green. The naming-sound from the bank had been real. So was this. Both were the same animal: the old male did what survived, and surviving did not remember who pulled you from the soup.

Rook was left at the channel-edge with the thing coming on, slow, certain, and the hunger in him gone silent over the long low body, struck dumb the same as it had gone silent over the dying fat-meat beast — not with wanting. With the knowing of meat that had met the thing that ate it.

The wrongness did not cower.

That was the wrong thing, and Rook marked it on the shelf where the weight of it would sit. The last time the apex had come near him — the print in the mud, the smell, the trace of a thing that had passed through — the wrongness had flattened itself against the base of his spine, small and silent and cold, a dog before a hand it knew. That had been the print. This was the thing itself. And the wrongness did not cower. It went still — not the pressed-low still of fear but a listening still, the still of a thing that has heard a sound it cannot place and is turning the whole of its attention to the placing of it. The wrongness was reading the Hesh the way Rook read the green. And the reading was not his.

---

Rook did not run. The flesh did not let him run, and it was not bravery; it was older than bravery and lower down. It was the hold-cunning, the old trained stillness the hands had beaten into the dark of him. The old male had run, and the running was a moving thing, and a moving thing woke the high eyes set to watch the bank. So Rook did not run. He went still, the whole of him, low to the firm mat, a stone that did not move, the way he had crept under the notice of the hands in the hold. And the high eyes passed over the place where the nothing was and followed the moving thing instead, the long heavy body shifting its weight after the old male, and the channel-edge where Rook lay went empty of its attention, and he was spared.

Rook was spared the way the swarm spared him. The Hesh dragged its long death past him a spear-throw off, the high eyes blind to him, and even pressed flat in the soup he was straining at the *why* — why the thing built for the long killing dragged its weight past the one small soft thing in reach and did not turn the broad head. He wanted the knowing of it so hard his teeth ached. It would not come. There was no word in him for it and no breath to chase it with, only the flat fact of being alive and unfound where the strong had run to ground.

It did not catch the old male. He was too far up the firm bank, and the Hesh was a thing of the water and the soft edge and did not climb to the high stone. It stopped where the firm ground began, the high eyes fixed up the bank, and it made a sound.

Rook had nothing to lay against the sound. It was low, lower than the king's lowest, felt in the soles before the ear, rolling up out of the great long body and over the drowned green into every living throat at once. The pressed-down fear that had moved down the shelf — this was the thing that had put it there. Far up on the high stones the whole tribe went silent under it, the silence of meat that had heard the thing that ate it name the bank its own.

The Hesh had come up into Rook's green to say the green was its green, and the high stones had heard it said.

---

Rook learned it that low, lying in the soup until the long body had dragged itself back into the rot-thicket: he was not the apex here.

He had begun to think it without the head ever saying it — stood up onto a ground that held his weight before the whole tribe, aimed the strong like a thrown stone, been named *this one*. Somewhere under all that keeping a thing had stood up in him that thought he had bled a standing into the green that was his.

The Hesh broke it. The standing was a standing *among the Vorth* — the eye against the strength. The green had its own apex, and the apex did not weigh and did not let stand and did not ask. Beside it the whole main-strength of the tribe was meat that ran, and Rook was meat that lay still, spared because he was too small to be worth the turning of a flat broad head. That not-knowing was the only standing he had against it.

---

Rook went up to the high stones in the dark, and the tribe was changed, and the change was the second telling of what had come into his green. They were crowded close, the way they crowded when the rains brought the wet rot into the small ones' chests, the gladness gone out of them. The bold small one found him at the lip, but it did not ride his shoulder; it pressed into him and went still against his chest, and it was not the running-to-be-kept of the glad lows. It was the running of a small thing to a big one to be put between it and the dark — and Rook was the big one now, the keeping-place, and there was nothing he could put.

Marrow came up the rock behind him, the rocking crooked gait quick even on the broken stone, and folded down at his flank where he had taken to folding. He did not crowd, the way the frightened tribe crowded. He had sat the root and watched the long body name the bank and watched Rook lie unfound in the soup, and there was a thing in the set of him, in the quick dark eyes going from Rook to the dark and back, that was not the tribe's flat fear. It was closer to a question. He was waiting, the high shoulder low, to see what the small thing that had been spared would do with the sparing. And under the question, where the caution lived, another thing — small, held close, wrong in the cautious body the way the wrongness was wrong in Rook's. The quick dark eyes went to the dark where the Hesh had gone and came back, and in the coming back there was something that was not fear. It was the look of a thing that had lived small and careful its whole life and had never once wanted to be large, watching the one thing it followed decide to be large, and wanting that too — wanting it the way the crippled want the sprint, with the whole body, and knowing they cannot.

The Kala folded down on his other side, the great gray-silver bulk, and laid the keeping arm over him. Her hand found his wrist — the same wrist the old male had lifted on the bank — and her fingers closed on it the way they had closed in the fever-fog, when she chose him. Then they opened. The hand pulled back, slow, not letting go of him but letting go of something — the sureness, the choosing-thing. She made the still-sound, but it was not whole. *Be still, you are held* — and the *nothing comes* died in the great chest half-made, because something had come. She made instead the low pressed fear-sound, soft, into the warm. The thing that had chosen him out of the fever-fog when he was a wrong thing the green could not finish, the warm wall at his back the whole season — she was afraid. And the hand that had reached and pulled back was what the fear had cost her. The chooser had been chosen from. The grip that had held him was a grip that could be torn loose, and the knowing of it was in the pulled-back hand and the sound that would not finish.

And up on his broken lip the king did not stand turned to the bank, marking the place the weak thing had learned to stand. He stood turned out, into the dark where the Hesh had gone, the heavy flat-eyed bulk of him still. But the stillness on him was wrong. Gull-Marr stood with one foot on the high stone and one foot on the ground beside it. Not the square planted stance that held the tribe. One foot down, one foot up — caught between the high ground that was his law and the low ground the law could not save. The tribe would not have read it. Rook read it the way he read the rot's law: the king whose whole life was the high stone had felt the ground under it go soft, and he was standing with one foot in the place he might have to run to.

Gull-Marr was afraid too. The king whose law was strength had met the one thing his law had no answer for, and there was no brand laid in the flat tongue that would turn the long certain weight off the ground it had claimed.

Rook lay in the warm and read the no-answer off him, and a thing turned over in him, low down, where he read everything.

---

Rook did not sleep that low. He lay with the small one against his chest and the Kala's fear soft in the dark and turned the thing over, the way the flesh turned a thing it must solve.

The strong ran from it. A thing that met the Hesh strength to strength was rolled and held and did not come back, and a king who was rolled was no longer king. So the king would do the strength-answer: hold the high stone where the long body did not climb, let the green take from the edges of the tribe what it took, and call it the law — the strong always called the thing they could not help the law.

And Rook was not strong. It was the first thing he had known among them and the truest. But he had learned the one law the king's law had no room for: there was a way that was not strength, and the weak thing had it.

The Hesh was greater than all the strength there was. It was too strong to be met. It was not too strong to be read. It was too strong to be fought. It was not too strong to be brought — the way Rook had brought the old male up out of the deep rot, not by the arm but by reading the ground it stood on and making the green itself the thing that took it.

Rook did not look at the thing straight, because the body had decided it before the head would say it, and the deciding sat lower than the place where looking was. The strong would hold the high stone and feed the Hesh the edges. Rook would not. There was a thing in him that the green had not broken, that the hold had not broken, that the hands had not found behind the gap where the three teeth were gone, and it would not lie still and let the thing that named the bank its own take from the edges of the only home he had ever known.

---

The seer came to Rook before the light, and he strained at it the low it happened, the same as he strained at the finger on his throat — reaching for the meaning and shut out of it. The ash-pale head folded down at the lip where Rook lay, the small old eyes going over his face the long reading way. The pale eyes went to the high stone where Gull-Marr stood with his one foot down, and something moved in the old face — not surprise, not grief, but the look of a thing read confirming a thing long suspected. Then the pale finger came up and touched the eye bound at Rook's chest — the ringed looking, the thorn driven through it, the Eye-and-Thorn off the dead expedition's relic the seer read as a thing that came from before and meant. The seer made the long meaning-sound, the one for the kept. Then the finger lifted and turned and pointed out, into the dark where the long body had gone, and made the sound again, the *means*, laid on the place the green emptied itself ahead of.

Rook pushed at it and the head came a long way after and did not arrive. The seer was laying the two sounds side by side — the mark at his chest and the thing in the dark — and folding them into one telling: that the wrong thing the green could not finish was the answer the strength-king had not got. The seer was not afraid the way the tribe was afraid. *This one, and that one, and the two are one telling.* Rook wanted the whole of it. He got the shape and not the inside, and the not-getting was a stone in the throat.

The far mouth stood up under it all the same, deeper than the bank's *this one* fed. To be read by the one that read the old signs — read as the answer to the thing the strength could not answer — filled him and frightened him both at once, because he knew the shape of what the seer had laid on him, and it was the shape of the Hesh, and the blood had already decided to go after it.

---

Rook lay in the warm until the light came gray through the broken green, and he kept the deciding behind his teeth with the rest, beside the brand he would give back someday across the water.

The Kala slept at last, the great slow tide of her under his ear, the hand that had reached and pulled back now resting light on his wrist, the fingers closed in the slow tide of sleep. The tribe would wake and crowd closer and hold the high ground and feed the Hesh the edges and call it the law. And Rook would not. The home was real because the thing that ate it was real, and the two came up the green together.

But the bank over the drowned thicket had taught him the harder thing too: the standing he had earned and the cost of it came up out of one root, and there was no setting one down without the other. The green had sent up the thing that took from the kept. Rook was the one thing in the whole frightened tribe that would not hold the high stone and let it take.

And the small scarred thing that read the ground and threw the vine laid down the new law, the hardest the high stones had taught it yet, behind the teeth where the name lived and the deciding pressed against it:

*The thing too strong to be fought is not too strong to be read. And a home is a thing you do not hold by being strong enough to keep it. It is a thing you go up into the dark for, knowing you are not.*

The wrongness turned over under his ribs, slow, and he felt it settle into the deciding the way a key settles into a lock it was cut for. It was not cowering. It was not listening. It was *ready* — the stillness of a thing that has found the shape of what it wants and is waiting, patient as the green, for the low to come when the wanting can move. And the wanting was not his. That was the hardest reading of all, harder than the Hesh, harder than the king's lowered foot: the thing inside him wanted the apex, and it did not want it dead. It wanted what the apex had — the bank, the green, the naming-sound that said *mine.* The territory-instinct that had settled into the den was settling now into something larger, and the wrongness was not afraid of it, and Rook could not tell whether the deciding was his or its, and the not-knowing sat with everything else the body carried and the head could not look at straight.

No standing on the high stones was ever the last one; each one only bought the ground you went further from. And the further had teeth now, set for the long killing, and high eyes that did not weigh, dragging its certain weight through the rot-thicket of his green and naming the bank its own. The king would not go after it. The strong would not go after it. Voss-Marr, born to hold the stone, would hold it — and feel the contempt come back when he had to send for it, and not know why the sending was harder than it was, and not know that the weak thing he had marked on the bank was already past the marking, already moving in the dark the weak thing moved in, the low-old place below the strong eye where the reading lived.

Rook would go. Not this low. Not the next. But he would read the ground it stood on, and find the line up the wall the strong could not climb, and make the green itself the thing that took it — or it would take him, and the Kala would carry the small scarred wrong thing up to the high clean shelf and square it and face it to the sun among the kept.

The spared thing would go up into the dark after the thing that had spared it. Not because he was brave — because the home was real and the thing that ate the home was real and the two could not be set one aside from the other.

He lay in the warm with the small one's quick heart and the Kala's slow tide and Marrow folded at his flank, watching the dark. Over the second mouth, full and frightened both, he laid the going-up. And out beyond the broken lip the Hesh dragged its slow weight through the place he had bled a home into, and did not know yet that the smallest thing in the green had marked it back.
