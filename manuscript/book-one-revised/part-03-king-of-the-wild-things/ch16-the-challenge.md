# Chapter 16: The Challenge

A thing weighed and not struck does not go away. It comes up the rock toward you a low at a time. Slow. No hurry, no sound, no bottom you can stand on, and the waiting for it is its own kind of dying.

Rook had lived in that waiting since the dead head of the Hesh cooled on the clean stone.

The king had not struck then. The whole tribe had turned toward the small scarred thing that brought the apex down, and the strength-king could not lay his hand on a thing the tribe had turned toward. So he had gone up onto his broken lip and let the lows go by, waiting for the gladness of the killed apex to thin off the high stones, so that when he came down at last he would come down onto a thing the tribe had set back down.

Rook had read that law off him a season back — the low he weighed him and chose not to strike. That was old knowing now, laid behind his teeth. What was new was this: the seer had drawn its line, and a king did not weigh forever.

So Rook did not let it be set down.

---

There was a thing in the way of the king, and the thing had a name, and the name was Voss-Marr.

Gull-Marr's prime blood. Strongest of the half-grown. The one the whole rock had watched grow into the next king the way you watch a sapling grow into the tree that will own the light. The line ran through him. When the old strength tired off the high stones, it was Voss it came down to — every low of his life had been the rock telling him so. He had stood beside Rook against the Hesh, and his cunning had been almost a brother's for a hunt. Then it had soured, because a thing was happening on the stones his whole blood had no law for. The wrong small thing was coming up the line he was born standing on.

That was the line Rook was leaping. Not the king's killing — the king's *place*. To challenge the king straight, over Voss's head, was to step over the heir as if the line he was bred into were a run laid in the mud for someone else's feet. Voss had stood at the foot of that line his whole life, waiting for his turn at the top. Rook did not mean to wait at the foot. He meant to take the top while Voss was still standing where the rock told him to stand.

The heir knew it before Rook did anything. It came off him across the crowded rock low after low, the heir's weather turning hard — the proud set of him gone stiff, the air around him cooling toward Rook where the Kala could not warm it. *That was mine. The line was mine. You bled beside me and now you reach past me for the thing I was made for.* He did not say it. He did not have to. It was in how he would no longer set his eyes on Rook, and in how the strong ones who ran at his shoulder went quiet when Rook came up the rock — weighing the small thing as their heir weighed it, a thing that had stepped onto a run it was never bred to walk.

The contradiction sat in Voss-Marr like a stone in the gut. The line that made him was the same line that had passed him over. Loyalty to the blood that raised him warred with the knowledge that the turning did not care for blood. He had been bred for the top, but the rock was choosing otherwise, and the watching strong ones felt the shift as surely as he did. Rook did not unstep. The line Voss was born into was the line between Rook and the home he would not give back.

---

In the lows after the Hesh, Rook did not have a word for the thing he did. He had the weather of it, and the weather was the weather he had worked since the green first tried to drop him: he made the weak thing needed, low after low, so the turning toward him could not go quiet.

He read the country the strength could not cross, and he kept reading it out loud. When the heavy old male with the torn ear took the half-grown down to the rot-thickets to learn the fat-meat beast, Rook went ahead and laid the firm run with peeled root so the light feet could find it, and the big ones came behind him set foot for foot over ground their weight had no law for. They came back up the rock fed off a country that went hungry past them every season before him. When the small one with the quick heart went high after the sap-nest and the limb gave, Rook read the give before the eye and was under the falling small one with his arms out, and the Kala made the long meaning-sound over the small one kept, not-squared, not-faced-to-the-sun. He made the loops. He banked the fire. He marked the deep rot's still mouths with a squared stone, so the heavy ones learned the lie of the ground from a thing the weak thing set down for them.

Every one was a small gift laid where the whole rock could see it. He did not lay them for the gladness; he had stopped needing that the first season. He laid them across the place where the king meant to come down, so the tribe was still turned when he did. And he laid them where Voss must watch — the heir fed off the runt's run, the next-king learning his own ground from the wrong small hands. Each one shifted the line a finger's width off him and onto Rook.

He had learned this on the shore, with the deep rot and the heavy thing too strong to lift. You did not stand against the weight. You made the ground it stood on do the standing. The king was the weight. The turning was the ground. Rook kept the ground from going firm under him.

And Voss-Marr watched. The heir watched the wrong small thing lay gift after gift on the ground the heir was born to own, and each gift was a finger's width of the line pulled out from under him, and the heir could not read the trick of it because the trick was not strength. Strength he could have answered. Strength was his whole law. But a thing that made itself needed was not a thing you could strike — it was a thing you could only watch, and the watching was the corrosive, the slow rust on the iron of the heir's birthright. Voss had been bred to rule the rock. No one had bred him for the thing that ruled by being useful, because the rock had never needed one before.

---

There was a thing that lived in Marrow that Rook had no word for, and it was kin to the thing that lived in himself.

The runt sat on the crooked haunch at the fire's edge in the dark before a light, the curled hand tucked against his chest, the quick eyes reading Rook across the flames the way he read the rot — by the law of it, not the fear of it. The broken grin was gone careful. He had read what the tribe was reading before the tribe did, and the careful grin was the runt's filing of it, set down without sound.

Rook made the asking-shape with the good hand: *what.*

Marrow made the shape back: *nothing.* But the high shoulder dropped a degree, the way it dropped when the runt carried a thing he could not name, and the not-naming sat between them the way the rot sat in the thicket — there, alive, not shown.

The runt had his own cage. The crooked body, the curled hand that would not open — a thing the rock had nearly culled, and would have, if the runt had not been fast enough and wrong enough to survive the culling. The cage had made him the same thing Rook's cage had made him: a thing that read the cost of a choice and could not look away from it. Marrow read the cost of what was coming on the high stones — the king's pride, the heir's birthright, the wrong small thing standing in the path of both — and the cost was more than the high shoulder could carry, and he carried it anyway, because that was what cage-things did. They carried the cost where it fell.

Marrow did not tell him not to do it. The runt had hunted the Hesh beside him. The runt had held the rope while Rook stood on the killing floor. The runt knew what the small thing did when the ground told him the reading, and the runt did not try to unread the ground. He only sat with the careful grin and the dropped shoulder, carrying the knowing the way he carried the vine on the wrong shoulder — where the curl did not protect, where the carrying cost the most.

---

The seer was the thing that broke the waiting.

The ash-pale head came across the crowded rock in the half-dark before a light, slow, the old bulk moving careful through the sleeping piles, and folded down at the lip where Rook lay in the warm with the Kala's tide at his back. The pale finger came to the Eye-and-Thorn bound at his chest, the relic off the dead expedition, the wreck's one made thing the seer read as a kin-omen. It made the long meaning-sound over it, the *means*, the sound with no edge of asking in it, only the weight of a thing that was.

Rook leaned into the *means*, wanting it to open. There was a knowing under that finger, an old buried thing about the disc and the dead men who carried it and the why of him — he pressed his own head at it, hard, the way you pressed a tongue to the gap where a tooth was, and the knowing buzzed and gave nothing. He had no word to ask it with. The seer did not carry the answer; the seer only carried that there was one. He wanted the rest. He was held off it by the gap in his own mouth, and the wanting was sharper than any refusing would be.

Then the finger lifted off his chest and turned. Not out into the dark — the thing in the dark was in the soup of the tribe's bellies now. Up. Toward the broken lip where the heavy flat-eyed shape stood black against the high green. The seer made the meaning-sound up at the king, then down at Rook, then drew the line between them, slow, a run drawn in the mud for a thing that could not read it yet.

*This one. And that one. And the two are one telling, and the telling is not done.*

And the finger did one more thing it had not done the low of the Hesh. On its way back it passed over Voss-Marr asleep at the foot of his line — and did not stop. It crossed the heir as if he were ground, and laid the telling on the king and the wrong small thing, and left the bred one lying in the run the omen stepped over.

Rook had the whole shape of it. There was a telling on the high stones, and it did not run through the heir the rock built. It ran through him. The line was being leapt in front of the only one who could read omens, and the leaping was being named.

Voss-Marr was not asleep. The heir's eyes were open in the dark, and they had watched the pale finger cross him and not stop, and the eyes were not the eyes of a thing that had been passed. They were the eyes of a thing that had been *weighed* and found lighter than the runt it was lying beside. The heir's breath came slow and measured, the breathing of a thing holding itself still with the effort it took to hold a fist closed and not open it. He would not forget the finger crossing him. He would carry it the way Rook carried the name behind his teeth — bitten, kept, not spoken.

The seer folded the pale hand back and went still. The king on the broken lip had watched the whole of it — the line drawn over the sleeping heir, the omen laid on the scarred runt. A named thing could not be left to thin.

He came down off his broken lip.

---

He did not rush. The strong never rushed the thing they had already weighed. He crossed the clean stone, the certain weight sliding, and the gentling went out of the high stones ahead of him the way it went out before the storm — the small ones pressing back into the piles, the bold one already gone off Rook's shoulder and up the rock. Far off at the lip, Voss-Marr had come awake and risen, and he did not come down. He stood at the foot of his line and watched his father go to settle the thing that should have been his to settle, on the stone that should have been his stone, and the watching was a wound.

He would remember that he stood still while the wrong thing was answered as an equal and he was passed like ground.

The Kala's tide stopped at Rook's back. The great gray-silver bulk of her came up over him, the keeping arm closing, and she made a sound he had not heard her make — low, with the teeth in it, set between the king and the thing she had chosen. She would stand between them. The thing that had carried him out of the fever-fog would put her own great body in the run the king was sliding down.

Rook laid his scarred hand on the gray-silver arm and made the still-sound back at her, soft, falling, the one she had taught him — *be still, you are held* — turned the wrong way this low, the small thing telling the great one to be still. Because if she stood between them, the king struck her, and the law of the strength read her standing as proof of the thing he meant to prove: that the wrong small thing had no standing of its own, that the turning was borrowed off her bulk. If she fought for him, he was a kept thing on lent rock that poured away the moment the great arm was moved.

He would not be a kept thing. He had been a kept thing — the grip on the back of the neck, the flat-tongued brand that said *come* and *be still* and named you property, the barb of it lodged where the three teeth had been and would not work loose. He took his hand off her arm and moved out from under the keeping, onto the clean stone, the smallest thing on the high stones, alone on the open rock in front of the whole turning.

The Kala's sound died in the great chest. She held. He felt the holding cost her — a tremor in the gray-silver arm, the keeping instinct straining against the thing she had read in the law, the same law he had read. She let him stand out of her arm onto the killing stone, because she had read the same law he had: the only standing worth the name was the one no arm carried.

And the wrongness, the thing low under the ribs that had been quiet since the den, that had folded itself small against the bone in the presence of the apex and stayed there through the long hunt — it stirred. Not the bright rising of the kill-feeding. Not the old pull toward fire or blood. A steadying. The wrongness planted itself the way it had planted itself in the den, only this time the territory it held was not a hollow in the rock. It was the clean stone. The open ground between the king and the boy. It took the space the way a root takes a crack in stone — pressing in, filling, and the filling held. The holding was not Rook's.

He felt it settle and did not step back. The wrongness had opinions about ground, and the ground it was claiming was the ground he was choosing to stand on, and the two choosings were not the same thing, and he could not tell which one was holding him upright.

---

The king stopped over him.

The flat eyes went down the great length of him to the small scarred thing on the open stone, and there was no warmth in them and no letting-stand, only the weighing — but it was a new weighing now, not the old one. A season back he had weighed a thing and chosen not to strike it. Tonight the choosing was gone. He weighed a thing he had already decided to lift, and the question was not *can it stand* but *how does the standing end*. He was half again Rook's height and many times his weight, the heavy bulk of the high canopy and the broken ridges, the strength that was the whole law of this rock. He could close the great hand on the back of the neck and end the telling in a breath.

He did not close the hand. That was the thing Rook read, and the reading set the shape of what came. If he closed the hand he would be ending him off-hand, with no thought in it, and the tribe had seen the wrong thing bring down the apex he turned away from — it would read the closed hand as the king putting away a thing he could not stand beside. He could not put Rook away quiet. The seer had named the telling. He had to win it in front of the turning, in front of his own heir, so the whole rock read that the line still belonged to the strong and not to the small thing that had walked across it.

So he lifted the great head and made the sound.

It came up slow out of the deep of the chest, lower than the gentling, lower than the asking, a sound with the whole weight of the high stones in it and the teeth out — not the careful teeth he had laid on the old male the low he reminded the strong whose ground it was. The full teeth. The bare ones. It rolled down the clean stone and the whole tribe went still under it, the green going still under the storm before the storm broke, and Rook did not have their word for the sound but he had every grain of its weather: *here. This stone. You and me. Now, in front of them all.*

The challenge. Laid down bare on the clean stone in front of the whole turning, and in front of the heir who should have stood here first.

And the thing the body did then, before the head could find the words to be afraid with — it did not step back.

---

There was a place where the deciding sat, and it was not in the head. The head had all the law. The head knew the scale of what stood over him, that there was more strength in the king's one arm than in the whole of him, that the body could not win this the way it had won everything else — by being light and reading the ground. There was no ground to read on the clean stone. No rot to make the mouth, no high seat to turn the strength on. Only the open rock and the king and the small thing, and the king's whole law that on the open rock the strength was the way.

The head knew all of it, and the body did not step back.

It did not step back because of the far mouth, the one no kill had ever fed, the one that had reached out of him his whole life into the empty and found nothing to hold — and had found a thing to hold here. The Kala had chosen him before there was a thing to choose him for. The old male had lifted his wrist before the whole bank and named him. The tribe had turned, and the turning was the first ground his whole life that had not poured away the moment a hand opened. A thing too light to fight for itself was not too light to fight for the only home it had ever had. If he stepped back, the king's law read it true: the wrong thing had no standing of its own, the turning was borrowed, the home was lent. And then the ground poured away and he was property again, the brand laid back on him in the only tongue this rock knew.

He would not give the home back. He had given a name away once to the hands that took him, all but the one behind the teeth, and he had spent every low since taking the rest of himself back off the killing ground a day at a time.

So he held the name behind his teeth, in the gap where the three were gone, the one un-stolen thing, *Rook*, the word his mother had given him before the ship, before the hands. He bit down on it the stone-bite, the one you bit when the body wanted to bolt and the bolting was the death of you. And he lifted his own small head to the great flat-eyed bulk over him, the smallest thing on the high stones, with nothing in his two hands and no ground to read and no arm to stand inside, and he made the sound back.

It came up out of him low and torn, scraping over the gap where the teeth were gone, nothing of the king's weight in it and all of his meaning. *Here. This stone. You and me.* The asking thrown back as an answering. The challenge taken.

The whole tribe read it. The smallest, softest, wrongest thing on the high stones had stood out of the keeping arm onto the open rock and answered the strength-king's challenge in his own bare tongue, over the head of the heir who was bred to stand here, and the rock went so still he could hear the steam working off the green below and the far small water in the channels and the slow vast tide of the Kala's breath behind him, holding, not coming between. At the foot of his line Voss-Marr had gone stiff as carved stone. Whatever was left of the near-brother was burning off him now. Rook did not look at it. But he felt it set, the way you felt a thing harden that would not soften again.

And the wrongness under the ribs did a thing it had never done in front of the tribe.

It rose. Not the slow settling of the den, not the quiet steadying of a moment ago. It came up the way a thing comes up when the ground it has claimed is challenged — standing, bracing, the warmth that was not body-heat spreading through the chest the way fire-heat spreads through a wall. The clean stone around him went still in a new way. Not the tribe's stillness, which was fear. The stillness of the air itself, the way the air went still in the rot-hollows where the biting things would not cross. A width of dead air, pressed to his skin, the same barrier the swarm would not cross in the den — only now it was wider, and it was not his doing, and the tribe could see it.

He did not know if they saw it. He did not know what it looked like from outside. He only knew the wrongness had stood up inside him and was holding the ground he had chosen, and the holding was not his, and the choosing was, and the two things were separate, and the gap between them was the whole unanswered question of what he was.

---

The king held the weighing look on him a long breath after the answer, and a thing moved far back in the flat eyes that was the nearest the strength-stone came to letting-stand: what one strong read in another when the other did not break. He had come down sure the wrong thing would step back, that one bare challenge laid full-toothed would set the telling back to *is* and not *could*. It had not stepped back. It had stood out of the arm and answered.

Rook had not won a thing. The answer was not the winning. The strength was still his whole stone and there was still more in his one arm than in the whole of Rook, and the clean stone was still the ground his reading could not read. The telling the seer had named was not done. It was begun — laid down bare between them on the open rock, to be finished body to body in front of the turning, the next low or the low after, coming now as sure as the tide. No longer weighed and waiting. Said aloud, and answered, and owed.

He turned his great heavy body and went back up onto his broken lip, slow, the certain weight sliding, and he did not look back. It was not the dismissing not-looking of the first season. It was the not-looking of a strong thing that had been answered on its own stone and must now go and weigh, again, the thing it was sure it had finished weighing.

At the foot of the king's line, Voss-Marr stood in the dark the king had left. The heir's father had come down onto the clean stone and passed the heir's own ground — the ground the heir was bred to hold, the ground the heir had *earned* in the soup with the wrong-blood's hand under him — and answered the runt as an equal, and the heir had stood still and watched it. The shame of it was on him like the rot had been on him in the thicket, and it dried the same way — stiff, pale, cracking where it set. He turned his back on the clean stone and went up the rock alone, and no strong thing followed him, because the strong things read the weather the way Rook read the ground, and the heir's weather had gone cold.

---

The Kala folded down over him when the king was up, the keeping arm coming over the small thing that had stood out of it. She did not make the still-sound this low, the *nothing comes*, because the thing that was coming was named now and a thing on the high stones did not lie to the kept young about the named thing. She made the other sound, the low even one with the gladness folded in — only it was laid on him soft, and there was a thing under the gladness he had no shelf for: what a strong one laid on a thing that had stood where it did not have to stand.

He made it back, low and torn, two falls scraping over the gap where three teeth were gone, the far mouth full at last, the near one easy.

Marrow came and folded down at the edge of the keeping, the crooked body settling against Rook's side, the curled hand tucked between them. The runt did not make a sound. He set his weight against Rook's weight, the way he had set his weight against the rope — not pulling, not lifting, just *there*, the holding that was its own kind of standing. The broken grin was gone. What sat on the runt's face now was older than the grin, and quieter, and Rook had no word for it, but the body read it the way it read the ground: the look of a thing that had decided to stand where it stood until the standing was done.

The bold small one came back to his shoulder in the dark when the rock had gone quiet, the quick heart easy against his neck, the keeping-place become the thing it kept. He lay in the warm with the near and the far, the small one's quick heart and the Kala's slow vast tide and Marrow's weight against his side, and he laid the new law down behind his teeth, in the gap where the name lay, the hardest the high stones had taught him yet:

*A standing no arm carried was a standing you must stand to the end alone. The home worth keeping was the one you must step out of the keeping arm to keep.*

He did not sleep. He lay in the warm with the challenge laid down bare on the clean stone, and over all of it he laid the next going-up, the one the seer had drawn the line to — body to body on the open rock where his whole life of reading the ground was worth nothing.

He had brought the green's apex up the rock dead, and the high stones had only the one thing left on them stronger than what he had killed. He had answered it in its own tongue, over the head of the one who was made to stand there. There was no further left to go but the one.

The strength-king was up on his broken lip in the dark, weighing. At the foot of his line the heir lay awake too, learning the hard new law — that a line was only yours until a thing with no right to it walked across. And under Rook's ribs, the wrongness held the ground it had claimed, patient, settling, the warmth that was not body-heat banked low like a fire in a den.

The telling would be finished the next low or the low after, on the open rock where the only thing in his two hands was the home he would not give back.
