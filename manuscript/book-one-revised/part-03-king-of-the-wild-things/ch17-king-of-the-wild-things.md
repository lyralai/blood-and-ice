# Chapter 17: King of the Wild Things

The strong always came in the rush. That was the law the green had taught him — off the old male in the rot, off the Hesh on the moved line. The strength did not wait. It came sure of itself, and the sure thing moved too fast to read the ground it stood on.

So he did not give the king the ground he read.

---

Gull-Marr rose off his broken lip in the green half-light, the whole law of the strong behind him and the high stones emptied of everything that was not the two of them. The tribe had gone to the high rock and the far edges, the small ones gathered in. The green emptied itself before a killing thing the same — a hole in the noise where a hole should not be. The seer stood apart, the ash-pale head up, not pointing, only watching. Gull-Marr crossed the bare stone toward Rook, the heavy flat-eyed bulk of him moving slow, because the slow was its own telling: the slow said he did not have to hurry to the thing he was going to take.

And the king was not the only weight on the stone. Off to the side, where the half-grown crowd thinned, Voss-Marr stood with the prime blood in him and the king's own line, and the air around him was the air before a strike. This was his. The lip, the seat, the green laid out below — the strong of his blood were going to lift him onto it when his time came, the ripe thing off the branch. He had weighed Rook since the day the wrong thing came up the rock soft, and the weighing was always a held blow. He came to see his birthright kept. He did not move. But the held blow was the loudest thing on the high stone that was not the king.

Rook had stood under that telling on a deck, and in a hold with a hand closed on the back of the neck. The body knew the slow certain coming-on, and it had learned that the slow certain thing was the thing that bent.

Gull-Marr was twice him and more, the great gray-silver arms that turned a Vorth off a rock with no thought in them, the flat eyes that had weighed every challenge the stone ever threw up and let none stand. Rook was the smallest, softest, wrongest thing on the high stones. He had no arm that would turn the king's head a finger's width. Every standing he had earned here he earned without one, and he would not get a second this low.

---

The body decided before the head could find the shape of it. It always had.

Gull-Marr came the last of the distance in the rush, the strength let go, the great arm swinging round to take Rook off the stone the strong way — and Rook was not where the arm went. He dropped low and inside it, light where the king was heavy, gone off the place he stood before the place could know a foot had been there. The arm passed over his back. He felt the wind of it. Then he was at the king's legs, behind the knee, and he did the thing he had done to every strong thing in his green and to no Vorth: he did not meet the strength. He read where it stood. He pulled the ground from under it.

He took the back of the knee with his whole light weight thrown low and sideways — not a blow. A law. The king was a tower built for standing, not for the give. The leg folded the wrong way under the weight it was not braced to lose, and the great bulk that had never once been read went down onto the bare stone, hard, on the place Rook was already gone from.

The high stones made a sound. The whole tribe in one breath drawn in.

---

It was not done. The strong were never done in the first give.

Gull-Marr had the great hands flat on the stone already, the bulk gathering to come up, and the flat eyes found Rook where he had rolled clear, and they were not weighing now. There was a thing in them Rook had seen once, in the old male in the soup — the thing that had weighed the stronger thing that held it and not found the law it knew. For one breath the king was a thing that did not know the ground.

Rook could have fed the second mouth here.

It stood all the way up over the king gone down, and there was a thing the body wanted that was older than the head and lower down: to take the back of the neck as the hand took his, to lay the brand on the fallen king, *mine, be still, you are a thing*, to make him the thing on the deck Rook had been. It had wanted to be the hand for as long as the hand had been on its neck. The wanting came up bright and hot, and the heat of it was the old heat — the same furnace that had opened in the kill, the same fire that had wanted the taste of the spilling. The wrongness knew what a fallen king was. It knew the way the old blood knows a thing it has not tasted in a long time and has not forgotten.

He did not feed it.

He took the standing instead of the neck.

And the not-feeding cost. The wrongness went down slow and unwilling, the way a dog goes down when the hand that fed it has closed. It did not fold itself small against the bone the way it had with the apex. It went with its teeth. The heat banked, not dead, and the banking had a sullenness to it — a thing set aside, not a thing finished. The wrongness had opinions about fallen kings, and the opinions were not Rook's.

He felt the difference. The holding and the wanting were not the same thing. He held the not-feeding the way you hold a breath when the body wants to breathe, and the holding was work, and the work was the first cost of the crown.

---

Rook got the loop off his belt — he always had the loop at his belt — and he did not put it on the king. The king was not a thing that ran. Rook was up on the broken lip before Gull-Marr had his bulk under him, standing on the seat of the weighing, the high stone laid out below him as the green laid below the king.

The king came up. Of course he came up. The strength did not stay down for a thing that did not break it. He came up off the stone shaking the give out of the great body, the flat eyes climbing to the lip where Rook stood on the seat that was his, the whole law of the strong gathering in him to take it back.

And he stopped.

He stopped because the tribe was not where he left it.

It turned when the king went down — Rook felt it turn like a tide in the channel, the whole weight of it before any of its parts. The old male with the torn ear had come up off the high rock to the foot of the broken lip, the great head turned not to the king but to the small scarred thing on the king's own seat. The half-grown ones were crowded below the lip, eyes up. The big ones had come down off the far edges. The whole tribe looked up at the thing on the king's seat, and not one of them moved to the king.

The strong itself had read which one of them did it.

---

Gull-Marr stood at the foot of his own lip, the great bulk of him below Rook for the first time since Rook came up the rock the soft weak wrong thing on lent ground, and he weighed it.

Rook watched him do it. Slow. A thing that should not be, and was.

The strength did not leave him. Rook was not strong enough to break it, and they both had the whole shape of that. He could come up the rock. He could take Rook off the lip as he meant to take him off the stone.

But the ground was gone.

This was the law Rook learned on the bank with the old male's wrist lifted, the hardest the high stones had taught him till this one: a king of the strength-law was only king so long as the tribe read the strength as the thing that kept them. They had just watched the strength go down to a thing too light to pull, and watched the wrong small thing not take the neck, not lay the brand, but stand up on the seat of the weighing and hold the green as the king held it. The strength was still in Gull-Marr. But the tribe no longer read it as the thing that kept them. It read the thing that read the king down.

He could come up and roll Rook and hold him and not give him back, and he would be, after, a strength the tribe had watched be bent — and there was no king in a bent strength, only a strong thing on a rock that had learned to read it. He knew this from the inside, as Rook knew the rot. He weighed the rock, the turned tribe, the thing on his lip that did not take the neck when the neck was there. And the flat eyes did the thing Rook had never once seen them do.

They let it stand.

---

He did not come up the rock.

He turned the great heavy body, slow, the slow that was its own telling, and he went — not at Rook, away from him, the main-strength bent the one way it knew to bend when the ground was gone. He went down into the steaming green alone, no clear ground going down ahead of him.

Rook had seen what the deep green did to things that went in alone. The king had ruled the high stones his whole life, the firm runs and the clean shelves and the broken lip where the tribe slept behind the strength. The low country was not his ground. The rot-vapor took the great gray-silver shape and closed over it, and the king went out of the tribe like a thing gone under a pool.

Rook watched him go, and the weight of it settled low in the chest where the second mouth lived. He had not killed the king. He had done something the king's own law read as worse — he had made the strength irrelevant and let the strong thing walk into the green that waited for everything the stones could not hold.

A king who bends was no king. Gull-Marr chose the green over the bending, and the choosing was the last king's act left to him, the one Rook could not take and did not put on him. The brute, the flat-eyed weigher — he went whole and on his own feet, and the pride in it was something Rook had never read in him until the low he lost him. And in the going the reading broke, and what was under it was a thing he had no word for. The going cost more than the staying would have. Rook had spent a season reading him as one thing, and the going undid the reading, and what sat where the reading had been was too large for the shape it replaced.

Rook let the strength walk into the green with its life. The letting-stand was the first law of the new stone, laid down before he knew he was laying it. The thing in him that wanted to be the hand stood quiet at last, because he did not feed it. He did not know what he became if he fed it. He had laid that law down over the Hesh going under, and he kept it through the king.

---

And he had not forgotten the held blow at the side.

Voss-Marr did not move when the king went down. He did not move now. But Rook was up on the lip, and the air off Voss-Marr was changed, and the body read it before the head could shape it.

There was no rush in him.

There was a thing worse than the rush — a thing gone hard and flat and still, the prime blood standing where the seat it was promised had been taken by a thing softer than every thing he was ever told he stood above. Rook had leapt the whole line his blood was raised to fill, lit on the stone, and now would not even take his neck — and the not-taking was the thing the air off him read back: that Rook had weighed him and found him too small to finish. He bled beside Rook against the Hesh. He stood at his shoulder. And the meaning that came off his still body was the brand turned around — *you stole what was mine and you will not even fight me for it.* The held blow did not come. It went back down into him and set, and the setting had the weight of a thing that would not soften again.

And the strong ones at Voss-Marr's shoulder — the half-grown males who had run at his side since the season they were all small enough to tumble together — went quiet too. Not to Rook, not yet. To each other. The silence among them had the weight of a thing being read. They had been bred at Voss's shoulder for the turn that would put their blood on the lip when his time came, and the lip had gone to a thing none of their line had law for. They were not deciding whether to follow. They were deciding how long to wait before they stopped.

Rook had not made a smaller enemy than the king. He had made a hotter one, the kind that burned low and long, and he had made it with a faction at its back that had nothing left to lose but the line they were born to stand in. Voss-Marr went to the far edge with the prime blood gone quiet in him, and the strong ones went with him, and they did not look at the lip again, and the not-looking was the loudest thing they had ever put on the air.

---

The tribe came up.

They came up the rock as the bank had come to Rook with the old male's wrist lifted, only this low there was no one to lift the wrist; the wrist was already up, standing on the seat. The old male with the torn ear came first. He made the sound a strong thing made to the thing that stood between it and the squared dead, low, slow, the great head bowed — and he did not stop at the bowing this low. He climbed the last of the rock and stood beside Rook on the broken lip, the strong set foot-for-foot at the side of the weak, and turned out to the green, and made the sound again, plain, where the whole tribe could read it: *this one. The lip is this one's.*

And then the others came. Not the strong — the ones the strong wasted.

Grist came first of them — the Skell Rook read off the squaring-ground and did not let the order grind off the bone, the grind-marks of his own people still on him, climbing to the foot of the lip on the side no strong thing watched. He was leavings, as Rook was leavings. But the leavings read the order faster than the strong did, because the leavings always read the order first; it was the thing that nearly killed them. Grist did not come because the small scarred thing on the lip moved him. He came because a king who counted what the strong wasted was the first thing on these stones that had a use for the ground a Skell stood on, and the one with the grind-marks on him had been measuring that use since the day Rook laid the Hesh's head down on the clean stone. This was not the gladness of the turned. It was the arithmetic of the discarded — the cold reading that the new order was the only order that left a run for the wrong-made to stand on, and the standing was better than the edge.

The Torn One came behind him — the Vorth she-warrior the order put outside the order, the long old wrong healed crooked down one side of her, driven to the edges and not forgotten by Rook. She did not bow. She was not made to bow. She came to the foot of the lip and set her feet and turned out to the green like a thing that meant to hold a line, and that telling was plainer than bowing. She had read the new king and found in him the only thing on the stones that would put a weapon in her crooked hand and call the hand a weapon too.

Stub came last, the broken castoff, the one the strong stopped reading long ago because there was nothing left in him to take. He dragged up the rock slow on the half-leg the order would have let the green finish, got to the foot of the lip, and looked up. That was all he had to give and he gave it.

The ones the high stones threw away had come to the thing that was thrown away and did not stay down. The whole tribe turned up to the broken stone where the small scarred thing stood, and they made the long even sound with the gladness folded into it, the rains-break sound — only this low the killing thing that lifted was the king's own flat eye, and it had gone down into the green. The stone was easy under them. The fear was off them. The tribe was whole. And at its low side stood the ones it discarded, gathered to Rook — and these would be the truest of all of them, because they alone knew what the order did to the thing it did not value, and they alone had made the cold reading that the new king was the only thing between them and the edge.

---

And the Kala came up.

She came up last, the great gray-silver bulk, slow, the keeping thing in her small dark eyes turned the proud way it turned on the bank, only deeper now. She folded down beside him on the seat of the king's weighing, the keeping arm coming over him as she had since the fever-fog. She made the still-sound, soft, falling — *be still, you are held, nothing comes* — and the *nothing comes* did not die half-made in the great chest this low, because the thing that came had gone down into the green and would not come up the rock.

Rook made the sound back — low, broken, the two falls scraping where the teeth were gone. And the far mouth — the one that had pressed out of him his whole life into the empty water, into the bones, into the drowned far edge where nothing ever came — it was full. Not fed. Full.

He had a seat the strength walked off of and left him on. For the first low in all the lows he had counted alone, he had a thing the body had no shelf for and the head could barely hold: a place that was his that the ground did not lend. He had come up among them the soft wrong thing on lent ground that poured away the moment a hand opened. This low the hand was open and the ground stayed. He was the king of the wild things, and the wild things were his, and he was theirs, and the body did not know what to do with the fullness but hold still inside it.

---

The seer came across the high stone in the last of the half-light, when the tribe had gone to the easy sleep all down the shelf and the dead king's seat had become a thing Rook sat on.

The pale finger came up and touched the Eye-and-Thorn bound at his chest, the relic off the dead expedition, the sigil that resonated up out of his drowned-deep where no word lived — and the seer made the long meaning-sound over it, the *means*, the one the keeper made over the squared dead.

And then the pale finger lifted off the Eye-and-Thorn. It did not point out into the green where the king went. It did not point up at the lip as it pointed the dark Rook brought the Hesh's head up. The telling was done. The two were one. The lip was his. So the finger did a new thing.

It came back, slow, and laid itself flat against his chest, over the breastbone, under the bound sigil, where the warm blood went — and it stayed there, the ash-pale head bowed, the old eyes shut. The seer was not reading the Eye-and-Thorn this low. The seer was reading *him.* A thing came off the seer that Rook had the whole shape of and no word for: the meaning-sound made low, not the *means* laid over the dead, but a *means* with a question folded into it.

The seer had read the standing and the lip and the dead king. Every eye on the high stone had read those. But the pale finger was not on the standing. It was on the blood. On the thing that closed the splits on his forearms too fast as he dragged the Hesh's head up the rock, the thing the fever-fog could not knot and the swarm rang off by a hand's width. The wrong things. The seer read them. Not as standing. Not as king. As a thing older than the high stones, a thing the body did and the body did not agree to, a thing the seer had no word for and laid the wondering *means* over and did not finish.

And the not-finishing was itself a telling.

The seer was the oldest knowing on the high stones — older than the king's law, older than the keeping, the ash-pale head that drew the lines and named the means for every dead thing and every living omen the tribe had carried since before Rook came up the rock. The seer had a word for the green, and a word for the rot, and a word for the kept dead. The seer had no word for what lived under Rook's breastbone. The oldest wisdom the stones carried could not name the thing that had chosen this boy, and the not-naming was not ignorance. It was the shape of a thing older than the wisdom itself, and Rook felt the seer's not-knowing in his own chest like a cold draft through a gap he could not find the edges of. An answer had edges. This had none.

And Rook wanted the rest of it. He wanted it bad, the dry-throat want of water in the fever. The finger was on the exact place the wrongness lived, and he reached toward the meaning-sound with everything in him, because the seer was closer to the thing than he had ever gotten — closer than the bones, closer than the drowned-deep where the sigil hummed — and there was no word in it for him to take. The seer had the shape and not the name. Rook pressed at it and the press went nowhere. Whatever made the blood close too fast was older than the tribe's tongue, older than the king's seat, older, maybe, than the warm of the mouth that made his name over him before the ship. The green had no word and the seer had no word and his own torn mouth had no word. The not-knowing was a wall he put his whole weight on and it did not give.

He kept his eyes on the green and let the pale finger lie where it lay. Then it lifted. The seer looked at him long, the ash-pale head tipped, and made the meaning-sound once more, the question still in it, and folded down and was quiet. The seer did not finish it either. Kept it on the old shelf as Rook kept it on his, both of them straining at the same shape from the two sides of it and neither reaching the middle.

---

Rook did not sleep that low.

He sat on the seat of the king's weighing with the green laid out below, the tribe easy behind him, the discarded ones gathered at the low side of the stone, the Kala's great slow tide at his side, the bold heart warm on his shoulder.

The high stones gave him two things this low, and they came up together, and there was no setting one down without the other.

The first was the crown. The wild things were his. The wanting that reached out of him into nothing his whole life — into the bones, into the empty water, since the warm of a mouth he could not find anymore made a word over him before the ship took the word — had a thing to lean on at last. It leaned. The thing held.

The second was the seer's finger on his blood. The wondering *means.* The thing older than the crown, that he reached for tonight and could not close his hand on.

And under the fullness the body read a plain thing and a hard one, and it was new. The high stone had nothing left on it stronger than him. A thing with nothing left to fight went still, and the still thing waited. But there was more under the plain reading, and the more sat in the chest where the fullness sat and pressed against it: he had something to lose.

Every low before this one, the body had known what the taking looked like — the hand opened, the ground poured, the name went under the teeth and stayed. The body had practice in the losing. It had no practice in the holding. The fullness pressed against the ribs like a meal eaten too fast, and the hands did not know whether to grip or to spread, and the grip and the spread were the same fear wearing different skins. He had been a thing with nothing, and nothing could be taken from a thing with nothing, and that had been its own kind of freedom — the freedom of the already-empty. Now the far mouth was full and leaned against the fullness and whispered the only thing it knew: *this can be taken.* Every breath of the holding was a breath of the losing, and the body learned the new terror on the king's seat, the one the cage had never taught him because the cage had never given him anything worth holding.

The crown was the highest thing he had ever stood on, and the body knew it the way it knew a tide before the sky showed the moon: the highest thing you stood on was the thing the next taking came for.

He kept the wondering set down. He kept the crown behind his teeth. He kept the bold heart warm on his shoulder and the Kala's arm over him and the green below, and he sat the king's seat in the half-light of the new low, the king of the wild things, full and undrowned and waiting, for the thing the water had not shown him yet.
