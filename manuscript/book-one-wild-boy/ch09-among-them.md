# Chapter 9: Among Them

He learned the high stones the way he had learned his green. Soles first. Fingers. Teeth. The head came after. It always did, dragging behind the body.

---

The first thing he learned was where he might put his weight.

There was a country up here that was not the country the eye saw. The bare sun-bleached shelf looked all one thing, all heat and stone. It was not one thing to the tribe. There was the high clean ledge where the sun struck first, for the still ones, the laid-out dead, squared and faced, no living foot going up onto it but the keeper's. There was the wide lower shelf where the kept fire held, banked and fed and never let die. And there was the broken lip where the king sat, a clear bare stone all around him, like the clean hand's-width the swarm left around the man-shape. He learned that stone with the body before the head. He went to cross it on a mending low, and a great arm came down across his chest, not hard, only sure, and turned him. The bare stone was the king's. He learned it the way he had learned the rot-pool was death — by being stopped at the edge of it.

The king was Gull-Marr. He did not have the sound yet on that first low. He had only the shape of him sitting highest, heavy past all the others, the old silver come into the dark of his hide along the great ridged shoulders, and the eyes that went over him and did not warm. His was the one head on the whole shelf that did not tip toward him. The rest had started to. He looked at him the way a man looks at a wrong thing — a thing that should not be, and is, and has not yet been turned over to see what is under it.

He filed it. *The king has not chosen him.* The keeper had chosen him, and the old silver one had bowed to the mark at his chest, but the king had only looked, and looked away. A thing the king had not chosen was a thing on borrowed stone — the ground that poured away the moment the hand that lent it opened. He had lived his whole life on it.

But the king had a problem now that was not there before the burning man-shape came up off the channel-stones. The seer had bowed to the mark. The tribe had seen it. A thing the old blood knew was not a thing the king could unmake without cost, and the cost was the one thing Gull-Marr had never paid on his stone: the appearance of yielding to a claim he did not make. So he did not yield. He waited. And the waiting was a blade held flat between two hands, and he was the thing between them.

---

The second thing he learned was that they had sounds for everything, and the sounds were a country too.

The keeper made the gentling sound at him, low, up and down, no edges, and he had thought it was one sound. It was not. He lay against her warm through the mending lows with his ear to the great slow heart, and he learned the gentling had many shapes, each a different thing. The one she laid on him when the firelight was low — soft, falling, the tail of it trailing off — meant *be still, you are held, nothing comes.* A shorter one, two beats, hard at the front, that she threw when a small one wandered toward the lip, snapped it back like a jerked line snaps a hooked thing: *no, back, the edge.* And one that rose at the tail, asking, that the big ones threw to each other and answered, *where, here, where, here,* the line of sound between them saying *you are still ours.*

He took them all in. He held them behind the gone teeth, in the gap where three were torn, the warm hollow where he kept things. They stacked up in the dark of him, and something turned over. Slow. He had sounds once. There had been a mouth that made sounds at him, before the black, low and soft and falling, and he had made them back, and the making-back meant they were two and not alone. He had lost it. But he had theirs now. He stole them one low at a time, building back a thing he had lost the shape of, with another body's names.

The tribe watched him take them. Not all of them. Some. The ones who came close enough to hear the torn shapes he made back at the keeper. They watched the king's face when he did it. They watched to see if the sound he threw would change the weight of the blade the king held.

---

The small ones taught him fastest, because the small ones were not afraid.

The big ones still moved around him with the half-step of a thing drawing back from a thing it half-understood. But the small ones had not learned what should not be, so nothing about him was wrong to them. They climbed him like they climbed the big ones, and made their sounds in his face, close, and waited. This was how a sound was given. Close. In the face. The mouth shaping it slow so the eye read it while the ear took it.

There was one he marked before he had its name, and the body marked it first, the way the body marked all the surest things. It was small even among the small. Wrong-made. One shoulder rode high, hauled up past the other and held there, and the hand on that side was curled in on itself, a knot of fingers that would not open. It did not walk so much as rock — a crooked, swaying gait, the high shoulder leading, the bad leg swinging out and round. And it was quick. That was what the eye snagged on. A thing made that wrong had no business being quick, and this one was quicker than the straight-made ones, darting between the big legs, taking the gap before it closed.

He knew it before he knew anything else about it. It was a runt — the one the strong meant to leave on the rock. He had lived that. He knew the shape of a thing supposed to be culled and not culled, that survived in the narrow space the strong did not bother to fill, on quickness and wit. He read it off its body the same as he read the king off his, and what he read was *mine, near as a brand.*

It came to him with no fear at all on the first low, the boldest of them. It rode his chest in the firelight and pointed its small curled hand and made a sound and waited, giving the world its names. The kept fire — a sound low in the throat, round at the back. The water in the rock-hollow — another, wet at the front. The high green, the bare stone, the dark coming down, each with its sound, the runt laying them on him one at a time, watching to see if he took them.

He never answered. The beast kept his throat shut and he let it; a torn sound out of him would turn the small face the wrong way. But he was not empty. He was full to the back of the teeth with their sounds. And the runt did not need him to answer. It needed him to take, and he took, and the curled hand pointed again.

This was a wrongness he could not file with the body's. The body's wrong things sat easy — the fever that walked through him and left him clear-headed, the swarm that spared him, the wound that drew shut clean and fast where every other body's went to rot in the wet. But this — a small wrong-made thing laying the world's names on him, free, asking nothing back — this had no shelf to sit on. The far mouth took it instead. The names went down into it and did not fall straight through. Not enough. Never enough. But the empty closed a little around them, like a hand closing on a thing it meant to keep and not crush, and he lay under the runt's finger and felt it new.

The king watched the runt on his chest. He did not stop it. He did not come down. But the watching was a thing the tribe read, and what they read was that the king had not yet decided whether a runt claimed by a wrong thing was still a thing he could afford to leave on the rock.

---

The keeper had a sound he learned before all the others, because it was the one the tribe laid on her.

It came out of the big ones' mouths when they called across the shelf, when a small one needed the keeping. Two falls. Soft, then softer, the second dropping away under the first. *Ka-la.* They threw it up at her where she tended the still ones, and she lifted the great head and answered, and came. He heard it thrown low after low until the shape was worn into him. He learned what it pointed at. Her.

The Kala.

He took it like he took her warm. It was not a name the way the name behind his teeth had been a name — given by one mouth in the dark, his and no one's else. This was a name the many made and the many threw. But it was hers. It pointed at her and at nothing else in all the high stones, and when he had it whole behind his teeth he found he had stopped taking her as *the warm.* He took her as the Kala. He had not decided it, no more than he had decided to be held.

---

There was one low the king came down off his bare stone.

He did not come to him. He came to the fire, heavy and slow, the clear bare stone moving with him like the swarm's clean line moved with him. But this low his eyes were on him as he came, and the gentling went out of the shelf. The runt slipped off his chest and away between the big legs, quick, the high shoulder leading. The keeper went still beside him, her great arm tightening over him, not hard, only sure.

Gull-Marr stopped at the edge of his bare stone and made a sound. Not the gentling. Low and flat, careful, laid down with teeth in it. *What is this. Why is this on my stone.* And the keeper answered, soft and long, the keeping in it. *Mine. This is a kept thing. I keep it.* And the seer came slow into the firelight on the king's far side and lifted one finger and pointed — not at him, at the disc bound at his chest, the eye, ringed, looking, the thorn driven through the looking — and the king's flat eyes went to the mark, and held.

The tribe watched the three of them. The keeper's arm. The king's silence. The seer's finger on the mark that the old blood knew. The king looked from the mark to his face and back, and he did not choose him, and he did not unmake him. He let the borrowed stone stay lent. Kept by one. Marked to another. Borne on sufferance.

But the sufferance had a weight now that it had not had before the seer bowed. The king could not unmake the mark without appearing to unmake the old one's knowing, and the old one's knowing was older than the king's stone. So he held the blade flat and waited for the mark to show what it was worth — or what it cost.

He filed the king's danger where his bare stone was already filed, and let the warm of the rest have him — because three of them had spoken *about him,* and a thing spoken about was a thing that *was,* and he had not been a thing in any tribe's mouth since the hold made him a thing in the worst one. But the mouth that spoke about a thing could close on it, and the king had not yet opened his.

---

The sound came on a dark when he was not reaching for it.

That was the thing about it, after, when he turned it over and could not wear it smooth. Every low he had gone in behind the teeth, into the gap where three were torn, reaching to find a thing to send back into the warm — and every low the hollow had been warm and empty, his own sound gone down into the deep with everything from before, only the brand left, the ugly hold-word that closed like a hand. He had reached and reached and found nothing of his. So this dark he did not reach. He only lay against the Kala in the low firelight with her slow vast heart going through his ear and the runt come back to ride his chest, and the keeper made her still-sound over him, soft, falling, *be still, you are held* — and the runt made a sound back at her, a clumsy shape of it, learning as he was learning, throwing it up at the warm —

and he made it too.

He did not decide it. There was no place in him where the deciding happened. The thing rose like heat off the firestones into the wet dark, up through the chest, up to the gate of the throat where the beast kept its hand. The beast grabbed for it the way it grabbed to throw him flat. And the beast missed. The sound got out.

He made the still-sound. Low, up and down, no edges. Back at the Kala.

It was hoarse. Cracked, half a shape, scraping over the gap where three teeth were torn — a sound that had not been a sound in this mouth since the hold closed on his neck, since before the black. The throat did not know how to do it anymore. It had been a throat for breathing and the low warning-rasp so long that the sound tore coming out, cost skin. But it was the right shape, near enough, and it landed on her.

He felt it land. The great head came up off the stone. The slow heart against his ear did not change, but the breath did, the vast tide of it catching once, and the eyes that had not warmed on the first low came down to his face, close, and read him slow. He had made her sound back at her. He had laid the gentling on the one who gentled, handed it back in a torn and living voice, and there was no part of her left that took him only as the burning man-shape carried up to die.

She made the still-sound again. Soft, then softer. And waited.

And he made it back. Better the second time, the throat learning it on the way out, the tear not so deep. And he understood what he had done. He had not found his own sound; the hollow was still empty of the one that was his. But the throat still worked. A sound could go out of him toward a warm and land and not close. He was not only the place where a sound was torn out. He was a place a sound could still be made.

The king watched from his stone. He did not come down. But the watching was a thing the tribe read, and what they read was that the king had heard the wrong thing make the keeper's sound, and he had not yet decided what that meant for the blade he held.

---

The seer heard it.

It was across the fire, low and folded into itself, the milk-pale eyes half shut, and at the torn sound out of him they opened, and came around, and found him. It looked from his mouth to the disc and back. A thing that should not make a sound was making a sound. A known wrong thing, and now a voice where no voice was. The seer read it as it had read the mark, and bowed the great head, slow, the firelight running red along the silver.

He wanted to know what it read. He pressed at it — the mark, the bow, the milk eyes finding the disc as if the disc were the loud thing and he only the wrapping. He pressed, and the body had no head for it, only the deep slow shock of *known,* and no words in him yet to hold the question still long enough to turn it. It sat in him unturned. Not because he would not look. Because he could not reach the underside of it. One low he would have the words. He meant to.

---

He made their sounds back, after that, low after low. Not many. Not well. The throat was slow to remember a thing it had forgotten so deep, and the words came torn and half-shaped and cost skin each time. But they came. He made the edge-sound at a small one wandering toward the lip, and it snapped back at the torn shape of his voice as it snapped from hers — and the doing of it, the throwing of a sound that *moved a thing,* opened a place in him he had not known was shut.

The runt learned his torn voice as it learned the big ones'. It threw the rising asking-sound across the firelight, *where, where,* and he threw the answer back, hoarse, cracked, *here* — and it came quick and crooked over the stone, the high shoulder leading, the curled hand reaching, and climbed his chest, because the line of sound ran out from him and it followed it home. A warm thing came up a line of sound to him. Nothing in all his green ever had. The green never answered. The black water had taken the mouth that answered, and he had lived in a country that threw no sound back ever since.

---

Then came the low he chose one.

The runt had a name in the tribe's mouth and it was not a name. It was a noise the big ones made at it, short, flat, thrown down and not up — the sound you made at a thing you stepped near and would step around. A dismissal worn smooth by use. They did not call it up, to bring it, the way they called the Kala. They made the noise to be rid of it, and the runt rocked clear of the big feet and did not lift its head as if anything were owed it, because nothing was. The leavings — what was left on the rock the strong did not bother to finish.

And he lay there with the tribe's torn sounds fresh and live in his mouth for the first time since the black, and he looked at the small wrong-made thing on his chest — the high shoulder, the knotted hand, the quick dark eye that read him with no fear when every grown thing here still half-drew-back — and he wanted to throw a sound *up* at it. Not their noise. A sound that brought, not one that rid.

This one he did reach for. This was not the still-sound rising on its own past the beast's hand while he lay still and let it. This he chose. He went down into the deep where his own sound was drowned, where he would not yet bring up the thing that was his, and he found instead a word from the other country — the man-country, the tongue that had made him before any tribe did. Marrow. The rich thing inside the bone. What the strong cracked the bone for and then left — the deep soft worth the cracking did not get. Like him. Like the runt. Too small to be wanted, too good in the deep to die.

He laid it on the throat slow, the shaping before the sound, the way the small ones had taught him. He sent it up — torn, cracked, scraping the gap where three teeth were gone. But this one he aimed. This one he *meant.*

"Marrow."

The dark eye came up. The curled hand stilled on his chest. The runt did not know the sound, could not, it was from no country it had ever heard — but it knew the *shape* of the throwing, up and not down, a sound that reached for a thing to bring it and not be rid of it. No mouth in the high stones had ever thrown a sound up at this one. He watched the small wrong body take it as he had taken the Kala — not deciding, only taken — and something in the runt settled, the high shoulder dropping a finger's width: a thing that was, for the first low, called and not dismissed.

He had made that. The still-sound had got out of him; this one he had let out. He had gone down with his will and brought a word up out of the drowned country and laid it on a living thing on purpose, and it had stuck. The first time he had taken a sound, chosen what it would mean, and set it on the world to hold. There was a name behind his teeth that a hand had closed on once. One low he would open the gap and bring it up. This was the small practice for that great freeing — his own hand learning the choosing the closing hand once did to him, but laid up, not down. Given, not shut.

Marrow. He threw it again, surer, and the runt rocked up the line of it and tucked under his jaw, the knotted hand at his throat where the torn sounds lived.

Marrow. His. The first thing he had named and not been named by.

The king watched from his stone. He did not move. But the watching was a thing the tribe read, and what they read was that the wrong thing on the borrowed stone had claimed the leavings the king had left on the rock, and the king had not yet decided whether that claim was a debt or a threat.

---

He did not have his own sound yet. He reached for it once, this dark, sure for half a breath that the making of all these others would have shaken his loose. The hollow was warm, and empty, and there was only the brand in it. He would not send that. But the others were working at the warm place where his had gone silent — he felt it like weather coming, bone-sure, before the sky showed it. One low the hollow would give his own sound back, and he would open the gap and let it into the high stones, and they would have it. He had learned the hand could choose. He had named Marrow. The rest was only finding the courage of the deep.

The Kala made the still-sound over him. He made it back, low and torn, soft-then-softer, two falls. And Marrow, half asleep now in the red light, made it after them both — clumsy, the wrong shape, learning — and the three of them lay in the kept warm above the drowning green and threw the one soft sound back and forth, around and around, *be still, you are held, nothing comes.* No part of it closed like a hand. And the far mouth, for the first low in all his counting, held it whole.

The king watched from his stone. He did not come down. But the blade he held was no longer flat. It had a tilt now, and the tilt was toward the three of them in the firelight, and the waiting had a sound in it that the body read even if the head did not yet have the word.

The blade would fall. The only question was whether it fell on the mark, or on the keeper who held it, or on the wrong thing that had made the keeper's sound and claimed the leavings and now lay in the warm that the king had not chosen.

He closed his eyes against the red light and let the still-sound have him, and the far mouth counted the lights it had, and did not count the lights between.
