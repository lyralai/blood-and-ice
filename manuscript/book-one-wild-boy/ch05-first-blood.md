# Chapter 5: First Blood

The morning the rain broke, he learned the next law. A den kept the rot off a body. A body still needed feeding. The green did not feed you for staying still.

The sound from the night-ridge did not come again. He had lain in the den-dark with the fire stood up gold and the rain hammering the leaves over the mouth and waited for it, and it did not come, and he went under into sleep and woke into the wet green light of dawn with the coals breathing low and the place around him steaming and close and his — and the night-sound unsolved. He turned it in him once — too heavy for a swine, too quiet for the wind — and found no shape there, no word. So he left it where it lay and crawled out into the dripping green. The body had its own count, and the count had come due.

The mud-crabs were gone. The fat white grubs from the rot-log were gone. He had spent himself on the den for days, hauling stone and breaking deadfall and weaving the leaf-roof tight, and the spending had come out of somewhere, and the somewhere was empty now. The near hunger was awake and loud. Not the other — the other no meat answered. This was the true one, the one in the belly that ground when there was nothing in it, and it ground now, low and steady, while he stood in the steam coming up off the wet earth and felt how thin he had gone in the building.

---

He went to the channels first, because the channels were what he knew.

The tide was out. The mangrove roots stood bared and dripping down to the brown water, arched and tangled like the bones of something half-buried, and the still pockets sat in the hollows of the mud, each a little held world the tide had left behind. He had taken crabs from these. Pulled grubs from the soft wood. He crouched at the lip of the first with the cage-patience — the one good thing out of all that taking — and waited, and read.

Nothing. The pocket held black mud and root and his own thin face looking up through the brown. Nothing that moved. The next was the same. The next. He worked them down the bared flat, pocket after pocket, and where there had been life there was only slow water and the crab-holes empty, and he understood the thing late, with his body, as he understood everything: he had eaten down the easy law of this edge. The pockets had fed him until they were empty, and the tide did not bring them back fast enough for the mouth that had emptied them. They gave what they had. Now he had to go further to be fed.

He went further. Out to where the warm brown water still moved, the deep channels the tide did not empty, and he lay on the wet root-knees with his arm in to the shoulder, reading with his fingers what the eyes could not reach. The water was warm as blood and thick with rot. His fingers found shape in the dark — something hard and ridged gripped to the root, a shell the size of two fists, grown to the wood. He braced and pulled, and the edge of it opened a line in his finger, and the dark blood threaded out into the brown and was gone. He got it loose. One shell. He cracked it on a root-knee and swallowed the knot of pale flesh whole, and the near hunger barely felt it go down.

One shell. A day of building spent against one shell. The cage-lie rose in him, small and quick, whispering that this was enough, that he should lie still and let the hunger pass — and he drowned it. The lie had kept him alive in the hold by making him small. Out here it would make him dead. He stood up off the warm roots and looked down the long flat to the moving channel. The green had stopped giving. It had started to ask.

---

He found them from the high ground the next day, where a hump of broken stone lifted the trees above the drowned flat, and the near hunger made a sound in him that was almost the far one's.

Down where the channel widened into a brown bay and the mud went soft and black, dark shapes lay wallowed in the shallows. River-swine. He had no word for them, only the shape — long heavy bodies the color of wet mud, broad-backed and slow, rooting with blunt-tusked snouts along the bottom. They worked the mud methodically, swinging their heads in slow arcs, flipping stones with the flat of the snout, eating whatever the mud held — crabs, root-fiber, the pale eels he had pulled from the dark water with his fingers. Things he had spent hours pulling from the channels, and they took them by the mouthful. Now and then one heaved itself a length through the muck and settled. They were fat. That was the thing the eye fixed on and would not leave. They were fat as nothing on this edge was fat, fat as he was not — the crab thin and the grub thin and he thinner than both. They were made all of the fat the green had wrung out of him.

The near hunger ground. Under it the far one stirred and leaned toward the dark shapes, and he made himself look off, because that leaning was not a hunger he could trust.

He crouched on the broken stone and learned them. He would not go down there knowing nothing and bleed for it, the same as he bled for everything. They were heavy in the mud, slow, but not stupid. When a heron lifted off the shallows one swine threw its head up fast, faster than the heavy body said it should, tusks bright, and did not settle until the water was still. They kept near the deep channel, always near — three lengths, four, no more — between the fat bodies and the brown edge where the water would take them into a thing he could not follow. To take one, he had to be the thing standing in the four lengths it needed.

He had nothing. Hands and a body gone thin and the cage-patience. A swine was the most meat he had ever stood near, and the most that could hurt him — those tusks, that weight. Between the two of them the near hunger ground all night and would not let him sleep deep.

---

He made the thing to do it with, because the hands could not do it alone.

The deadfall behind the den had hardwood in it, the dense dark wood that did not rot soft like the rest. He took the straightest limb, long as he was, dried at the heart to a thing with no give. The end was broken to a rough point already, and he worked it against a flat of the broken stone, grinding, turning, the wood going pale where the dark skin wore away. It ate the day. His hands, barely closed from the den-work, opened again — a split across the palm, the blood welling warm and red, the one warm bright thing. It would close in three lows. In the wet it should have gone green and foul, and it would not. He had stopped asking why. He put the bleeding hand back on the wood.

The split closed the way it always did — too fast, the skin pulling shut. Only this time the closing had a taste. Iron. Bright and sudden, flooding the back of his mouth the way the smell of the channel flooded the nose, as if the closing itself had a flavor. Not his blood. Something in his blood that tasted of other blood, older, and the taste did not belong to the cut or the healing. It belonged to the thing under the ribs. The wrong thing tasted the healing the way a tongue tastes meat — identified it, weighed it — and went still with the stillness of a thing that had found something it recognized.

He put it on the shelf. The shelf was heavy. But the taste stayed at the back of his mouth all through the grinding, iron and old, and the wrong thing sat low and quiet and pleased.

The point came up sharp. He tested it against his own thumb and it bit. That was the answer. It bites. He had made a tooth. The fire had made warmth out of dead wood and the den had made a held place out of dead stone, and now the dead wood had made a tooth out of nothing but grinding and blood. The pulling did not stop. It only found the next dead thing to pull a living use from.

He bound a stone-flake under the point too, lashed with split root-fiber drawn tight to dry hard, an edge to widen what the point opened. The split across his palm wept red into the stone dust and the dark wood drank it. By the third low the edges had crawled together into a pale seam, tight and hot, the skin around it drawn drum-thin like something stitched too fast. The ache sat behind the seam like a coal banked under ash — not the clean hurt of a wound doing what wounds do, but something else, something that had used the blood and left its mark in the doing. He closed the hand and the seam pulled. It would hold. It always held. But the holding left a heat under the skin that did not belong to him, and he carried it the way you carry a brand you did not ask for.

He slept with the spear beside him. The near hunger ground, and he did not feed it. Tomorrow it ate or it did not.

---

He came down on them in the wet dark before the sun, into the wind.

The wind was the thing. It carried his smell, and a swine's head came up fast — he had learned that on the high stone — so he came into it, his smell going back behind him into the empty green where there was nothing to warn. The mud was soft and black under his bare feet and he read it with the soles, each step set where the foot would not slap and ring and lift the heavy heads. The spear was in both hands. The near hunger was loud. He put it down under the cage-patience and he went.

They were wallowed where he had seen them, three lengths from the brown edge, four. He picked the one furthest from the channel and came around in the wind to put himself between it and the water — low, slow, a thing of mud moving over mud. The body had done this in the hold, made itself nothing, crept under the notice of the hands. He could smell the swine now, thick and rank and rich — mud and dung and the hot animal under it — and the far hunger leaned so hard toward the smell that for a breath he was not the boy creeping with the spear. He was only the leaning. That frightened the body more than the swine did, and he pulled himself back into the boy, into the hands, into the point of the wood.

It saw him.

The head came up out of the mud fast, the small dark eye found him, and the heavy body heaved up out of the wallow faster than the slowness said it could — straight at the channel, straight through where he stood, tusks down. Because he had done the one thing right. He was between. There was no thinking left, only the body, and the body dropped its weight behind the spear and drove the ground-sharp point into the thick of the dark shape as it passed, behind the lowered head, where the fat was, and the wood went in.

The swine screamed.

He had not known it would have a voice like that. A high broken tearing sound, almost a child's sound, almost a sound he had made in the hold when the hands came and the dark was total, and it went through him and the hands wanted to let go and run, and he did not let them. He drove his weight onto the wood. The body bucked under it, huge, all muscle and fat and fury, and he went down into the black muck with the spear still in his hands and in the swine. It was stronger than he was by far. For a long stretch it was not a killing, only a holding-on — the swine heaving toward its water and dragging him with it, the tusk raking his thigh in one wild throw, a hot line opening down the muscle. He did not let go. The tusk had opened him and the opening should have been fire and it was not — it was the hot line and then the heat that followed, the heat he knew, the wrong closing already pulling the edges tight — and somewhere in the holding-on the pain left the leg entirely and there was only the grip and the wood and the dark shape thrashing.

And then the warmth came out of it.

It came out around the wood, dark and fast, far more than welled from his own splits — a flood of it, hot, running over the black mud and over his hands, and the smell was iron, bright iron. The taste of it hit the back of his mouth though none had touched his lips. The same taste the healing had left — iron and old — only now it was not a trickle at the back of the throat. It flooded him. The whole front of his body was soaked in warmth that was not his, and the far hunger opened.

It opened the way a mouth opens. Not a leaning, not a reaching, not the quiet interest it had taken in the fire or the den. The wrong thing under his ribs opened like a mouth and the blood ran into it and it was fed. Not the way the belly was fed — not filled, not quieted. Fed the way a coal is fed by wind. Made brighter. Made hotter. The iron taste at the back of his throat was the taste of the far hunger swallowing, and the swallowing was not his, and the thing it swallowed was not the meat. It was the spilling. The fact of the spilling. The swine's blood running over his hands and into the mud, and the wrong thing drinking the fact of it the way it had drunk nothing else — not the fire, not the name, not the held dark of the den. The blood. The first blood he had spilled on this island that was not his own.

The heaving slowed. The screaming thinned. The strength that was dragging him to the channel went out of the body fast and then all at once, and the dark shape sagged into the wallow under him and was still, and he was lying on top of it with the spear gripped in his torn hands, shaking, breathing through the gap in his teeth, his whole front soaked in warmth that was not his.

He had killed it. The near hunger was silent now, struck silent, because here was the answer to it — meat past all the meat he had ever stood near. But the far hunger did not close. It was still open over the cooling body, still leaning, still bright with the iron taste, and he lay in the smell and felt the thing he least wanted to feel: that the second hunger had taken the killing and wanted more.

Not the meat. The killing.

---

He dragged it up toward the den slow, the same as it had dragged him toward the channel — only now he was the one hauling, and it did not fight.

It cost the whole day, hauling the dead weight up off the flat through the root-tangle and over the broken stone, the bark taking skin off his hands and the tusk-line down his thigh weeping clear. The leg should have slowed him. By the count of a body it should have stiffened and swelled and turned the muscle to fire and put him down. It did not. It pulled and wept and that was all, the same wrong closing, the same wrong speed — only now the iron taste came with the closing and he could not separate the two. The healing and the taste. The healing and the thing under the ribs. All one wrongness, all one shelf.

The hauling gave him time with the body, and the time was not all gratitude. The swine had been a thing in the mud — fat, dangerous, meat. Now it was a dead weight on the end of the spear-drag, and the dead weight had a shape he could not put aside. The head lolled and the small dark eye was open and the tongue hung out and the tusks that had opened his thigh were just bone now, wet and dull. He had ended a thing that ate the same mud his fingers had searched for crabs. A thing that had grunted and rooted and heaved its bulk through the shallows and been alive in the same green he was alive in. It was not the first living thing he had killed — the crabs, the fish, the grubs — but it was the first thing he had killed that could have killed him back, and the killing had changed them both. The swine into meat. Him into something the far hunger recognized.

He got the swine to the mouth of the den and worked it where it lay, the same as he had learned to work the crab, only there was so much of it — the fat under the hide finger-deep, and under that the dark red meat, the iron smell going into the near hunger and the far both. He worked the stone-flake along the belly and the hide parted and the last warmth came up off the opened body — steam in the dawn air, the final heat of the thing — and the far hunger leaned toward the steam, and he made himself look away from the open body and focus on the work of his hands, because the work was all that kept him inside the boy.

---

He cooked it that night in the den.

The fire stood up gold, the rain on the far side of the leaf-wall, and he held the dark meat to the flame on a green stick and the fat ran and hissed and flared, and when he tore into it it was hot all the way through. He ate slow, chewing on the side where the teeth still were, and the near hunger fed at last, filled, went quiet, the grinding gone out of the belly for the first time since the den-work began. The fat was the thing. It sat in him rich and stayed, putting back the strength the green had wrung out of him. This was the first time the green had put back more than it took.

And the far hunger did not feed.

But he had not known it would lean so hard toward the spilling — toward the iron and the running warmth — and then lean still after, over the full belly, as if the swine had only shown it the shape of the thing and not given it. He sat with the fat going into him and the hunger that no meat filled open wider than the meat reached, and he understood the thing he would carry off this green. He could feed the body past full. And the other would watch him kill to do it, and want the killing more than the meat.

The iron taste had not left the back of his throat. It sat there the way the fat sat in his belly — warm, heavy, and not his.

---

He did not look at it straight. He had learned not to. He fed the fire and put his back to the rock, the blood still on his hands though he had wiped them on the grass, the tusk-line down his thigh already gone tight and clean at the edges where by the count it should have been hot and swelling.

The swarms came for the swine in the night, and something else came with them.

He woke to feed the coals, the body doing the work half in sleep, and he saw them first — the biting cloud, the ants and the carrion-flies, come up to the swine-meat he had set on the cool side. They blanketed it black and seething, as the swarms blanketed every dead thing on this island. They ringed the meat and ringed the den and did not come onto him. A clean hand's-width of bare skin all around his body where they would not cross, where they had never crossed. He had stopped wondering at it. Not because he had answered it — because he had tried, and the green gave him nothing to answer it with, and tonight he was too full to fight the green for the reason.

Then the swarm lifted.

It lifted off the meat all at once, the black blanket rising in a hum and scattering into the dark, gone. The green outside went quiet. Not the night-quiet of the rain. The other quiet, the held-breath quiet, the small things gone to ground because something large was reading the dark. He knew that quiet. He had made it himself, creeping on the swine. He was the thing the small things went quiet for. And now he was the small thing, and something was making the quiet for him.

Something crossed outside the den.

He did not see it. The fire was low and the leaf-wall was between, and he did not move to lift the flame, because the body had gone still as the stone it lay against. He heard the weight of it — a slow soft fall too heavy for a swine and too quiet, a thing that set each foot down the way he had set his in the mud, a thing that hunted the way he hunted. He smelled it under the iron and the fat. A smell he had no shape for, rank and wrong and old, a green-rot smell with a hot animal hunger laid through it, the smell of a thing that had eaten things like him. It came to the swine-blood soaked into the ground, and stopped there, in the held breath of the whole green.

He lay with the spear under his hand and his own warmth gone tight and small in him, and the far hunger — the thing that had opened all the way to the blood, the thing that wanted the killing — drew back. All the way back. Not leaning. Not quiet. Pressed flat against the base of his spine, small and silent, the way a dog presses itself low before a hand it knows.

That was the worst of it. Not the weight outside, not the smell. The cowering. The wrong thing knew fear. The wrong thing knew something above it. And if the wrong thing was afraid — the thing the rot could not touch, that tasted blood before it reached the lips — then whatever walked the dark outside the den was not on the same shelf as the swine.

It worked the blood-ground a long while. Then the weight moved off, slow, soft, back into the green it had come out of, and only when the small things started their sound again did he let the breath out of him.

He did not go out. He fed the fire up high and laid every stone across the low mouth and waited for the light.

---

In the light he looked.

There was a print in the soft mud where the thing had stood and read him through the wall — deep, wide as his chest, the toes long and spread and clawed. No swine. No thing he had a shape for. A thing made to hold the ground and tear it.

And there were other marks. He saw them now because he was looking for them. A tree ten paces from the den, the bark stripped in long gouges at a height above his head — old gouges, the wood beneath them gray and weathered, healed over with months of rain. A cluster of bones in the root-tangle below the ridge — not fish-bones, not crab-shells, but long bones cracked lengthwise and sucked hollow, the breaks old and white, too large for a swine and too broken for any natural dying. He had walked past them before. He had walked past all of it, because he had been reading the green for what it could give him and not for what it was already hiding.

The green had gone quiet for this thing since before he arrived. The swarms fled it. The whole island made way for the weight of it as nothing made way for him.

He had read this green for a month as a hard place — heat and rot and fever and bad water that asked and asked and gave only what it had. He had read it wrong. The hardness was only the floor. There was something above him on this green, something the green itself went quiet under, and the swine he had killed and called the most that could hurt him was only the meat the real thing came for.

He crouched over the print a long time. He wanted it to tell him what had made it. It would not. It was one more wrong thing. The fever-air that did not sicken him. The cuts that closed too fast. The swarms that went around him. All of them gave him their shape. None of them gave their name. But he did not let this one lie still. He kept his eyes on it. He kept his hand on the spear. The fever and the wounds were wrong things about him, and he could carry them. The print was a wrong thing about the green, and the green was where the body had to feed.

He had spilled the most warmth he had ever spilled, and something out in the dark had smelled it spill, and come, and read him through the wall, and let him live — this once, for reasons of its own.

The pockets had given what they had, and then they had made him go further. Now he knew there was a further, and he knew what waited in it, and the hunger that wanted more had gone very quiet.
