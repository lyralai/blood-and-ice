# Chapter 1: The Shore

Heat woke him.

Not all at once. In pieces. The face first — wet, slick, a film of warm water standing on him that was not sweat and not sea but something thicker, the breath of the place gone to water and laid down on him like a hand. Then the smell. Rot and green and bloom and something gone sweet past sweet — the smell of too much living all pressed together and dying at the same time. Like a hold full of bodies, except this was a hold the size of the world. Salt in the cracks of his lips. Under the salt, the iron taste of him, the taste of the inside of his own mouth gone out into the air where it should not be.

He opened his eyes.

Green. Green over him, green and gold and black, a roof of it so high the sky was only rumor, only a wet white glare leaking down through a thousand leaves that did not move. Nothing moved. The air did not move. It lay on him. It went into his mouth thick enough to chew and did not want to come back out. There was no wind in it. Only the green, going up and up, dripping, breathing, the great wet weight of it pressing down to hold him where he had been dropped.

He closed his eyes. The heat came for the rest of him, and took its time, like a careful hand clearing a table one piece at a time so nothing fell and made a sound.

---

Mud under him. Black mud. Not sand, not deck, not the straw of a cage. Mud and root and the half-rotted mat of fallen leaves, warm as a body, soft as a body, and under the soft a tangle of roots hard as bone laid in ridges to meet his back. They pressed through the thin wet rag he wore, through the skin, and held him pinned to the warm of the world.

He told his left hand to close. It closed. He told his right. Nothing. He asked it again, and at last it answered, one finger at a time, slow as a thing waking from the dead, the knuckles grinding like the joints were full of grit. The pain came up the arm and into his teeth. It filled him to the back of the skull. He was glad of it.

That was the first wrong thing inside him — the gladness. He knew it was wrong the way a dog knows a kick is coming before the boot moves. The pain was his. The slavers had not given it to him and they could not take it back. It was the first thing in a long time that was only his, and being glad of a thing they had not controlled shamed him in a place he could not reach, because it meant they had taught him that pain was a gift, and the teaching had stuck even when the teachers were gone, and the thing that had been taught sat in him like a splinter he could not dig out with the fingers he could barely close. He held the gladness and the shame of it together in his jaw, and his jaw held, and that was all.

His tongue found the gaps in his teeth. Three. Two below, one at the side. The holes were fresh — blood and socket and a whistle of air where teeth used to be, the gum soft and torn, and when he pressed them the hurt was bright and far down in the jaw. He did not remember them going. He remembered a hand — the big one, the one with the cracked nail that never healed, the hand that had fed him once when the cage was thin and the others had died and feeding the property was cheaper than losing it. That hand. Then the floor coming up. The rest the sea had taken and would not give back. He spat. Red on black. The red sat on the mud a moment, the only true color in all that green and rot, and then a thread of black ants was on it before he had finished looking, and it was gone.

He did not ask *where am I*. The body knew this work and did not wait for words. Hurt, wet, wrong, wake, move. Move or be meat. That was the whole of it. That was everything he had been taught and everything he was.

He moved.

---

The ground ran down to the water in long shelves of black mud, slick with green slime and the wet ropes of roots that came down out of the trees and felt into the dark water like fingers hunting the bottom. The water was brown. Not the live green-black of open sea — brown, still, skinned over with a sheen that threw the white glare back oily, and where the roots broke the surface it did not splash so much as swallow. Things moved in it that he did not see, only the lines they left, a slow seam opening across the still skin and closing again. It was not a sea. It was the place where the green had drowned and gone on living under the water, until there was no telling which had eaten which.

He got his knees under him. He got his feet. The world swung once, hard, and he put a hand to a root and waited for it to hold still, and it did, slowly, like the floor of the ship coming back level between the rolls.

He breathed out. The air was so wet and so hot that breathing was half drowning. The bad air of a fever-place — he had smelled it, in the hold, the swamp-breath that rises off still water at night and lays men down shaking and does not let them up. This was that air, thick with it, the rot-vapor standing so heavy he could see it, a haze low among the trunks like smoke that will not rise. He breathed it in. He breathed it out. He waited for the swimming behind the eyes, the cotton in the head, the first crawl of the shaking-sickness that this air carried the way the cage carried lice. He had seen it take a grown man between one watch and the next — a sailor called Aldic who had traded for a piece of fruit in port and eaten it smiling, and two watches later was on the boards with his teeth going and his eyes turned inward and nothing anyone could do but lie there and listen to the sound of him going. Rook had sat with his back to the bars and his knees drawn up and listened to the sound a man made when the fever cooked him from the inside, and the sound had gone into the place where the useful things lived, the things the body kept without being asked.

He waited for it.

It did not come. His head stayed clear. The air went in foul and came out and left nothing behind. He stood in the worst breathing-place he had ever stood in and his eyes did not swim and his hands did not shake, and somewhere under the hurt a small thing in him went still, and listened — the way an animal goes still at a sound it does not yet have a shape for.

Then something cracked down through the high leaves and a great fall of warm rain came all at once, no warning, just the sky letting go, and it hit his face and he flinched — not from the wet, from the sound. The sound was the whole world at once, a great green roar of water beating on a million leaves, with no edge to it and no end, and none of it was for him. The water did not care. The green did not care. He had been dropped here like a thing dropped, and the world would take him apart slow and never once look up from its work.

*Good*, he thought — not in words, in the set of his jaw, in the way the back teeth came together. *Let it try.* He had been taken apart by hands that meant it. The world did not even mean it. The world was only weather. He had outlasted worse than weather.

---

Thirst came before hunger. The hunger was there — it was always there, low, a second mouth somewhere under the first — but the thirst was louder, a dry rasp pulling at the back of his throat, and the body would be heard.

The rain was a cruelty. It came down all around him, more water than he had ever seen fall, and not a drop of it he could drink, for it ran off the leaves and down the trunks and into the brown still water that he knew without being told would twist his gut if he took it. He crawled along the mud-shelf with his mouth open like a fool, catching what he could off the broad leaves, off the cup a torn leaf makes. It was warm and it tasted of the green it had fallen through, bitter, but it was wet, and the rasp eased. Then the rain stopped as fast as it came. The roar climbed down into a drip, the green wringing itself out, and the heat closed over the place again like water over a dropped stone.

He followed a great ridged trunk to where the ground rose a little and went harder. There, in the split of a rock the green had not yet drowned, he found it — a seep, a thin thread of water weeping clear out of the stone and gathering in a hollow the size of his palm. Clear. Not the brown of the swamp. Clear all the way to the bottom of the little cup.

He drank.

He put his mouth to the stone like an animal and he drank. It was not cool — nothing here was cool — but it was clean, a taste of rock and of nothing, and nothing had never tasted so good. It drew a line down his throat into his chest, and he could feel the shape of his own insides by the wet going through them, the way a man can feel the shape of a hole by the echo his voice makes falling into it. The water found the hunger and went around it, did not touch it, as though the hunger lived in a room the water could not enter. He drank the hollow dry, then crouched and waited while it filled drop by slow drop, and drank it dry again. Four times. By the fourth the rasp in his throat had gone. The low hunger had not. The water did not reach it. Nothing reached it. He was beginning, even now, to understand that.

He sat with his back to the warm stone, wrung out, hollow, scraped down to the root. There was no one to see it. There was no one to make the low sound at him for it — the sound his mother had made, the one that lived in the place where her face should be, the low sound that was not a word but was closer to a word than anything the slavers had put on him. A sound made for a child, made in the dark, made without thinking, the way a body breathes. He could not make it himself. He had tried, in the cage, and the sound had come out wrong, too high, too thin, the sound of a thing imitating a thing it was not, and he had stopped, and he had not tried again. He sat until the worst of the hollowness had passed through him, and the white glare did not change, and the green did not stop dripping, and he was still here.

---

There was one thing the green could not reach.

He had a name once. Before the hands, before the floor that moved, before the cage and the dark and the tar-stink. His mother had given it to him. He did not have her face anymore — the sea had taken that, all but a warmth and a closeness and the low sound — but he had the name. She had put it in his mouth when he was small and he had kept it there. The slavers had never got it. They took the teeth and could not take the thing hidden behind where the teeth had been.

*Rook.*

He held it behind his teeth, in the soft gap where they were gone, and he did not say it. Saying it would be giving it to the air, and the air here was thick and hungry and gave nothing back. He only held it. It was small and hard and whole when nothing else of his was whole. It was the one thing on this whole drowned green shore that was wholly his, that he had carried off that ship in the only place they had never thought to look — under the tongue, behind the gap, in the dark of his own mouth.

The slavers had their own sound for him. Low, made behind the teeth, ugly, a sound that was not a word so much as the felt-shape of a hand closing on the back of his neck. It had meant *property*. It had meant *come here*. It had meant *shut your mouth*. They had put that sound on him a thousand times and he had never once answered it as a name, only as a man answers the lash, with the back and not the name. That sound was not his and it was not him. He had left it in the sea.

But there had been one — the one with the cracked nail, the one who had fed him when the others died. That one had used the sound differently, sometimes, in the dark, when no one was watching. Had said it lower, without the hand on the neck, almost the way you would say a thing to a dog you had decided to keep. Not kindness. Something thinner than kindness, something that lived in the space between cruelty and habit — the sound a man makes when he has owned a thing long enough to stop thinking of it as a thing, without ever once thinking of it as a person. That was the worst sound of all, because it had made the boy think, for one moment in the dark, that he might be something other than what they said he was, and the thinking had been wrong, and the wrongness of it had settled into him like a bone set crooked, and it had healed there, and it was part of him now, and he could not straighten it any more than he could straighten the root that had grown crooked under the stone.

The name his mother had given him was the other thing, the opposite thing — not enough to live on, not enough to fill the low hunger, and still something. In all this green it was the one something.

---

Night came, and the green that drips and breathes all day turned into something that hunts.

He did not see the dark come down so much as hear the place change its voice. The drip went on, but under it a new sound started up, and another, until the whole black green was shrilling and sawing and clicking, a chorus of small throats, and over it the wet boom of larger things calling across the water, and under all of it the rising hum of a hundred thousand wings, the biting things lifting off the swamp to find the warm of him. They settled on the wet of his arms, his neck, the corners of his eyes, soft and silent and stinging, faster than he could brush them off. He could not stay in the open. It would bleed him dry by morning, or worse — he had seen what the bite of the small things did in a fever-place, the sweat and the shaking. Aldic again, in his memory, the man's skin gone to welts and then to worse, the fever and the bites together taking him apart in layers. The body knew it, and pulled him up off the warm ground and set him looking, low along the rock and root, the way a hurt thing looks for a hole.

He found a crack in the rock where the ridge breaks. Not a cave — a split, a gap he could pull into sideways, stone at his chest and stone at his back, tight as the cage and then tighter. He did not like the tight. The body remembered the tight — the cage had been tight, and the space under the gunwale where they had put him when the sea rose, tight and dark and full of the tar-stink and the other bodies pressed close, the living and the dead all breathing the same air until you could not tell by the sound which was which. But fewer of the biting things could reach him in here, and the wanting-to-live was stronger than the not-liking, and he folded himself in. Knees to his chest. Arms around the knees. Face to the stone. And the smell of the place closed over him — the den-stink of standing water and old rot, a hollow where the green had died and gone to a black soup in the dark, thick enough that a man could choke on it sleeping.

He should not sleep here.

He knew it before the head could catch up. This was fever-air, the very breath that lays men down. To curl into a rot-hollow and breathe its soup the night long was to wake shaking, or not to wake. The body should fight him, should drag him back to the bites rather than let him down into this. It did not. He breathed the foul thick dark and his head stayed clear in it. His gut did not turn. The crawling sweat of the sickness did not start.

Why. He wanted to know why. The question came up sharp out of the dark and there was nothing to answer it with — no words for the thing, no one to ask, only the foul air going in clean and the small still thing in him listening where the fever ought to be. The small still thing. It had been there since the shore, since the first breath of the rot-air that should have dropped him, a thing under the hurt and the hunger that did not move and did not speak and did not explain itself. It listened. He did not know what it listened to, or what it was, and the not-knowing was a splinter of a different kind — not the splinter from the ship, not the one that came with the shame, but something new, something the shore had put in him, and he could not tell yet whether it was the thing that would keep him alive or the thing that would eat him from the inside while the green ate him from the out.

He pressed into the warm rot-dark and let it hold him, the only thing that had held him since the hands let go. He kept his mother's name behind his teeth. He slept.

---

He dreamed in shards.

Hands. Big hands, rough, the skin like the rope, dragging him by the arm so the shoulder screamed. A floor that moved — wet wood under him, the reek of tar and too many bodies, the world always tilting, so that even now in the dream he could not find which way was down. Bars of rope and bone, a cage that breathed when the ship breathed. A voice low above him in no words he owned, and the voice made the sound, the ugly behind-the-teeth sound, and the sound meant *down* and meant *still* and meant *you are a thing, and the thing may yet be thrown back to the water.*

Then the other voice — the one that came at night, when the others were sleeping or dead. The cracked-nail hand putting something into the cage. A piece of hard bread, a corner of dried fish. The hand pulling back. No words at all. And the boy eating, and the taste of the bread meaning nothing and meaning everything, because the hand that gave it was the hand that hit, and the bread was not kindness, and it was not cruelty, and the thing it was had no name in any language the boy owned, and he ate it in the dark with his back to the bars and his eyes on nothing, and the taste of that bread was the taste of every complicated thing the world had ever done to him, and he could not sort it into the clean piles of good and bad that the body wanted, and so he swallowed it whole the way he had swallowed the bread, and it sat in him undigested, and it would sit in him still.

The floor tilting past all tilting. A sound of wood giving. Water coming in warm and black across the moving floor, climbing, and the bodies in the dark all moving at once. The hands above him. The hands letting go.

He woke.

---

The light had come up dim and green and low, a drowned light filtered down through the leaves so it never quite reached white. The night-chorus had worn down to a few last sawing throats, and the drip went on, and somewhere out over the water a single great voice boomed once and was quiet. He pulled himself out of the crack and everything in him complained at once, the joints, the back, the gaps in his jaw, a body's worth of small voices all saying the same thing. He unfolded slow.

He looked at his leg for the first time in the light. A gash down the outside of the thigh, long as his hand, crusted thick with dried blood and mud, the lips of it dark, the skin around it red and tight and hot to the touch — a small fire lit under the skin. He had seen wounds go this way. He had seen what came after, in the cage, in a wet hot hold — the red climbing, the smell turning, the leg going to green rot and then the body it was on going quiet and gone. A man called Pol who had been cut loading cargo, a clean cut that should have healed in a week, and three days later the leg had gone the color of a plum and the smell of it had filled the hold until even the slavers had gagged, and Pol had lain in his own heat and looked at the ceiling and talked to people who were not there, and on the fifth day he had stopped talking, and on the sixth he had stopped everything. In a place like this, wet and warm and full of small mouths, a wound like this was a death that had only not yet finished arriving. He pressed the skin beside it and it gave back pain, clean and deep. He let it be. There was nothing else to do with it. The body would do what the body would do.

He drank at the seep until the cup was dry and dry again. Then he followed the ridge up where it let him, slow, a hand on the stone, climbing out of the drowned green toward the white glare, and the trees thinned, and the ground fell away below him, and he saw the rest of it.

Green. Brown water. Green again. And no end to it. Hill on hill of green going up into haze, valleys drowned and steaming, a far line where the green met a white sky and he could not tell if there was sea beyond it or only more green. No smoke standing off a far hill. No boat dark on the water. No wall, no roof, no field, no smudge of anything made by hands. No other breathing thing that walked on two legs anywhere in all that drowning green, as far as the eye could throw itself before the world went to haze.

He stood where the rock broke out of the green, and the heat leaned its whole wet weight on him, patient, like it had all the time there is, and at last he understood the thing the body had been trying to tell him since the heat woke him on the first morning.

This was a different alone.

The cage had been alone — but the cage had walls. It had other bodies close in the dark, breathing, warm. It had the knowledge that hands would come. To hurt — but to come. To take notice. To know he was there. There was a kind of company even in the hands that hurt a body; they had at least learned the shape of the body, the weight of it, how it moved when it was hit and how it stopped moving when it had had enough. They had learned him the way a man learns a tool — not with love, not with care, but with a knowledge that was its own kind of bond, the bond of the hand that holds. This had no walls. This was the whole green world laid out endless with one boy standing in it, and nothing in it that knew his one word, nothing that would answer it. The second mouth opened wider at the thought. The low hunger that no water reached, that no meat would reach. It wanted to be known. There was no one. It would not close.

He looked down at his own arms in the dim green light. Through the night the biting swarm should have feasted on him; he should be a map of welts, fevered, his eyes swelling shut. But the bites were few, and already fading, and where he would have been eaten alive the skin was near clean.

He did not understand it, and he tried to. He reached for it — the air that had gone in clean, the rot-hollow that had not sickened him, now this — three wrong things in two days, and he pushed to make them into one thing, into a why, the way you press scattered teeth-marks to read what bit you. They would not come together. There were no words under his tongue for what his own body was doing without asking him, and the more he reached the further the shape of it slid off into the haze, and he was left only with the small still thing in him, quiet, listening, where the answer should be. So he set it on a back shelf and did not turn it over — not because he did not want to, but because there was nothing yet to turn it over *with*, and the green was full of teeth, and a boy who stood still wondering was a boy the green ate.

Then the heat pressed harder and his leg was on fire and his mouth filled with the iron taste, and the moment let him go.

He turned back toward the crack, the stone, the thin thread of clean water. He walked careful. The rock was sharp and the green was full of teeth, and there was no one to carry him if it opened him. There was no one. The body learned the lesson and wrote it down where it wrote everything: *the shore gives once. Then it makes you go further.*

He was alive.

He did not yet know whether that was mercy, or only the long way around to dying. The hunger did not care which. It only opened, and waited, and went on being hungry in the dark behind the first.
