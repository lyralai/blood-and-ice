# Chapter 3: The First Fire

The dark came down like the tide going out, all at once and from every side, and the green came alive.

For days he had made his peace with the light. The sun was a killing thing here, a white weight on the back of the neck that did not lift, and the heat in the breathless under-green was a wet hand pressed over the mouth — but he had learned its law. He kept to the shade in the worst of it. He drank before he thirsted. The day he could carry.

The night he could not carry.

The night the green woke up. When the white weight lifted, the rot-warm dark filled with the things that owned this place, and they came for the live heat of him. Cords of ants followed a smell of blood through the black. Soft wet things dropped from the leaves and felt for a place to bite. And the fever-air rose thick off the swamp-edge, a breath out of the drowned green that hung low and warm and rotten — a man breathed it and the shaking-sickness got into him. It poured up out of the ground at night, and against it the law of the shade and the law of the water sat in his chest light and useless as ash.

The first nights he had lain in the den-crack and let the dark have him, and woken whole. No sickness in him from the fever-breath. No mark where the swarm should have fed. That was the thing he could not look at straight — and he tried to. He pressed at it in the dark. Why this body and not another. Why the rot went around him. But he had no words for the shape of it, and the not-knowing slid off and left him only the fact. He set it down. The small dark shelf of things this body did that the body never agreed to, and he did not turn it over.

But not-being-touched was not the same as not being afraid. The dark held teeth he could not read. Things moved in it that he had only heard — a heavy something far off in the high green, a wet sliding at the channel-edge, a cough that was not a bird. Night was when the green hunted. He lay in his crack in the black with his eyes open and his heart slamming, holding it off with nothing but listening. A thing that held the dark off with only listening did not sleep. It waited.

He needed a thing that pushed the dark back. A thing the green was afraid of.

---

He had seen fire.

In the cage there was fire — far off, up where the men were, a glow at the top of the ladder-hole and a smell coming down through the dark. The smell he remembered better than the light. Old smoke, and meat cooking on the smoke, and under it the men's warmth and the men's voices, all of it up there, none of it down where the boy was. The boy got the black. They got the fire. That was the shape of the whole world as he had first learned it: fire was a thing other people had, behind the hands, and you smelled it and you did not get it.

But he had watched. A boy in a cage watched everything, because watching was the only thing the hands could not take. He watched the fire for the law under it. He saw a man strike a stone on a steel and the spark jump. He saw him feed the small light dry stuff first, threads and dust, and only then the bigger wood. And he saw that if he fed it big too soon the small light drowned and went out, and he had to start again and curse. There was a law in it. There was a law in everything. He had not known he was keeping the fire-law against a day he would need it. He kept it anyway. He kept everything.

So now he went looking for what fire ate.

---

The green gave him wood the same as it gave him everything: drowned, and crawling, and not where he first looked for it.

There was no want of wood here. That was not the trouble. The trouble was that every stick on the floor of this place was wet to the heart, soft with rot, ringed with the white threads of the thing that ate wood. He laid his hand on a fallen limb thick as his leg and it gave like rotten meat. A cord of pale ants poured up over his wrist. He threw it down. Wet wood did not take a spark — he knew this from the cage, from the man who cursed. And the wet was in everything here. The rain came most afternoons in a roar that turned the ground to soup, and a thing left out was green-furred by morning.

So he learned the next hard law. He did not look on the ground. He looked up.

There were dead limbs still hanging in the high green, snags caught in the crooks of the living trees, lifted off the rot-floor and turned in the wind and the sun, dry to the heart where the ground-wood never was. He climbed for them. He learned to read a snag for the white-thread rot before he trusted his weight to it, and twice he read wrong and came down hard in the leaf-wet and lay there with the breath knocked out, listening for what the noise had woken. The second fall opened the gash on his palm again — the one from the glassy stone, the one that had closed too fast. It opened and it bled, and he lay in the wet leaves and held his breath and watched it, and the blood came slow and dark and then slower, and before he had taken three breaths the lips of the cut were drawing shut again, the skin knitting closed the way a mouth closes around a word it has decided not to say. He watched it close. The wrongness of it sat in his stomach like a stone. A thing that mends when it should break is a thing that does not follow the law of things that break, and he had lived by that law his whole life — in the cage, where a cut was a countdown, and the countdown was measured in days or in death. His body was not counting. His body had thrown the clock out and kept going, and the not-counting frightened him in a place the dark could not reach, because a body that did not break by the law of breaking was a body he did not know the rules of, and a thing you did not know the rules of was a thing that could turn on you.

He tore the high wood loose and carried it back armful by armful to the lip of his den-crack, out of reach of the next rain. He split the pieces and found them dry inside — the first dry thing he had held since the sea.

He gathered more than he needed. He gathered against the rain like he gathered against the hunger, both mouths open the whole while.

It cost him two days. Two days of work eaten, climbing and dropping and splitting and stacking, the sweat running off him faster than he could drink it back. The tide-food he had been counting on went un-gathered. When he crawled down to the low water on the third morning the best mud was bare and picked clean — the birds had been at it, and the crabs, and what was left was too small or too deep or the clear-glass fish he could not eat. He came back with a single brown frog and a handful of the soft fruit, and his hands were shaking, and the shaking was not from the work. The shaking was from two days of a body spending what it did not have, and the near mouth opening wider with every hour he gave to the wood instead of to the food. That was the cost of the fire before he had even lit it. The green did not give him a thing for free. It made him choose — the dark or the hunger — and he chose the dark, and spent two days of eating against a hope that he might push the dark back, and the spending left a mark on him, a hollow under the ribs that said: *this had better work.*

There was a fly-cloud over the rot-limb at his feet thick as smoke. There was a clean hand's-width of air around his own skin where they did not come. He crouched there in the middle of the work and looked hard at the bare ring, and the wanting-to-know went nowhere — there was no thread in him to pull, no name for what held them off, only the wood waiting and the light going. He had wood to gather. He gathered it.

---

The spark was the next law, and it was the law that broke him.

He had no steel. The man in the cage had struck stone on steel and the spark had jumped. He had stone — the ridge above the swamp was nothing but stone — but no steel, no scrap of worked metal anywhere in all this drowning place. He did what the body did when it had no answer. He tried everything. He struck stone on stone, hard, again and again, until his hands ached and the gashes that had closed too fast threatened to open. He got nothing. A dull click. A smell of struck rock, flat and mineral. No spark. Not one.

He tried faster. He found a stone with a glassy shine to it, dark and sharp where it had broken, and struck that, sure this time, and it only chipped, and a flake of it bit into his palm. He bled. The blood welled up warm and red and bright in the green dark, the one clean color in all the rot, and he watched the small cut begin already to draw its edges shut faster than a cut should. He pressed his thumb to it and watched it close and he leaned toward the wrongness of it the way a tongue leans toward the gap where a tooth was — and it told him nothing. Even his own blood mocked him. He had heat in him. He could not get it out where the wood was.

He worked until the light went under the canopy, early, the green swallowing the sun long before the sky was done with it. He worked the next day. The dry wood lay stacked and ready and he could not light it, and the not-being-able was a new kind of hunger, worse than the belly had ever been. The belly he had learned to feed. This he could not. This he did everything right for and it gave him nothing — a flat click and a smell of rock and the dark coming down thick with its teeth.

He wanted to break the wood. He wanted to throw it in the swamp. The wanting came up in him hot and stupid, the cage-rage, the rage of a thing that had done everything asked of it and still got the dark. Still got the back of the hand. He held it behind his teeth, in the gap, because he had learned young that rage spent out loud only gave the hands more to take. He swallowed it. He crouched over the useless wood and made himself go quiet and watch. Watching was the thing that had never once failed him when everything else had.

But the rage did not go down easy. It sat in his chest and would not be swallowed, and in the dark of the second night without fire, without food enough, with the hollow under his ribs growing and the fly-cloud humming and the fever-air rolling over him thick as a blanket — in that dark the rage found a new voice. Not the cage-rage. Something under it. A heat that was not the air's heat and not the rage's heat, a heat that lived where the small still listening thing lived, in the deep place under the ribs where the far hunger sat. It woke and it leaned toward his anger the way the far hunger leaned toward the fire he could not make, and for one moment the two of them — the rage and the thing under the rage — were the same thing, the same heat, and he felt them reach for the wood together, and the wood was only wood, and it did not light, and the moment passed, and he was alone in the dark with his own shaking and the sound of the green.

He did not sleep. He lay in the crack and pressed his back to the stone and made himself breathe, and the heat under his ribs sank back down into whatever dark it lived in, and he did not follow it down. But he knew, the way the body knows a thing before the head will hold it, that the heat that lived under the rage and the thing that kept the fever off and the cuts that closed too fast — they were all one thing. They were all the same wrong thing. And the wrong thing had wanted the fire too.

He set it on the shelf. He set it down hard, the way you set down a thing you are afraid of dropping, and he did not turn it over.

---

The friction taught him. The green did not teach it. The body did, out of the long memory of the cage.

He was turning a split stick in his hands, only turning it, the way the fingers worried at a thing when the head had given up — and the place where it rubbed the wood under it went warm. He felt it before he knew it. A small heat under the work, no bigger than the heat of his own palm. He went very still.

Wood on wood. Not stone on stone. The spark did not jump out of the rock here. The heat came up out of the wood itself, if you pushed it hard enough and long enough and never let it cool.

He remembered, far off and dim, a thing half-seen. A man on the deck once, before the dark of the hold, spinning a stick between his palms over a board, fast, his whole weight bent down into it, smoke rising thin off the place where they met. He had not known he had kept it. He kept everything. He found a flat soft piece of the dry wood and a harder stick. He cut a small pit in the flat piece with the bitten edge of his glassy stone, set the stick in the pit, and spun it down between his palms — the body remembering it without his asking.

It was the hardest work he had done since the sea.

His palms burned. The skin tore where it had only just closed and the blood made the stick slick and he wiped it and spun again. His arms shook — not a dark-shake, a work-shake. The muscle giving out, the breath sawing. The heat lay on him thick and wet and stole the strength out of the work. He had to stop, gasping, the green swimming muddy at the edges of his eyes. And then he started again. Because under his hands the wood was hot now. A smell was coming off it, faint and brown and good — old smoke, the smell from the top of the ladder, the smell of the warmth that was always other people's — and the smell was his this time, coming up out of his own hands, and he would spin until his arms came off before he let it cool.

He thought of the cracked-nail hand. Not the face — the face was gone, the sea had eaten it, the way it had eaten his mother's face and left only the warmth and the low sound. Just the hand. The hand putting something through the bars. Hard bread, or a corner of dried fish. The hand pulling back. He had hated that hand. He had eaten what it gave him. He was thinking of it now because the smell of the smoke was the smell of the cage at night, the smell of the world above the ladder, and with the smell came the old want — not for the bread, not for the fish, but for the thing the fire had meant up there. Warmth. Voices. The sound of men who did not live in the dark. The fire had been the line between the boy in the cage and the men above him, and he had smelled that line every night of his life in the cage, and now he was making the smell himself, out of dead wood and his own bleeding hands, and the making did not feel like the cage. The making felt like the thing the cage had taken from him — the thing that was on the other side of the ladder-hole, the warmth that belonged to people and not to property.

And under that thought, small and still and listening, the wrong thing leaned toward the heat in the wood with an interest that was not his. He felt it the way you feel a hand on your shoulder in the dark — there, and warm, and not yours. The thing liked the fire. The thing wanted the fire the way it wanted nothing else since the shore. He pushed that away too, but not before he noticed: the wrong thing had reached for the fire twice now. Once in the rage last night. Once now, in the work. Both times when it cost nothing — only leaning, only wanting, the heat of the thing moving toward the heat of the wood. It had not helped. It had not lit the fire for him. But it had leaned, and the leaning was new, a thread he could not pull without the whole wrong thing unraveling, so he did not pull it. He spun the stick.

---

It came on the third try of the day, when the light was going and the night was rising up out of the ground and he had nothing left.

A wisp of smoke, thin as a hair, lifting from the black pit. Then more. Then, when he tipped the board and breathed on the dark place — soft, soft, not too much — a thing caught in the dust he had ground there, a small live red eye, no bigger than a seed, glowing and fading as his breath found it.

He had made an ember.

He cupped his hands around it against the night air and tipped it into the nest he had ready — the finest shavings, the dust, the pale fiber he stripped and dried from a split vine. He breathed. The threads took, a small flame standing up gold in the green-black, and he fed it the way the law said. Threads first. Then shavings. Then the small split pieces, careful, careful, not too big, not too soon, or he would drown the small light and start again and curse.

He did not curse. He fed it right.

And then it was burning. A fire. His fire, standing up off the dark stone in the falling dark. Gold and orange and moving, its heat coming with the light. And the green pulled back. The biting clouds thinned where the smoke went. The wet things on the leaves did not drop. The dark that he had held off every night with only his eyes and his ears took one step back from the ring of the light, and inside that ring he was — not safe, there was no safe here — but held. The fire stood between him and the green and said *not here*, and the green, for the first time, listened.

He had never in his life had a thing stand between him and the dark.

---

He cooked a clear-glass fish from the channel — the kind that had knotted his belly raw. Held it to the flame on a green stick, the way he had seen meat held at the top of the ladder. The fat hissed and ran and the smell went up into his nose. When he tore into it it was hot. Hot all the way through. The flesh came apart soft and went down easy, the fire eating the wrongness out of the meat before the meat could come into him. The gap where the three teeth were gone held the warmth like a cup, and he held it there, that small heat against it, and did not swallow until it went.

The near mouth was quiet for the first time in days. The frog-and-fruit scraping of the last two days had kept the belly alive but not filled it, and the fish — his fish, from his channel, cooked on his fire — sat warm and real and whole. He had not known how hungry he was until the hunger stopped. The stopping was so sudden it made him dizzy. He sat back on his heels and the fire swam in his eyes and for a moment the whole green world was only gold and the smell of cooked fish and the heat on his face, and the moment was so good it frightened him, because a thing that good was a thing that could be taken, and he had learned what taking felt like, and the learning sat in him the way the fish sat in his belly — warm, heavy, and his, until it was not.

---

He sat by the fire a long time.

He fed it, and learned its law as he went — how it wanted air under it, how it sank and reddened and needed more before it died, how the high dead wood fed it clean. He was careful with the wood — it cost him two days, and the rain would take what he left out. But he let it burn brighter than he needed anyway, because of the thing it was doing to the other mouth.

The far hunger. Low under the first, the one no fish or crab or root had ever once dropped into. He felt it move.

Not fed. He would not lie to himself the way the cage had taught him. Nothing was fed; the fire was not a body and did not breathe and did not know the count of him. But it leaped and fell and changed. It answered his breath when he bent close. It did a thing back when he did a thing to it, and that was more than the green had ever given — the green only ate, only closed over, only took its dead back into itself and never once turned its face to him. So the far hunger, the one he had begun to believe nothing on this whole drowning island could so much as touch, leaned toward the fire. A starving man leans like that toward a smell that is not food but is at least the memory of food. He had leaned like that once, in the dark of the hold, toward the warmth of the body chained beside his — that never turned its face to him and was gone one morning, hauled up the ladder, and he never knew its name.

He had a name. He held it now, behind his teeth, in the gap where the three were gone — Rook, his mother's word, the one thing the slavers never found because they never thought to look under a child's tongue for the only thing he owned. He said it now in the firelight. Not out loud — never out loud, the green was listening — but behind the teeth, against the warm of the flame. *Rook. Rook.* His mother's voice in it somewhere far down, her hands in it. The word the last warm thing before the hold, before the hands, before all of this.

The fire did not answer to the name. Nothing here did. Not yet. But it leaped and fell and was not the green, and for the first time since the sea spat him up he was not alone with only the dark and the two hungers and an island that took and took and never asked him anything.

---

He lay back in its light, and the body thought the way a body thinks when it has won a thing it did not think it could win: *I made fire. Out of dead wood and my own hands. If I can make fire, maybe there is something else on this island I can make. Maybe the far hunger is not a thing I only carry. Maybe it is a thing I can someday feed.*

And under the warmth, as if the thought rang a bell low down in him, that hunger stirred. Not toward the fire now. Toward nothing he could see. It opened wider than the fire could fill and waited there, patient, the way the swamp waited at low water, and the warmth lay on top of it and did not reach the bottom.

He pulled his hands back from the flame and looked at them in the firelight. The torn palms. The glassy-stone cut already gone. The gash from the fall reopened and re-closed, a thin pink line where a wound should still be weeping. These hands had made fire. These hands had torn and bled and healed too fast and torn again, and the healing was not his doing and the tearing was, and both of them were true, and neither one was the whole truth of what his hands were. He set them in his lap, palms up, and the firelight sat in them the way water sits in a cup. He could not tell whether what he felt was pride or dread. He felt both, and let them sit together the way the warmth and the hunger sat together — one on top, one underneath, neither going anywhere.

---

In the deep of the night he woke once and the flame had sunk to coals, red eyes breathing in the black, and he fed it half-asleep, the body doing the work without him. And as he laid the wood he saw the other wrong thing, the crawling one, ringing him in the dark as it always did where he slept. A thing had died somewhere close in the wet, and the long cords of ants had found it, and the swarm covered it — a living crawling skin over the dead, the fly-cloud hanging thick and humming above it in the firelight. It came to the very edge of where he had slept. And it stopped. A clean ring of bare warm ground around his body where the swarm did not cross, where the live heat of him held them off like the fire held off the dark. Two rings around the little ground he had borrowed from the island for one night. The fire's ring and the body's ring, and he could not say which one was the stranger, because the fire was a law he had learned and the body was a law he had not, and the two of them sat side by side in the dark around him and held the green at bay, and he did not understand either one well enough to trust it.

He wanted, fierce and wordless, to know what he was that the rot would not have him. He reached for it. There was nothing to take hold of — the fever-air that did not sicken him, the bite that left no mark, the cuts that closed too soon, all of it facts and no shape, a question with no edges. He set it on the shelf with the rest, not because he did not want it but because there was nowhere yet in him to put it.

Not now. Now he had a fire. The near mouth was quiet, the dark was held off at the ring of light, and he was not all the way alone.

He sat close, fed, lit, the smoke keeping the night-cloud thin — and somewhere out past the ring of light, far off in the high black green, a sound came across the dark. Low. Long. Not the wind and not the water and not any beast he had learned. A made sound. A sound with a shape to it, the way a word has a shape before you know the word, and it went down past the near mouth and touched the deep one — touched the place where no sound had ever reached — and the far hunger, which had leaned toward the fire and was not filled, turned now in the dark toward the sound the way a head turns to a name.

He stopped breathing. He listened.

It came again.
