# Chapter 2: Hunger Law

He stayed in the den-crack three turnings.

Not by choice. The body would not move. The leg had swelled where the rock opened it, the skin around the cut drum-tight and shining, gone a color no leg should wear — a deep, taut red that throbbed with its own pulse, as if the wound had ambitions of its own, as if it meant to become a thing separate from the leg that carried it. He pressed it once, to know it. The heat pushed back against his hand like the flank of something living. He did not press it again.

Thirst he could answer. The seep was ten steps and a crawl, and the crawl he could pay. Food was another country, on the far side of a green he had no strength to cross. So he lay in the wet dark with his face near the wall of the crack and let the turnings go by him. The fever-thick air, the rot-stink off the standing water below the rock, the breath of the drowned green — it should have lain on his chest like a hand and stopped him. It went into him and did no harm, and he did not ask why. There was no asking left in him, only the slow accounting of what the body had and what it spent, and how the one number fell away from the other.

There had been a man in the cage beside him — not Aldic, another, one whose name he had never learned because the man had arrived empty. The slavers had put him in during a port call, and the man had been sick already, his skin the color of wet paper, and in the dark of the hold he had pressed himself against the bars the way Rook pressed himself against the stone now, seeking the only cool surface in a hot world, and the man had whispered. Not words. A sound — low, constant, the sound a body makes when it has decided to die and is negotiating the terms. The sound had gone on for two nights. On the third the man was quiet, and on the fourth he was cold, and the slavers had dragged him out by the ankle with no more care than a man gives to hauling a sack of wet grain from a flooded hold. Rook had watched through the bars. He had not felt anything. That was the cage's other lesson, the one after the pain: the body learned to stop spending on the dead. It was not cruelty. It was arithmetic. You could not afford the cost of feeling for a thing that was gone, because the cost would eat the strength you needed to stay alive, and then you were the cold thing being dragged out by the ankle, and there was no one left in the cage to stop spending on you.

He lay in the crack and did the arithmetic. The leg was bad. The food was gone. The water was clean but water was not enough. The fever-air that should have been killing him went into him like nothing. These were the facts, and facts were all the cage had taught him to trust.

---

The hunger started on the second turning, and it did not start in the belly. It started in the head. A fog. His thoughts were few to begin with — the hurt saw to that, and the cage had taught him young that thinking too much only gave the hands more to take — but the fog thinned what little there was to almost nothing. It came up soft, like vapor off the swamp at the low of the light, a thing with no edge to it that was suddenly everywhere. Each crawl to the seep was longer than the last. The rock blurred at its edges, and he stopped halfway and put his cheek to the stone and waited for the world to firm up before he trusted his hands to find the water.

By the third turning the fog was everything. That should have frightened him. Some old true part of him knew it should — the part that remembered the cage, that remembered the way a man's eyes went glassy when the hunger had eaten enough of the thinking, the way the body turned to a thing that only breathed because breathing was the last habit to go. He had seen it. He had watched the hunger take men past the point where they were men, watched them become breathing meat that did not answer to its name, and the slavers had let it run because feeding property was a cost and dead property was only a loss if you counted it, and they did not count what they did not value. The fog should have frightened him because the fog was the first stair on the going-down, and the going-down did not have a stair that came back.

But the fog ate fear along with the rest. It ate it the same as it ate the edges of the rock. And far down under the soft green of it, the small still part of him that listened knew that too, and pulled.

---

There were two hungers in him, and he was slow to learn they were not one.

The first was for meat. That one he knew. It sat where hunger was supposed to sit, low and plain, a wringing under the ribs, and it spoke the old language the body had always spoken, the language of *eat or end*. He had carried it his whole life. In the cage it was the weather. You did not fight it. You bent under it and waited for the slop that came or didn't, and when it didn't you ate the silence and the dark and tried not to eat your own tongue, and sometimes that worked and sometimes it didn't and the men who it didn't work on went quiet in a different way. Call it the near mouth.

The other had no belly to sit in. He went looking for it with the inside of himself, the way a tongue goes after the gap where a tooth was, and could not find where it lived. It did not live anywhere a hand could point to. It woke when there was nothing breathing near him. No voice. No body close enough in the dark that he could feel the life come off it. In the cage that hunger had always been full, and he had never known it for a hunger because he had never once felt its floor. There were always bodies. The breath of the others going up in the dark. There was even the cruelty, and the cruelty was a kind of feeding — the hands that hurt him at least knew the shape of him, at least kept the count of him. A thing that was hurt was a thing that was seen.

He had hated those hands. He had hated them with a completeness that was the only completeness the cage allowed. And yet — and this was the splinter, the old splinter, the one he could not dig out — the hatred had a floor, and under the floor was a thing he could not name, a thing that was not gratitude and was not love and was not the cold attachment of a dog kicked enough times to call the boot familiar. It was the simple fact of being known. The hands that hurt him had learned his body. They knew where the ribs were, how the shoulder sat, the sound he made when the pressure went past bearing. They had studied him the way a man studies a tool — not with love, not with care, but with a knowledge that was its own kind of bond, the bond of the hand that holds. And the loss of that bond, in the green, was a hunger he had not expected, a hunger that had no food in all this drowning world, and it sat under the first like a stone under water, heavier for being hidden.

Here there was no one to fill it. It opened low under the first and would not close. The near one he could maybe answer — there was meat in this green, if he could get to it. The far one was harder. Nothing in all this drowning green was what it ate.

He pulled himself out of the crack. One at a time. The near one first. The far one he carried, because there was no setting it down.

---

Things moved in the green. His eyes had taken them in across three turnings and filed them behind the fog, but now the hunger made him look. It did not tell him to hide; it told him to *hunt*, and the body woke to that older word and leaned toward it the way a dog leans toward a hand that might feed it, not trusting, not hoping, only turning to the old direction because turning was what the body did.

Below the rock the land fell away into standing water, black-green and warm as blood, hung over with vine and the great flat leaves that held the rain in cups. Things moved in it. The water shivered where it should have been still. Life crowded the low places like it crowded the cage. Too much of it, packed in tight, eating each other for want of anywhere else to go. The jungle did not starve. It ate itself, and called it living.

He took a fish with his hands. It cost an hour. He lay on the warm mud at the lip of a root-pool with his arm in the water to the elbow and went still. The hunger made him patient — a vicious, focused patience that was not calm but was the closest thing to calm his body knew, the patience of a thing that has no option and so turns the wanting into a kind of stillness. He waited until his arm had stopped being his, until it was only a thing the fish had forgotten to fear. When a pale shape drifted close — a clear fish, near no color at all, only a shadow and a moving — he drove his hand down and shut his fingers and held, though it thrashed and his whole grip begged to spring open and let the small wet life go.

He had it. He brought it up streaming and twisting and bit into it while it still kicked. The taste was good and wrong at once, slick and faintly green, the flesh catching in the gap where the teeth were torn out. He ate the whole of it. The bones that bent, the skin, the tail flicking his chin as the head went down. Before he had even swallowed he was reaching for another, the want gone wild now that it had been fed.

Then he was sick.

---

It came fast. His stomach knotted like a fist and he brought the fish back up onto the mud, mostly bile and half-chewed bone. His guts wrung out and then wrung again on empty, and he lay with his cheek in the warm mud and shook, and this shaking came from inside, from somewhere deep and wet and low, worse than any hurt that came from outside — because the body knew how to set itself against an outside thing, and there was no setting yourself against the body turned against the body. He had watched men die of this. Of the sickness that came from the inside, the gut-rot, the fever that climbed out of bad meat or bad water and turned a man into a shaking heap that could not keep down even the rain. In the cage he had listened to them go, and the sound they made was not screaming. It was smaller. A whimper, steady, the sound of a body that had lost the argument with itself and was reporting its loss to no one.

Then the heat came up in him. Not the air's heat — his own. It climbed out of the belly into the head and the fog went thick and red at its edges, and he knew this. There was a thing that lived in bad meat and bad water and turned on you, and he had seen it climb up out of a man's gut and into his eyes and shake him to pieces and lay him down quiet and not let him up. The fever. It took the small and the weak first, and he was small now and weak now, scraped to nothing, and he felt it lay hold of him with both hands.

*Ate the fish. Now the fever. Now dying.* That was the whole of the thought, round and plain. He held his mother's name behind his teeth while the heat shook him, because if this was the going then he would go with the one thing still where he put it, the one un-stolen thing, held under the tongue, never spoken. Not because it would save him. Because it was his, and a thing that is dying has a right to hold what is its own, even if the holding is the last thing it does.

He thought of the cracked-nail hand. Not the face — the face was gone, the sea had eaten it. Just the hand. The way it had put the bread into the cage, a corner of hard bread, and pulled back. That hand had been the closest thing to a kindness the cage had offered, and it had not been kindness, and the bread had not been a gift, and still he thought of it now, in the shaking, because the body reached for what it reached for and the reaching was not a thing a man could govern. The hand that hit and the hand that fed were the same hand. That was the truth the cage had given him, and he carried it the way he carried the name — behind the teeth, in the dark, where it sat and would not be put down.

The dark came up and took him before the fever could finish.

---

He woke.

That was the first wrong thing, and it was a long while before he had head enough to know it for wrong. The light had gone and come again — a whole turning passed while he lay in the mud at the pool's edge with the swamp breathing its rot over him. He was alive. The heat had gone out of him, faster than it came, like water out of a cupped hand, and it had left no mark but the weakness, and the weakness was only hunger in the fever's coat.

He took the count the way the body took it, not in the head first but in the gut and then the head. The belly was wrung out but quiet, the wrongness clean out of it. Whatever had climbed out of the fish into his blood and lit the fever was gone, and it had taken none of him with it.

That was not how the fever went. He had *seen* how the fever went. Aldic, who ate the fruit and died smiling. Pol, whose leg went the color of a plum. The man with no name who had whispered against the bars and gone cold on the fourth night. The fever did not come up in a night and burn out in a night and leave the body whole. It took a man down a stair at a time, and at the bottom was the going, and most who started down did not climb back. He had started down. He had felt his foot on the first stair. And here he was at the top again, hollow and shaking but whole, the green still breathing its poison over him and none of it left in him.

The small listening thing in him woke and pulled at the back of everything. *Why am I still here. Why did it let me up.* He reached for the why of it and his hands closed on fog — there was no head in him yet to hold a question that size, only the hollow and the shaking and the green pressing down. The why would have to wait on a body that could stand. Now he needed to eat, and he could not eat the thing that had put the fever in him, so he would learn what else this green held.

*The green poisons what is easy to catch. What it lets you take, it takes back another way.*

---

So he watched the birds.

There were birds in the green — bright, loud, quick, dropping out of the canopy to the water and picking and choosing. He was too weak to do much but watch, so he watched hard. He watched them the way he had once watched the hands — not for what they were, but for what they reached toward and what they left. The hands had taught him the language of intent. You did not listen to what a man said. You watched what his hands went for, because the hands went for what the man wanted before the man knew he wanted it. The birds were the same. Their small bright bodies told him what the green would give and what the green would take.

They tore at the soft fruit that hung low and split. They snapped up the bright frogs from the broad leaves. They would not touch the clear fish. Not one. They dropped beside one drifting plain in the shallows and looked at it with one flat eye and left it, every time, with a quickness that was not indifference but *decision* — the bird had weighed the fish and found it wanting, or found it dangerous, or found it not worth the risk, and the weighing was done without thought, the way the body does its weighing, in the blood and the instinct and the old knowledge of a thing that has lived long enough in a place to know what kills.

And they kept well clear of the deep still pools where the water sat black and gave off a rot-breath even his fog-dulled nose could find — off water that had forgotten how to move. Those pools the birds would not even fly over. They went around, adding the detour to their path as though the black water had a shape in the air as well as in the ground, a boundary the bird could feel without touching. He filed that. The green had laws, and the birds knew them, and the birds had lived longer than he had.

He did what the birds did.

The soft fruit was sweet going down and stayed down. The dull brown frogs stayed down too. The clear fish he left where they drifted. It was not enough — it was the difference between dying slow and dying slower. But they had not died of it, and he was not too proud to take his schooling from a bird. In the cage he had learned from worse teachers. When the birds moved, he moved. When they put their heads down, he ate what they ate. They did not know they taught him. If they knew, he thought they would stop. Nothing in this green gave anything up gladly, and he would not either, in their place.

---

The water taught him next, and the water was a harder master than the birds and a fairer one.

There was a rise and a fall in this drowned green, slow, under the day. The far edge of it ran down to where the swamp met the salt, and there the tide breathed in and out like the sea breathed — the only thing in all this world he had come knowing, the only rhythm the cage had not been able to take from him. In the cage there had been a rhythm too: the slavers' schedule, the clang of the bucket, the boot on the boards that meant *morning* and the boot that meant *night*, and the body had learned those rhythms the way it learned breathing, and when they stopped — when the ship broke and the cage opened and the green took him — the body had lost its clock and had not found a new one.

Now it had one. The tide. It was not the schedule of hands and boots. It was the schedule of the world itself, the great slow breathing of a thing too large to have an opinion about him, and the body settled into it the way a body settles into breathing — not because it chooses to but because the rhythm is there and the body is built for rhythm and will take what the world offers.

When the salt-water drew back, the low places off the channels were bared, and the things that lived there were caught in the mud until the water came again.

He learned the breath of it. Not by counting — the numbers were never his — but by the body, the only book he could read here. The look of the light laid on the water. The turn of the smell when the salt was about to change its mind. A falling-off in the steady drip of the green that meant the going-out had begun.

Low water was when he ate. He learned to be at the bared mud when it showed, hand already wet, ready. The rest of the time he banked what little strength he had in the crack. He had once seen a man in the cage — not Pol, not Aldic, another whose name he had not been given and had not taken because names were not given in the cage, they were taken — hoard a crust against a hunger he knew was coming. The man had wrapped the crust in a rag and hidden it inside the lining of his shirt and slept with his hand on it, and when the slop did not come for two days the man had eaten the crust in small bites, and the slavers had not found it, and the man had lived. He had learned the cost of being caught with nothing. So had Rook.

The best low places were the farthest out, the ones the water bared only on the lowest of the lows. Those held bigger things. Slow crabs, heavy and dark, that he took two and three of before they woke to him. And a fat tide-thing, a pale eel-bodied life that buried in the warm mud, with a smell to its flesh, a smell of *something* instead of nothing. He ate it braced for the knotting and the bile and the climbing heat, and the knotting did not come. It stayed down. It sat warm and real in the belly, and the meat-hunger gave one step backward — only one — but it was the first ground he had taken since the green woke him, and he took it and held it close, the way he held the name behind his teeth. Small and hard and his.

---

The leg got worse before it turned, and then it turned wrong.

He did not look at it for two turnings. There was a kind of not-looking that was its own knowing. The body kept the count even when the eyes refused, and the count said the leg was bad. But the food had put a little clear back in his head, so he looked. The cut had sealed, but the skin around it was swollen and tight, and at the lips of it the flesh had gone a thick wet yellow that did not belong to a body that meant to live. In this wet, in this heat, a cut like this should go to rot — fly-blown, the wrongness climbing the leg in red roads while the body it lived in went quiet and then gone. He had watched it happen from a cut no longer than this one. That was enough to act on.

He washed it at the seep. Clear water cupped in his hands and poured over the hot skin, again and again. Where it ran, the swelling pulled back a little, so he kept at it. He made the crawl a dozen times a turning, spending strength he could ill spend, because the leg was the kind of wrong that did not wait, and the other wrongs could.

On the second turning the cut was smaller. Not just closed — smaller. The lips of it had drawn in toward each other. The yellow was fading back to the plain red of healing flesh. The fire under the skin was burning down to nothing.

Too fast.

He went still over the leg, the small listening thing in him waking and pulling hard now, harder than he could hold off. He had seen wounds. On the ship, on the bodies packed against him in the breathing dark, he had watched cuts like this one take a moon and more — watched some never close at all, only widen and weep and stink until the body wrapped around them went still in the night and was gone. He knew the slow arithmetic of damage, not in words but in the long evidence of a body that had been hurt and had counted, over and over, how long the hurt was meant to last. There was a count for a cut like this, and the count was in moons, and the leg was not keeping it.

The leg was mending in turnings what should have eaten moons. And in this wet, in this rot, it should not have been mending at all. It should have been feeding the green.

He looked at his hands. The gashes the sharp rock had torn in his palms that first morning were gone — closed to clean lines, the kind a hand carries a week after the hurt, that had stopped being wounds and started being only marks. They were three turnings old. He turned his palms up in the green light, and the listening thing in him went very quiet.

He wanted to know. That was the new thing, and it surprised him. He lay over the mending leg and he *pushed* at it. The fever that had come to take him and gone out of him in a night and left no mark. The rot-air he slept in that dropped every other body and did nothing to his. And now the wounds that closed too soon in a wet that should make them fester. They were all of a kind. Facts about this body that the body had not agreed to. He turned them this way and that, looking for the seam where they fit together, and there was no seam he could find — no word for what made a boy mend like this, no thought big enough to hold it. He reached and reached and his hands came back empty every time.

There was a feeling under the not-knowing. Not fear — the fog had eaten the fear, or the fever had burned it out, or the cage had taught him to spend fear only on things he could fight, and this was not a thing he could fight. The feeling was closer to the thing he had felt in the cage when the cracked-nail hand put bread through the bars — the not-knowing of a thing that was both threat and gift, both harm and help, and no way to tell which, and no one to ask. The cage had been full of things like that. The green was full of them too. The difference was that in the cage the things came from hands, and here they came from inside him, from a place he could not see and could not reach and could not question, and the not-knowing was worse for being his own.

So he did what a boy in a cage learns to do with a thing he cannot use yet. He put it on a shelf at the back, behind the hurt and the hunger, where it would keep. Not because he did not want it. Because he could not carry it and eat at the same time, and eating came first.

Now he needed to eat.

---

The close pools ran thin. He had cleaned them out, and the nearest mud with anything left in it was far enough down the green that the round trip ate most of a turning.

So he widened. He watched the green like he had watched the birds — not for food now but for the law under it. He found the broad-leaf vines that bled a milk that itched the skin, and left them. He found, in a back channel where the mangrove roots made a cage of their own, more of the fat tide-eels, more meat on them than the crabs and no sickness after. Fruit and eels and crabs were the meat that kept. The clear fish and the still black pools were the meat that turned. He built the law of this green one stay-down and one sickness at a time, writing it in the only book that took ink here, which was the body.

And the green, for its part, watched him back. He felt it the way he had once felt the slavers' eyes — not a gaze, not a thing with a mind behind it, but a weight, a pressure, the sense of being measured by a thing too large and too old to care about the outcome. The green did not want him dead. The green did not want him alive. The green wanted, if a thing without wanting could be said to want, to go on being green, and he was in the way of that the way a stone was in the way of a river — the river did not hate the stone. The river simply went around it, and in time the stone was worn to nothing, and the river did not notice.

The hunger for meat did not leave. But it pulled back. It stopped being the thing that drove him and became a thing he carried — present, low, his, the body's rent paid out daily for the keeping of a life. He could stand under it now. He could think over the top of it.

The second mouth did not pull back. Not by a step. Not by a breath. He fed himself. He drank himself full. He washed his own wound, he learned the breath of the tide. None of it reached the far one. It sat open in the dark under everything, where no eel or crab or fruit would ever drop, waiting on a thing this green did not have to give.

He thought of the cage. Not with hatred — the hatred was there, it was always there, but it was not the thing that rose first anymore. What rose first was a thing the cage had given him that he had not known was a gift until the cage was gone: the sound of breathing in the dark that was not his own. The warmth of a body pressed against his in the hold, not out of love but out of the tightness of the space, the living and the dead all breathing the same air. He had hated every one of those bodies. He had wished them gone. And now they were gone, all of them, every one, and the green was full of life and none of it breathed near him in the dark, and the absence of that sound was louder than any sound the green could make.

He could feed himself now. He could hold the warm dark of the crack around him at night and sleep without the fog. By every measure the cage had taught him to count by, he was winning.

It was not enough. The belly he had fed. The far one only listened, and went on listening, for a breathing thing that was not there. And lying in the dark, full and whole and alone, he began to see the thing the cage had never let him learn: that a body could win every count there was and still go on dying — of the hunger that no green would ever feed, the one that lived where the hands had been, in the space they had left behind when they let go.
