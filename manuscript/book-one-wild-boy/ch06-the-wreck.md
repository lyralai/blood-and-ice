# Chapter 6: The Wreck

The flood-river carried the smell to him on the third low after the swine, and it was a smell he had a shape for, far down, in the part of him that came before the green.

He was working the high channel where the brown water ran fast off the broken stone, reading the bank with his soles for the soft prints the deer left, when the wind shifted and laid the smell across his face. Not the green-smell. Not rot-of-leaf, not rot-of-meat, not the mud-and-dung of the swine or the wrong old hunger of the thing that had read him through the wall. This was a dead smell with a hard edge the green did not make — tar, and salt-eaten wood, and iron gone to rust, and under all of it bone. The near hunger did not stir for it. The far hunger lifted its head.

He should have turned away. The body knew that much. Every law the green had taught him said a strange smell was a thing to circle wide and read from upwind before you put your skin near it. But the smell went down past the laws, down to the drowned-deep place, where the hands lived and the ship lived and the low ugly sound the men made behind their teeth lived, and it pulled. It was the smell of the world he had come out of before the sea spat him up onto the green. A human smell.

He followed it.

---

He followed it down the flood-river all that low and into the next, where the water spread and slowed and the mangrove took the banks again, and the smell thickened until he tasted the rust at the back of his throat.

The river bent and opened onto a wide brown reach. There, run up onto a bar of black mud and broken to its bones, was the thing the smell came from.

A ship.

He had no word in his mouth for it and he did not need one. The body knew it as it had known the hands. It was the shape that had carried him — long and curved and made of wood worked by hands, a hollow thing built to ride the water and hold a hold inside it, a thing with men in it. This one would never ride again. It lay canted on the bar with its spine snapped and its belly stove in, the planks sprung apart and gone green-black with the years of wet, furred with weed and barnacle to the waterline and bleached bone-pale above. Vines had come down off the mangrove and into the broken hull and made it part of the green. A tree grew up through the deck. The mast was down half in the water, and on the mast there was cloth, gone to rotted rags, hanging.

He stood in the shallows a long while and did not go up.

The far hunger leaned the whole of itself toward the broken ship, leaned the way it had leaned toward the held-breath dark where the largeness had stood and read him through the wall. *Men were here.* The body said it under the head. Men were here, in his green, men with hands and mouths and the sounds that made words, men who were his as the swine and the thing that walked above him were not his. Men of the kind that took him and the kind he came from — all the same kind, the kind that made ships and chains and the low sound behind the teeth. He wanted to go up into the broken ship and find them. He wanted it so hard the wanting frightened the body, because the wanting did not stop to ask what killed the ship. A thing that did not stop to ask got killed.

So he made it stop. He made it ask. He read the reach as he read everything.

And the reach was wrong. Not the held-breath wrong of the den, not the largeness this time. The other wrong. The wrong of a place where many things died at once and the green had not finished taking them.

He saw it once he let the eyes go slow. The bones.

---

They were everywhere on the bar, and he had not seen them because the green had half-made them part of itself, as it had half-made the ship.

Long bones in the mud, pale and washed. A cage of ribs sunk in the silt with a young tree standing up through it. A skull, mud to the eye-holes, the jaw fallen open and gone, that grinned up at the green gloom with the wide grin the dead all wore. Another. Another. They lay up and down the bar and up the bank into the mangrove roots, scattered and pulled apart by the swarms and the tide and the carrion-kin over the moons — no order in them, no two together, a whole — he had no word for it, the head reached and found only *many*. Many men. Men past his count, dead here on this bar and left to the green.

He went up onto the bar slow, the cage-patience over the wanting, the spear in both hands, and he read them as he read the swine-bones, with the body, crouching, his jaw near the dead jaws, asking.

These men had not died as the swine died. There was no wound in them he could read, no spear-bite, no tooth-mark in the bone but the small worryings the carrion-kin left after. The skulls were whole. The long bones were whole. They were not killed-bones, the bones of a thing run down and torn. They were the other kind. They were the bones of things that lay down where they fell and did not get up — the way the old swine-boar lay down in the wallow when the moons ate his strength and did not get up, and the swarms came, and the green took him slow.

Something had laid all these men down at once and put no mark on them. He wanted to know what. He crouched over the grinning skull with the wanting gone tight and small in him and he asked it, *what came for you*, and the skull did the thing the bones always did. It said nothing. It grinned up at the green dark and let the question fall through it into the black mud.

But he had read this green for a moon now. He knew its ways the swine had not taught him. He looked at the brown water standing in the low pockets of the bar — the still water with the green skin on it, the water that knotted the belly of every other thing that drank it and slid off him. He looked at the rot-haze hanging in the mangrove, the fever-air that lay over every drowned reach, thick and breathless and warm, the air he had slept in a moon of nights and woken whole. He looked at the swarms. The biting cloud was here too, thick over the bones and the brown pools, the ants in the eye-holes, the carrion-flies in a hum over the whole bar.

And the body understood it before the head. *The green killed them.* Not the tooth. Not the largeness in the dark. The green itself — the water, the air, the rot, the small biting things — the green that asked and asked had laid these men down where they stood and put no mark on them and taken them slow. They had no laws for it. They sickened, and they died.

He pressed at it. He wanted the next thing, the thing under the thing — the why of it, why them and not him, when their air was his air. The head reached for the gap and could not close it. There was no word in him for what would close it. He stood on the bar full of dead men, breathing the air that killed them, and the knowing hung just under the brown water and would not surface.

Then he found the small skull.

It was half-buried under a long bone that had fallen across it, and he would have missed it but the long bone shifted when his knee brushed it and the small round showed through the mud. Not a man's skull. A child's. The plates of the skull not yet fused, the face short, the jaw holding milk teeth — small, white, perfect. He lifted the long bone off it and the whole small head came up out of the mud and the green light fell on it and the body went somewhere else.

Not the drowned-deep place. The other place. The one before the hands, before the ship — the place where the name lived in a voice that was not low and not ugly, a voice that had said things that were not *come* and not *be silent.* The small skull lay in his hands and it weighed nothing, less than the shell he had pulled from the root, less than a stone, and the far hunger opened so wide it swallowed the wanting whole and kept opening. A child of his kind had been on this ship. A child had sickened with the men and lain down on the bar and the green had taken the child as it took the men and the swine and the crabs, and the child had not gotten up.

He held the small skull and the body asked it one thing, just the one — not *what came for you* but *why you and not me.* The same question. A different weight. He had been a child on a ship. He had been cargo, small and chained, and the sea had taken his ship and spat him up onto the green, and the green had not killed him. This child had stayed on the bar with the men and died with the men, and the skull was the size of his two hands closed together, and the milk teeth were whole, and the green had eaten everything else and left the teeth because the teeth were the last thing the green could not dissolve.

The wrong thing under his ribs did a thing it had not done before. It went cold. Not the cold of fear, not the cold of the cowering before the largeness. A still cold, a quiet cold, a cold that had nothing to do with the body. As if the thing under his ribs recognized the small skull in some way the boy could not follow, and had drawn back from the recognition, and gone cold. He put it on the shelf with everything else and the shelf was heavy and getting heavier and the small skull sat in his hands and said nothing, the way the bones always said nothing.

He set it down careful. He did not know why he was careful with a dead thing the green had finished with. The hands were careful with it.

---

He went up into the ship.

He should not have. The wanting took him up over the sprung planks and into the broken belly of it, and the body let the wanting lead this once, because the pull was so deep — the smell of the world before the green.

Inside it was dark and close and stinking of wet wood and old rust and the dead, and the tree that grew up through the deck let a green-stained light down onto the slime and the wrack. There were more bones in here, in the dark of the hold, and these lay in a kind of order — men who had crawled into the hollow of their broken ship to die together out of the rain, as he crawled into the den. Some of them lay close, as if the last warmth of the living had drawn them in. One had an arm across another's ribs, the fingers settled on the shoulder-bone in a way that was not the carrion-kin's doing. The deep part of him did not want to read that. It wanted only to be near them, the men of his kind, even dead, even bone. He stood in the stinking dark among them and felt the far hunger fed a little, just by the nearness — and then not fed, leaning still, because bones did not turn and see him.

There were things in the hold the green had not made.

He found them with his hands in the slime and the dark, reading by feel what the eye could not reach. Hard things. Worked things. Things with edges the green did not put on a thing — the straight edge, the true round, the flat plane, the edges that said *hands made me.* He pulled them out of the muck one by one and the body knew each one before the head, with a deep slow shock of knowing, because these were the things from before.

A pot of iron, gone to rust-lace, that crumbled when he lifted it.

A length of chain.

The chain stopped the body still. He held it up in the green-stained light and the body filled all at once with the drowned-deep thing — the hold, the dark, the weight on the wrist and the neck, the felt-shape of the hand closing, the low ugly sound behind the teeth that meant *come*, meant *be silent*, meant *you are a thing.* The brand. The chain was rust but the rust held the shape of the link and the link was the shape of the closing and the body knew the link before it knew anything else on this bar. He dropped it in the muck and stood over it shaking, the gap behind his teeth aching where the three were gone, and for a long breath he was not on the bar. He was in the hold of the other ship, small, made nothing, the hands closing.

Then the green-rot smell came back and the green light came back and he was here, on his green, and the chain was only rust in the mud.

But he could not leave it there. He stood over it and the body worked the thing through, the way it worked the reading of a print or a tide. *The chain was here.* Not his chain — not the chain that had held him. This chain was this ship's chain. These men had carried chains. The body turned it slow. Men who carried chains were men who put them on other men, and the men they chained were his own — the small and the taken. He looked at the bones lying in their order in the hold and the order was not the order of a crew. It was the order of a hold. Some of these bones had been the chainers. Some of them had been the chained. Two kinds, then, where the green had made them one — the hand and the wrist it held, both eaten, both grin-boned, both dead of the same water and the same air. The green had not read the difference. The green did not care about the chain. It killed because it killed, and it had killed the taker and the taken with the same slow breath, and the bones lay together in the dark holding each other and there was no telling which was which.

He did not know what to feel about that. The body did not know. The far hunger leaned toward the bones equally, the taker and the taken, because they were all his kind, and the kind had carried chains onto this green and the green had eaten chain and chainer and chained and left the bones in order in the dark. He wanted to feel the green had done something for him — killed the men who carried chains, settled some debt — and the wanting was a thing to be ashamed of, because the green had not killed for him. The green had killed because that was what the green did. It did not care about the chain. It did not care about the child on the bar. It killed, and the not-killing of him was the wrong thing, not the killing of them.

He did not touch the chain again. But the not-touching did not mean the not-carrying. He carried it now the way he carried the brand and the gap — a thing inside him that the green could not rot because it was already part of the bone. The chain changed the bones. He looked at them now and saw two kinds where before he had seen one — the kind that branded and the kind that wore the brand, his kind and not his kind, all of them dead, all of them grinning, and the far hunger reaching for both and neither at once.

---

He found the rest in a chest gone soft as wet bread, up under the canted deck where the wet came slower.

Skins. Worked thin, scraped pale, marked all over in dark lines and small shapes — rows of them, ordered, the way nothing in the green was ordered. Some of the skins were larger, rolled tight, and the marks on these ran different: lines that went straight and bent at clean angles, with small shapes set at the ends of them, as if something were being measured and followed. The head did not give him the word for it, but the body knew the shape of a thing that mapped — a thing men made to hold the world beyond the eye, to say *this is where we are* and *this is where we are going.* He could not read any of it. The marks made a buzzing low in the drowned-deep place, almost a shape, almost a sound, and then the buzzing faded and the marks were only marks, dark scratches on dead skin, and he could not make them speak. But he knew they were made to speak. He pressed at them. He dragged his thumb along a row of the small shapes and waited for it to break open, and it did not break open. There was a way of reading them that he had once and did not have now, and he could not reach back far enough to find it. He rolled the skins careful and set them by. The men were dead but the marks were not dead. The marks were a thing the men could do that the green could not do. He would keep them, and maybe the way of reading them would come back, the way he kept waiting for the name to come back.

A knife.

It was under the skins, wrapped in a rag gone to paste, and when he cleared the rag the iron of it was dark with rust but the rust had not eaten through, because it had been wrapped, because a man had wrapped it careful before he lay down to die. He worked the rust off the edge against a flat of wet plank, grinding as he had ground the spear-point, and under the rust the iron came up bare and hard and true. An edge no grinding of stone on wood would ever make. An edge that would go through hide and root and bone like the green going through a dead man. He had made a tooth out of hardwood and blood. This was a tooth the men made out of the dark iron, and it was past his tooth as the swine was past the crab. He held it in his torn hand and the hand knew it. The hand had held a thing like this before. The grip of it sat in the palm like a thing remembered, and he pushed after the memory, hard, after where the palm had learned it — and the push went down into the drowned place and found only brown water, and no when, and no whose.

He kept the knife. The green had not given it. The men gave it, dying, as the swine gave the fat, and he took it, because the dead gave once and you carried off what you could carry.

---

And then, last, under everything, in the soft-rotted bottom of the chest, his fingers found the thing that stopped the whole of him.

It was small and hard and heavy in a way the iron was not, a worked thing, round, a disc of dark metal on a perished cord, and when he lifted it into the green light from the tree and rubbed the muck off it with his thumb the body did not file it. The body came up out of itself.

There was a mark on the disc. Raised, worked into the metal by hands, the true round and the true line, a shape made to mean. An eye. An open eye, ringed, looking. And through the eye, crossing it, driven into it, a thorn — a long curved barb, a thing made to pierce, laid through the looking eye so the eye looked out around the thing that pierced it.

He had seen this.

The knowing went through him the way the wrong-heat had gone through him on the day the seam closed in his palm, a long wave with no bottom. He had seen this mark. Not on the green — there was no this on the green, no eye, no thorn-shape worked in metal. He had seen it *before*, in the drowned-deep place, before the ship and the hands, before the sea, in the part of him the slavers could not reach and the green could not starve, the part where the name lived before it banked too low. The eye and the thorn. The body knew it the way it had known the chain and the knife and the smell of men, only deeper, from further down, from under the floor of everything he could reach.

He crouched in the broken belly of the dead ship with the disc in his torn hand and he reached down after the knowing, hard, the way he reached behind his teeth after the name, and it was the same — there was the shape, there was the certainty, *I know this, this is mine* — and there was no word in it, no when, no whose, no how. Only the eye, looking. Only the thorn, through it. Only the buzzing low in the drowned place, a thing trying to surface and never breaking the brown water.

He willed it to surface. He held the disc up to his eye in the green-stained dark and he stared into the worked eye and he asked it everything the body was screaming to ask — *whose are you, where did I hold you, what am I that I carry your mark under the floor of me* — and the disc gave him nothing back. It only looked. The thorn only pierced. The meaning was in there, he knew it was in there, and he had no way down to it.

And then the other knowing came, the cold one, the one the small skull had woken. The disc had been wrapped. A man had wrapped it careful and put it in a chest. The man had valued it. And the ship carried chains. The disc might be a thing the chainers had taken from the chained — taken the way the name had been taken from him, the way the teeth had been taken, a precious thing stolen and locked in a box. It might be his not because it was given but because it was taken. The wanting said *mine* and the cold said *maybe* and he could not tell which was true, because the dead held their secrets the way the brown water held its floor — still and dark and giving nothing back.

The far hunger stood all the way up. Wider than the men's bones had woken it. Wider than the smell. Because the bones were men of his kind and the marks were a thing men did, but this — this was particular, a thing that meant *him*, a thing from the inside of where he came from, and it was here, in his green, in his hand, and he could not read it any more than he could read the marks on the skins or say the name behind his teeth. It was one more thing that was his and gone. One more shape with the meaning starved out of it, and he wanted it back so hard the wanting shook the hand that held it.

He looped the perished cord around his neck where the chain had once sat, the worked disc against his chest over the breastbone, over the place the hunger lived, and the metal was the one part of him that was not the green's. The eye looked out from his chest now. The thorn went through it. He would carry it until the way down to it came back. It would come back. He told the body it would come back, and the body told him, from under the floor, that it did not know if it was telling the truth.

---

He went out of the dead ship in the failing green-dark, the knife at his hip, the rolled skins under his arm, the disc on his chest, and he stood on the bar among the bones of all the men the green had eaten, and the body understood the last thing, the thing under the thing, and it was so large he could not hold the whole of it at once.

The men had come onto his green. Men of his kind, with their ship and their iron and their marks and their brand and their chains and their eye-and-thorn. They had come onto the green he had made himself master of, and the green had done to them the one thing it had never done to him. It had laid them down where they stood and put no mark on them and taken them slow — with the water and the air and the rot and the small biting things, the water he drank and the air he slept in and the rot he denned in and the swarms that parted around his skin. Every one of them had died of the green he lived in. The child. The chainers. The chained. Every one.

And he was standing in the middle of their bones, breathing.

He did not have the head-shape for it yet. The head reached and found only the bones and the standing and the breathing, the men dead all around and the boy alive in the middle, and it could not close the gap between the two. The not-closing was its own kind of answer, the worst kind — the kind that said *you are not their kind, not the way the wanting needs to be.* Their kind died of the green. He did not. Their kind was his kind and the green did not care, and the not-dying made him something else, something the far hunger could not name and the head could not hold. The wanting reached for the bones and the bones said nothing, and the boy stood among them with the eye on his chest looking out at his dead.

He took the river back up toward the den in the dark, slow, the dead-iron tooth at his hip and the looking eye against his breastbone, and he leaned out behind his teeth into the rot-haze, out toward the before, toward anything at all that might turn and see him and make him a thing worth a sound.

And the bar behind him grinned up at the dark with all its many mouths and said nothing, the way the bones always said nothing, the way his own name said nothing now in the gap where the three teeth were torn.

But the wanting did not believe the nothing. It never had.

He carried the eye out of the place of dead men, and it looked out from his chest into his green, and he went on.
