# Prologue: Slack Water

The sea had lain like poured glass for three days, and on the third day the lookout raised a ship.

She came out of the haze bow-on, no wake, no answer to the glass, her sails hanging slack off the yards like wet washing. Coll watched her from the rail and felt the thing every sailor feels and never says: that a ship with no one at her wheel is not empty. She is occupied by whatever emptied her.

"Salvage," the mate said, the way another man might say grace. Vell had a nose for cargo the way a gull has a nose for guts, and he had pulled value from the sea for twenty years and never once been wrong about a hold. A ship adrift in the warm latitudes with her canvas still bent was a year's wage bobbing on the flat water, and Vell intended to have her before any other ship's master found the same current. He took the boat himself. He took Rusk the bosun, who was the kind of steady that other men leaned on without thinking — a big man, quiet, good in a crisis because the crisis never seemed to reach him the way it reached other men. And he took Tam, who had shipped four years with the mate and would follow him into a fire if Vell told him the fire was worth the walk. Because someone had to bail, and someone always made the boy do it, he took Coll.

They rowed across a sea so still it doubled everything — the dead ship and her dead reflection, joined at the waterline into one long wrong shape. The heat sat on the back of Coll's neck like a hand. Sweat ran into his eyes. He was sixteen and he had crossed this water twice and he had never once been cold on it.

He was cold before they reached the hull.

It came off her the way damp comes off a cellar — a breath of it, sliding out over the flat water to meet them, and the closer they pulled the deeper it went, until Coll's wet shirt turned to ice against his spine and his own breath began to smoke. *Smoke.* On the warm sea, in the dead calm, four men sat in a boat and watched their breath fog the air like it was deep winter, and no one said a word, because there was no word that did not sound like a child's.

"Tide's turned cold," Rusk said, which meant nothing, which was why he said it. He said it the way he said everything — level, undisturbed, as though the temperature of the air were a problem of rigging that would yield to a clear head and a steady hand. Coll wanted to believe him. He wanted it so badly that for a moment the cold was only cold, only weather, only a thing that happened, and the fear went back down where it lived.

Then they touched the hull, and the fear came back up, and this time it stayed.

They made the chains and went up. The deck was sound. That was the first wrong thing — Coll had boarded wrecks, and wrecks were chaos, lines parted, gear adrift, the story of the bad hour written in the mess. This ship was *neat*. The lines were coiled. A pannikin sat upright on a hatch cover with a skin of something frozen in the bottom of it. Everything in its place, and over all of it the cold, and the silence, and that smell — not rot. That was the second wrong thing, and it took Coll a long moment to understand it, standing on a dead ship in the killing heat of the southern sea: there was no rot. In this warmth a corpse went green and swollen by the second day. There was no green here. There was no smell of death at all. There was only the dry, clean, cold smell of a thing that had been kept.

Vell moved through the ship the way he moved through every hull — with authority, with the eye that measured, the hand that touched a bulkhead and knew the timber. "She's a trader," he said, running his palm along a rail. "Southern registry. See the work on the scuppers — Quarathi yard." He said it the way a man says the name of an old friend. He knew ships the way some men know horses, by the shape of the bone under the muscle. This one he read in a glance, and what he read was sound, well-found, a good ship gone wrong somewhere, and the wrongness of her did not reach the part of him that measured value. He was already below the hatches in his head, counting.

They found the crew amidships, in the lee of the boat, where men huddle.

For one mercy Coll thought they were sleeping. Then Vell turned one over with his boot, and the man came up light — too light, light as a bundle of canes, light as the husk a locust leaves clinging to a wall — and his face was the face of a man, all the lines of him still there, the stubble, the burst vein in one eye, but the skin had gone to grey paper drawn tight over the bone, and the eye was a dry stone, and there was nothing under the skin. Nothing. Drawn out and gone. No wound on him. No blood on the deck, not a drop, not a smear, not a thread of it anywhere on that neat clean cold ship — and a sailor knows, a sailor has scrubbed enough of it, that you cannot empty a man and leave no blood.

Tam began, very quietly, to pray. He prayed the way he did everything — steady, certain, the words worn smooth by long use. His hand found the rail and held it. His eyes stayed on the husks. The prayer was for them and it was for himself. His own judgment was screaming, and four years of following Vell had taught him to trust the mate's judgment over it.

"There's a hold full of cargo under us," Vell said, but his voice had gone thin, and he was not looking at the hatch. He was looking at the husks. He could not stop looking at them. Twenty years of pulling value from the sea, and nothing had prepared him for the thing that had no price he could name. "There's a year under our feet." And Coll saw the mate's hand go out — not toward the hatch, not toward the cargo — toward the nearest husk, the way a man reaches for a thing to confirm it is real. Vell caught himself. Pulled the hand back. But Coll had seen it, and Vell had seen Coll see it, and for one half-second the mate's face was stripped bare of authority and craft-pride, and what was underneath was a man who needed to understand a thing before he could leave it.

It was Coll who heard it. The others were grown men full of their own fear, loud inside their own skulls, but Coll was sixteen and frightened small and quiet, and so it was Coll who heard the sound come up out of the open hatch behind the mate. Not a footstep. A draw. A long, slow, patient draw, the sound a man makes pulling at a pipe that has nearly gone out, the sound of something taking in — and with it, up through the hatch, came the cold in a wave, real cold, cold that bit, cold that made the frozen pannikin and the smoking breath seem like nothing, and Coll's heart slammed once and he felt it — felt the warmth go out of his own chest toward the dark of that hatch, *toward* it, the way water leans toward a drain.

"Off," he said. He could barely make the word. "Off, off, get *off* —"

The thing came up out of the hold the way oil comes up through water.

He would spend what was left of his life trying not to remember it, and remembering it. Pale. Dry. The shape of a man the way the husks were the shape of men — all the lines still there and nothing inside — but moving, unfolding, tall, its skin drawn grey and tight and its mouth not a mouth and its eyes two holes that the cold poured out of. It made no sound but the draw, that endless thirsty draw, and where it turned its face the air went to frost, and Rusk, who was nearest, simply stopped. Stood. His breath smoked twice and then did not smoke again, and the big steady man who had never been reached by crisis the way other men were reached — the man other men leaned on without thinking — went grey and light and dry standing up, his blood going out of him into the cold and the dark with no wound to mark the road it took, and he folded down onto the clean deck a husk among husks, neat, in his place. The crisis had reached him at last. It had simply taken all of him at once.

Tam went over the rail. Coll heard the splash and then nothing, no swimming, the glass sea taking him without a ripple, and that was a mercy too, maybe, the only one on offer — though Coll would wonder, years later, whether the sea had taken Tam at all, or whether Tam had seen something in the cold that made him choose the water, and whether that choice was a kind of surrender that the thing on the deck counted as its own.

Vell did not run. The greed held him one half-second too long — but it was not the greed for gold, Coll understood that now, would understand it for the rest of his life. It was the greed of a man who had never once in twenty years left a thing unexplained. The hold was there. The answer was there. If he could just get his eyes on it, his hands to it, the thing would have a name and a measure, and the fear would become a problem of cargo, and he would solve it the way he had always solved things. His eye flicked to the hatch. His hand went out again, the same reach, the need to *know*. And the cold took him where he stood with his hand still half-raised toward the answer he had spent his whole life believing was waiting under every hull.

Coll ran. He did not remember the rail or the rope or the water. He came to in the boat with no oar and no memory of casting off, one half-mile of flat glass between him and the neat cold ship, and he lay in the bilge and shook and watched his own breath, watching to see if it would stop smoking, certain it would stop, waiting through the long bright afternoon to go grey and light and dry.

It did not stop. The cold thinned. The killing heat came back over him like a blanket and he wept into the bilgewater for the gift of his own sweat.

When his own ship found him at dusk they asked him a hundred times what he had seen, and he told them, and they wrote *fever* in the book, because there is no other word a ship's book will hold, and they were a thousand leagues from any shore where men knew the older words. The master was a man called Harren who counted the cost of every deviation in stores and days, and the salvage rights of a ship adrift in the warm latitudes were worth the risk of a fever hold, but not the risk of whatever the boy described, which was not fever, which was not anything Harren had a column for in his ledger. He turned them south. He did not turn back for the drifting ship, and when Coll asked him why — the only time he found the courage to ask — Harren said, "Some cargo costs more than it pays, boy. I have a whole ship full of men who have families waiting in port, and I will not feed them to a thing I cannot name to save a hull I never owned." It was the closest thing to mercy Coll heard from any of them. He would learn, later, that Harren had taken the long way around to avoid the latitudes where the ship had been found, adding twelve days to the run, and that he had entered *fever* in his own private log alongside a second word, older, that Coll could not read and did not ask to.

By dawn she had slid back into the haze she came from, neat and silent and full of cargo and the kept, carried on a current no chart marked, going wherever such things go to wait out the long calm between one ship and the next.

Coll lived. He never again crossed warm water without watching his breath, and for the rest of his days warmth itself carried a quality it had not carried before — a borrowed thing, a coat taken off a hook that did not belong to him, that might be asked for back at any hour, by any hand, without warning. He left the sea within two years. He could not stop counting the warmth in a room, measuring it the way Vell had measured holds — checking it, weighing it, waiting for the moment it would thin and the cold would come in under the door. He married. He had children. He held them close and the warmth of them was the truest thing he ever touched, and it was never quite enough to stop the listening, the deep animal listening for the sound of a draw, a long slow patient pull, coming up through the floor of the world.

And once, years on and far away, an old woman who read the older words told him a thing he did not understand and did not forget: that the cold ones do not hunt the warm out of hunger, the way a shark hunts, mindless and clean. They hunt it out of *memory.* They were warm once, every one of them. They remember it. And there is no thirst in all the world like the thirst of a thing that remembers being alive.

He thought of Vell then — Vell's hand going out toward the husk, the need to touch it, to *know* it — and for the first time he understood that the mate's greed and the cold ones' thirst were not different hungers. They were the same hunger. The thing on the ship wanted back what it had been, the way Vell had wanted the answer that was under the hatch, the way Coll himself wanted the warmth to be his and not borrowed, and the wanting was the thing that killed you, because the wanting did not know how to stop, and the cold did not know how to stop. The only mercy in all the world was the small stupid stubborn fact of being alive and knowing it, and holding it while you could, against the draw.
