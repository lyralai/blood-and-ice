# Chapter 8: Taken In

The rot-thicket took everything that lay down in it. It took the dead men in the wreck, who lay down in the green and let it have them and never got up. It came for him the same, the same patient mouth opening slow.

It did not take him.

---

Six lows he counted the young one down to the channel and back, the swamp pulling off the same stones on the same string as the tide, the young one coming down to glean and climbing back to the high fire — and on the seventh the green decided to kill him. It decided to kill everything, sooner or later. He had learned that letting it decide was how he lived. The thing that fought the green died fighting. The thing that lay still under it sometimes woke whole. So he let it.

There was a still pool in the deep rot under the fallen giant. Scum-skinned, brown, the water that lay where the green had died and gone to soup, and it breathed its fever-air up slow and thick all night against his back where he curled into the seam worn to his shape. He had slept against worse. But this low he woke with the throat shut and the head gone to wet stone, the gut turning, the legs gone to rope — the shaking-sickness on him at last, the heat rising off his own skin, the bad air worked all the way in. The death that laid everything down in the green. It had come for him as it came for all the rest.

He had burned before. Once after the clear-glass fish. Once after a thing struck him in the dark and the leg went black and hot to the hip. Each time the green leaned in close and opened its mouth on him, and each time he woke whole — the black going out of the leg, the heat going out of the head, fast, faster than a thing should mend, faster than the dead men mended, who did not mend at all.

He had wanted to know why. He had lain in the dark and pressed at it like a print he could not read, turning the why over until the head went raw — *why me, why not them, what is in me that the green cannot eat* — and there was never a bottom to it, only the buzzing where the answer should be, and no word in him to lay against it. So he did the only thing the question let him do. He set it down. He lay still and he let the green try.

He drank from the rainwater caught in the great cupped leaf he kept wedged in the roots, and he waited for the wrongness in him to win the way it always won.

He did not know they were watching.

---

The young one found him on the second burning low.

He was out of the hollow by then. The fever drove him out — it always did, the body wanting open air and running water when the heat was on it — and he crawled down through his own green to the channel-edge where the swamp pulled back and bared its stones, to lie in the wet mud and drink and shake. Too sick to keep his shape. Too sick to be nothing. A man-shape laid plain on the bared stones in the first light, burning, and he did not care, because the green had decided, and a thing the green had decided did not waste its last strength hiding.

And the young one came down to glean, and there he was on its stones.

It stopped a long way off. He felt it stop the way he felt a herd turn — the green changing shape around a will in it. He lifted his head off the mud. Across the bared stones it stood, big-armed, young, the dark hide slick with the wet, and it looked at him with the small head tipped, and it did not come and it did not go. The lip wanted to peel off his teeth. The old low thing wanted to start in his chest — *hands, hold, the weight on the neck* — but the fever had burned it thin, and all he could do was lie in the mud and look back, two things on the same stones, neither one moving.

Then it made a sound at him.

Low. Up and down. No edges in it. The gentling sound — the one he had heard a big one lay on a small one, soft, when they scooped the small one up off the killing ground against the warm. It made that sound at him, at the man-shape burning in the mud, and then it turned and went quick and crooked back up the green toward the high stones, the small-one's gait, and he thought: *it has gone to bring the many.*

The fast part of him, what was left of it, said crawl. Said go to ground, go deep, die in the dark where the hands could not close on him. He got his knees under him. The green tipped and swung. He got one hand to a root and the root was far away at the end of a long burning arm, and he went down again in the mud, and the last clear thing he had was the thought the body had carried since the hold, flat and certain: *better the green than the hand. Better eaten than owned.*

Then the dark came down loud, and he was gone into the fever, down under the brown water of himself where the buzzing lived.

---

He woke against a warm that was not the rot.

This was the thing he could not make sit right, after, when he turned it over and over and could not wear it smooth. The rot was warm. The stone held his back warm. The coal in its fist of fiber breathed its warm. He knew every warm in his green, and he had made his peace long ago with each one being a dead warm, a thing-warm, a warm that did not know it held him.

This warm knew.

It moved. It breathed — a great slow tide of breath, in and out, under him and around him — and it had a smell, rank and close and living, matted hide and old meat and a deep musk under it, and there was a heartbeat in it, vast and slow, going against his ear where his ear was laid to it. He was held. He was folded in against a great living thing the way the small ones were folded in, scooped up off the killing ground, and the second mouth — the far one, the one no meat answered, the one that had leaned and leaned across all that green and found nothing to lean on —

it went still.

It had never gone still. Not once in all the dark on this island. It was the hole under the breastbone that the kill could not fill and the water could not fill, the wanting-to-be-known that ate him from the inside while the green ate him from the out. And here, with the great slow heart going through him and the great arm folded over him, it lay down in the dark under his ribs and was quiet, and the quiet was so strange and so deep that the body shook harder than the fever ever shook it, and water came up out of his eyes that was not sweat, and he had no word for the thing happening to him. He only had the shape of the hole, going full.

He should have run. Every fast part of him, when it climbed back up through the fever, said *hands,* said *hold,* said the many take the one and unmake it. The arm was huge. It could close. It could break him as he broke a thing's neck without thought. He was held as a thing was held that could not get away.

But it was not the closing. He knew the closing. He wore it on the back of the neck through a whole dark sea — the slow sure grip that owned the whole of him, and the ugly sound that came with it, the brand, the hold-word made behind the teeth that meant *mine, be still, come, you are a thing.* He had carried that hand his whole life. He knew its weight to the ounce.

This was not that hand.

This hand held the small one. Not to own. To keep. The arm was folded over him to keep the rot off, to keep the warm in, to keep the burning shape from crawling back to die in the bad water — and the great head bent down over him in the dark and made the sound, low, up and down, no edges, *the gentling sound,* over and over against the top of his head. And the body knew the difference before the head could find it. The body wore the other hand. The body knew this one was not it.

He did not run. He was too sick to run, and he did not want to run, and the not-wanting was a stranger wrongness than any he had ever set down, and he did not set this one anywhere. He lay in it. He burned against the warm, and the warm held, and somewhere in the burning he understood that he had been carried — up out of his green, up the bare rock, up to the high stones where the kept fire held. That he was in among the many now, the very thing he fled. And that he was not dead.

---

He learned her in pieces, the way the fever let him.

She was big even among the big. Heavy through the chest, long in the arm, the dark hide gone gray-silver at the muzzle and along the great ridged back, the gray of a thing that had carried many lows. She was past her strength, and she was not near the king; she sat to the side of the high stones with the still ones, the laid-out dead, and tended them. She was the one who kept. He would learn the word the tribe laid on her later, when the words came, when he stole them off the others' tongues one low at a time. But the first thing he had of her was the warm and the slow heart and the gentling sound, and the second thing he had was this:

she lost one.

He read it in pieces, over the slow lows of mending. On the cleanest high shelf, up where the sun struck first, among the squared and faced dead, there was a small one. Small as the ones that rode the backs were small. Laid out faced like the rest, with the same care all down its little length, and she went to it. She crouched by it and laid a hand on it and took the hand away and laid it back, the way he had seen a big one do across all that green before he had a word for any of it, and she made a sound over it that was not the gentling sound — lower, with a tear in it, a thing that came up out of the great chest and went nowhere, that the high stones swallowed.

A small one that did not ride her back anymore. A warm she had made and tended that went out.

And then she came back down off the high shelf to where they had laid him, in the warm dark under the lean of the great trees against the rock, and she folded the great arm over him and made the gentling sound, the keeping one, and he understood the thing the body understood before the head, as it always did. There was a place in her shaped to hold a small thing against the killing ground. The green took the thing that filled it. And out of all the green she could have leaned her want toward, she leaned it toward the burning man-shape on the channel-stones that the green tried to kill and could not.

The far mouth in him had a hole in the exact shape of a thing taken. So did she. They lay down in each other's hole, the two of them, and neither one of them had a word for it, and they did not need one.

But the tribe had eyes.

He felt them the way he felt the flies — not as a single thing but as a weight, a pressure that had a quality to it. The big ones moved around the keeper and the burning man-shape she had carried up, and the quality of their moving was not the gentling and not the killing. It was the drawn-back half-step of a thing that does not understand and is waiting to. Some of them came close and made low sounds at the keeper — not the gentling, not the calling, but sounds with a harder edge, sounds that went in a line between her and the man-shape and carried something in the going. Questioning. The big head would lift and the keeper would answer with the keeping sound, soft, falling, and the questioner would go, and another would come, and the keeper would answer, and the line of sound between them said a thing he did not have words for yet but the body read: *why this. Why this and not the small one we buried. Why the strange shape when the green took yours.*

And the keeper would answer the same way each time. The keeping sound. The arm tightening over him, not hard, only sure. *Mine. This is a kept thing. I keep it.* And they would go, and the question did not go with them. It sat on the shelf like a stone, and another was laid on it, and another, and the keeper held him against the weight of them with the great arm and the gentling and the slow heart, and the body understood that this warm was not free. It cost the keeper something to hold him. Each low she held him the debt grew — the debt of a place shaped for one thing filled with another — and the tribe watched the debt grow and did not stop it and did not like it, and the keeper held him anyway.

---

There was a thing they saw that he did not know they saw.

It came to him slow, over the mending lows, in how the others moved around him. The young one that found him. The big ones that passed and looked. They did not look at him as a thing looked at meat. They did not look as the many looked at the one before they swept it. They looked at a thing that should not be.

And on a high morning when the fever had gone out of him at last — gone fast, gone clean, the head come back to itself, the gut quiet, the legs under him again, days before a thing should mend and the dead men never mending at all — one of them came to him and crouched and did not touch him, and made a long sound at the others, and the heads came up, all of them, and tipped toward him. And it went down through him sudden and bright, what they witnessed.

They watched the green try to kill him. They carried up a burning man-shape laid out plain on the channel-stones, the shaking-sickness thick on it, the fever-air worked all the way in — the death that laid everything down, the death that put no mark on the dead men in the wreck — and they laid it in the warm to go out. The way the small one went out. The way they had watched it take their own, low after low, since before any of them was big. And it did not take him. He climbed back up out of the brown water of himself whole and fast, the heat going out of him and leaving no mark, while every other thing the green decided to kill stayed decided.

This was the wrong thing in him. The buzzing thing he could not read. He did it where the many could see.

And the many did not read it as he read it. He read it as one more wrong thing he could not get to the bottom of. They read it as a sign. A thing the green could not eat. The longest tooth on the whole low country, carried up burning to die, and not dying — and the heads tipped toward it, and the line bent around it, the small wrong way the line bent around a thing that meant something the tribe had no word for either. Only the hole where the word would go.

---

The king came down off his stone.

Not far. Two body-lengths down the bare rock, no more, but it was the first time he had come since the burning man-shape was carried up, and the shelf went quiet the way the green went quiet when the largeness moved under the canopy. Every head turned. The grooming stopped. The small ones were gathered in against the big ones, quick, the young one that had found him pulling its glean-brood tight to its chest. Even the keeper went still beside him, her great arm tightening over his body, not hard, only sure. He did not have the sound for the king yet — that would come later — but he had the shape of him sitting highest, heavy past all the others, the old silver come into the dark of his hide along the great ridged shoulders, and the eyes that went over him and did not warm.

Gull-Marr stopped two body-lengths from where the keeper lay and looked at the man-shape she held, and the look was flat and measuring, the look of a thing that reads a print in the mud and has not yet decided whether the print is meat or danger. He had looked at Rook once before, long, and looked away. He was looking now because the fever had broken and the man-shape had not died, and a thing that did not die when the green decided it should die was a thing that changed the shape of the shelf.

The king made a sound. Low and flat, careful, laid down with teeth in it. Not the gentling. *What is this. Why is this on my stone.* And the keeper answered, soft and long, the keeping in it. *Mine. This is a kept thing. I keep it.* The two sounds hung in the air between them, the king's flat demand and the keeper's soft hold, and between the two was the question of the whole shelf — who decided what lived on the high stones. The king or the keeper. The longest tooth or the one who tended the dead.

It was not settled. It was held the way a green holds a thing — still on the surface, moving underneath. The king did not unmake the keeper's claim. He did not affirm it. He looked from the man-shape to the mark at its chest — the disc, the eye-and-thorn, bound at the breastbone where the chain had once sat — and his flat eyes held on the mark a breath too long, and then he turned and went back up the bare rock to his stone, and the shelf breathed out.

But the body had read the king's back as he turned, the way it read the green, and what it read was this: the king had not decided. The king was waiting. A thing the king was waiting to decide was a thing that lived on the flat edge of a blade, and the keeper held the blade's near edge and the king held the far one, and the man-shape between them was the thing the blade would cut when the waiting ended.

He filed the king's danger where his bare stone was already filed, and let the warm of the keeper have him, because the warm was real and the blade was not yet falling. But the king had looked, and the looking would not be taken back.

---

The old one came last.

It was the oldest of them he had seen, grayer than the keeper, the great body sunk in on itself, the eyes gone milk-pale at the edges but still moving, still reading. It came to where he lay healing and it crouched a long while and it read him as he read the green — slow, taking the whole shape of a thing before it went down into it. And then it did a thing that turned the gut over hard.

It reached one slow gray finger and laid it, not on him, on the thing at his chest.

The disc. The hard worked thing from the wreck, the one with the mark on it — the open eye, ringed, looking, and through the eye, crossing it, driven into it, the long curved barb, the thorn laid through the looking so the eye looked out around the thing that pierced it. He had carried it bound at his chest since the broken belly of the dead ship, because the body would not set it down, because there was a buzzing low in the drowned place of him when he held it, a knowing with no word in it — *I know this, this is mine* — and no when, and no whose.

The old one laid its gray finger on the eye and the thorn, and it went very still. And then it made a sound — low, and long, and with a thing in it he had not heard from any of them, a thing like the tear over the dead small one but turned the other way, turned toward, turned up. It looked from the mark to his face and back to the mark, and it knew the mark. He saw it know the mark.

And he leaned in. He could not help it. The whole of him leaned toward the gray old thing the way a starving thing leaned toward smoke, because it had what he had wanted his whole dark life — it knew. It knew the mark. It carried the answer to the buzzing in its milk-pale eyes, and he was inside a single arm's reach of it, and he strained at it. He pushed the head down into the drowned place of him where the buzzing lifted and tried the brown water — *tell me, old one, what is the eye, whose is the thorn, what am I* — and the brown water did not break. The buzzing lifted and fell back. There were no words in him to ask with, and none in it to answer, and the knowing sat in the gray head a hand's breadth from his and would not cross. He was closer to it than he had ever been to his own name, and it might as well have been across the whole green sea.

The old one drew its finger back and bowed the great gray head, slow, the way the keeper bowed hers over the small one, and made the sound again, and the others drew back from him a half-step, all of them — drawing back from a thing they did not understand and now half-do.

And the quality of the drawing-back was not fear. Not the fear he had expected, the pack-flattening, the many closing on the wrong thing. It was closer to the way the green drew back from the old stones — a making-room, a recognition of a weight that had always been there and was only now surfacing. They drew back from the mark, not from the man-shape. The eye-and-thorn was a thing older than any of them on the high stones, older than the keeper's tending, older than the king's stone, and the old one had bowed to it, and the tribe read the bow and the bow changed the shape of the shelf the way the king's looking had changed it — silently, and in a way that would not be taken back.

He was left with the deep slow shock of *known.* The disc meant something here. To them. The eye-and-thorn meant a thing the old gray one carried in its blood the way he carried the empty gap behind his teeth, and it had read it on him, and it had bowed its head. And he could not get one finger's width closer to what it was. He lay down with the wanting still in him, hot and unanswered. He did not set it down. He had not finished reaching for it.

---

The keeper came for him at the dark.

The kept fire was up on the high stone, red and low and fed, the way it had been every dark he counted from across the green, the held light in all the black that a thing made and tended and did not let go out. And he was up among it now. He was inside the warm he had leaned toward across all that drowning green with nothing to lean on. The small ones ran between the legs of the big ones, and one of them — dark and quick, no fear in it at all — came and looked at him and went away again. The heads were tipped one way. The king sat high on the bare stone and had looked at him once, long, and looked away, and the old one had bowed, and the tribe had drawn back, and the questions sat on the shelf like stones that had not been there before the burning man-shape came up off the channel-stones, and the stones were not going anywhere. And he was inside it.

The keeper folded down beside him in the firelight, the great gray-silver bulk of her, and laid the arm over him, and made the gentling sound, the keeping one. And he lay against the warm that knew it held him, with the slow vast heart going through his ear, inside the high stones, inside the many, the very thing he had fled.

And he did the old thing. He could not help it. He went in behind the teeth, into the gap where three were torn out, the dark warm hollow where the name his mother had set on him used to live, before the ship, before the hands — to find a sound to set against this, to send back into the warm the one thing that meant *him.* Not the brand. Not the thing they made of him. The true thing, his — *this is who is held.* He went in to find it.

And the gap was empty, the way it was always empty. Warm, dark, shaped like nothing. The water lay down full in the print the name left and was nothing like the name.

But for the first time, behind the teeth, in the empty place, he did not stop. The keeper made her sound over him, low, up and down, no edges, and he took it in through the ear and down into the chest where the wanting lived, and he laid it against the gap where the name used to be — not the name, not his, but a sound, a true one, a sound that came down out of a warm that chose to hold him — and the gap took it. The way the rot took a thing laid down in it. The way the warm took him.

He had no name to send. But he had a sound to keep now, laid in the place the name was torn from, and a warm to keep it for. And the far mouth, full at last, did the only thing it had ever known how to do with a thing it could not hold.

It began to count the lights it had, instead of the lights between.
