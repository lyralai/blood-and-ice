# Book Three, Chapter 1: The Ledger

A moon of darks after the lanterns stood, the boy learned what a ledger weighed.

It weighed like stone. That was the body's finding, before any count dressed it: he carried the city as he had once carried the den-walls, on the back and in the arms, except the den had sat still when he set it down and the city did not. The count ran every dark without him now — down nine tiers and up again, fed, owed, witnessed — and still the darks brought him the carrying, because the count could tell a tier what it owed and could not tell a tier what to do when the owing could not be paid. That work came up the stair to the rim in the bodies of runners. It came in slates. It came in the set of Maren's shoulders at the gray light, and the boy had learned to read shoulders the way he read mud, whole shape first, and Maren's shape this moon said: less.

*Findings,* he entered, at the rim, in the flat voice, thumb moving on the slate as the palm had once moved on the warm stone. *The salt is short against the dark season. The root-cellars of the third tier will not reach the thaw. The flats-farms that fed the tiers with the rim-trade are giving less, because the two hundred kneel where the tide-farms were. Entered plain: the city is more mouths than the city is food, and the count, which is a law and not a field, cannot plant.*

He stood a while at the gray light with his hand on the stone where he stood every gray light, and made himself read the whole shape of the season coming. The water had gone the color of a knife's blade. The wind off it had the thin pre-taste of the cold country, and under the stone, in the deep places, the quiet held — the Cold in its books, the dock at the rim holding, two hundred breathing in the mud. Nothing coming. Nothing gone. The Cold did not come and go. It kept.

The beast hated the ledger.

That was the other finding of the moon, and the boy entered that one too, alone, where the tiers would not hear it: the old thing under the ribs, the one from before the count had a name, wanted nothing to do with slates. It understood the walls — held ground was ground, and the wall-law was an old law, and the body's own. It understood the row; it had stood rows since the fourth turning. But the salt was not a thing you could hold. The root-cellars were not enemies. There was no shape in a ledger the beast could put its back against, and so it paced, all dark, all darks, up and down the floor of him, wearing a track, and the pacing had a lean in it the boy knew from the cage-years: the lean toward the door out. Toward the simple country, where hunger had a throat and you could put a spear in the answer.

He priced the lean, and he entered the price. *The beast wants a smaller world. The boy needs the bigger one. Entered apart, because they share the arms.*

---

The watchman's name was Ennin, and on the eleventh dark of the moon he walked down into the mud.

Kade brought it up the stair again, and this time the man made the words sit, and the sitting was worse than the not-sitting had been. Plain words, counted right, in the watch's flat report-voice: Ennin of the rim-watch, second standing, a freed-man of the old arena-mark, owing nothing, owed nothing. Eleven darks on the flats-stones, looking down at faces that breathed. On the eleventh dark he had set his spear against the rim-stone. He had taken off his boots — the report held that detail, and the boy made it hold the detail, because the boots were the finding — and he had walked down past the rim-stones, barefoot in the cold mud, out to the rows. And he had knelt.

Not among them. Beside them. One pace off the end of the last row, in the spacing, in the neat hand's-width of nothing the Cold kept between its own.

The rows had made room.

"The watch stood the whole of it," Kade said, and the wrapped hands lay open at his sides, empty of anything to hold. "None of the husks moved. The kept don't move. He knelt, and he is kneeling, carrier, and his breath runs the long breath with theirs now, and there is no wound on him and no mark. We have watched him eleven darks and this dark he is one of the things we watch."

"Has the Cold taken him?" Maren asked, from the slates, and the question was the wrong question, and the boy let it stand in the room until everyone could see it was the wrong question, because that was faster than saying so.

"No wound," he said. "No mark. No taking. The Cold does not take." He had entered this before, and he entered it again, plain, carrying, so the floor and the tiers below the floor could hold it: "It keeps. Ennin kept himself. Eleven darks of looking at a thing that breathes and does not live and does not die, and somewhere in the eleven the looking turned, and he went down to the thing he had looked at. Entered: the dock does not need to reach into the city. The city looks at the dock. Looking is a road. The road runs both ways."

The council took that, each in their own body. Maren's chalk stopped. Lorren, who had the walls now, went to the window-gap and stood with his back to the room, which was a thing the boy's cage-years priced whole: a man shows his back when the front has too much on it. The under-priest sat with his ruined hands in his lap and said nothing, and his nothing had the old weight, the doctrine's weight — the tithe had said for nine hundred years that the Cold takes the unworthy, and here was the Cold taking no one, and the wrongness of that was still eating the man dark by dark, a hunger with no meat.

"Then don't watch them," Brannoc said, into the quiet. The blind face was turned to the boy, and the old fingers made the small motion he made when a thing in stone wanted reading twice. "Boy. You wrote me once that a mark kept is a mark meaning to be read. Your rows at the rim are read now. Every dark, the watch reads them. Ask yourself what a mark does when it is read long enough." The fingers stilled. "It teaches. You taught the Cold rows with your own standing. The dock is teaching back. Pull the watch off the flats."

"Pull the watch and the city says we hide it," Asha-kel said, flat, the caller's flat, and behind her flat the houses' arithmetic ran like water under ice. "Pull the watch and the story goes down the stair without a witness, and a story without a witness grows a head on every tier. The houses enter that the watch stands. The houses also enter—" and there it was, the pause, the price of the free information coming— "that the row Ennin knelt in is a row of your spacing, carrier. The mark on the flats is yours now, whichever way it was made. What kneels in your shape, the houses must read as yours."

There it was. Pride, fear, and arithmetic in one flat face, and none of the three false, and the boy made himself hold all three as he held the season. The houses were right. That was the worst of it, and the count's law was to say so: the dock was in his spacing, the spacing was his shape, and a shape a man makes does not stop being his when another hand fills it. Ennin kneeling was a mark made in Rook's hand with another man's knees.

*Findings,* he entered, aloud, for the floor. *The dock grows now from inside the walls. Ennin is the first and will not be the last; the looking is a road and the darks are long and the flats breathe. Entered plain: I cannot spear a road. I can only price what walks it.*

Then he gave the orders, and each order was a notch in the season, and the floor took them as law: the watch off the flats, but a standing of two — never one; one looks too long — two, turned facing the city, not the mud, with the law of the standing spoken down its length: *you watch the wall, the wall watches you.* Lorren to break the sightline from the tiers with slate-fences at the rim, ugly and plain, so the story down the stair found no faces to grow a head on. Asha-kel to carry to the houses the price of their reading: the houses stood the row at the next holding, and their row took the flats-watch with it — the first row of the houses would stand the rim and face the mud, in their wool, in their fear, entered, owing, counted. Let them read his mark from the mud-side, and let the reading be witnessed, and let the cost of the reading be paid by the readers.

Asha-kel took that back down the stair with no face at all, which was the houses' way of saying the price had been heard.

---

The Red called the price fair.

He went to the door that dark, because a moon had gone by and the door had stood quiet a moon, and quiet from the Red was a thing the boy counted as he counted the quiet under the stone. The warmth lay along the wood low and level, books balanced, and when it spoke it spoke in the ledger-voice, flat, warm, exact, and the flatness had a new stillness in it — the stillness of a thing that has finished a column and is waiting to be asked.

*You handled the houses,* it said. *I want that entered. Not as praise, boy. As a finding. Six darks ago you would have priced their claim as an attack and answered it with terms drawn hard enough to crack. Tonight you priced it as fear and sold them the fear back at a markup. That is my arithmetic. You are running it in your own hand.* The warmth moved along the door as a hand turns a page. *Enter the question that follows: if the count is my arithmetic wearing your voice — whose ledger is the city?*

The boy sat down across from the door on the cold stone, and the sitting was the body's own doing, the knees going before the head agreed, and he let the body have it. Behind the door the Red waited with the patience it had learned from him — the holding-patience, the fourth-turning patience, the patience of a kept thing that had learned kept things pay.

"Entered," the boy said. "The question stands in the book. It will stand a long time. Now answer mine. The flats. Two hundred and one. The Cold is building my shape at the rim and filling it with my city, one kneeling freed-man at a time, and the count cannot plant food and the dark season is on the water. What is it waiting for."

The door was quiet. And then the Red said a thing it had never said in six moons of doors, and it said it in the ledger-voice, and the ledger-voice was what made it terrible:

*It is waiting for you to run out.*

The warmth lay flat. *Run the column, boy. The city is more mouths than food. You know it; I know it; the Cold kept your rate of holding when it read you at the walls. It knows what you hold and how long you hold it. It built a dock at the rim of a city that cannot feed itself, and it set the dock in your spacing, and it is letting the dark season do the rowing. Every dark the stores go down, the looking goes on, and every dark the looking goes on, another Ennin prices the mud kinder than the thaw he won't see. The dock is not a wall against you. I said that. It is not even a trap.* The warmth came off the wood in a slow wave, level as a ledger-line. *It is a ledger. The Cold is entering costs against you, dark by dark, in a column made of kneeling people, and it does not need to win. It needs you to spend. Every drop you burn, you go down your own ladder, and every dark you don't, the city goes down its. It has found the one war where your blood and your count want the same thing and can't both have it. That is why it read you twice, boy. You are the only thing it has ever met that counts.*

The boy sat with that a long time, and the sitting turned into the old stillness, the den-kind, the body's own, and under the stillness the beast paced its track and wanted the simple country, and the boy priced the pacing and let it run.

"Entered," he said at last. "The Cold runs a count. Mine is faster. It has been at its books nine hundred years and it has one column. I have a city." He stood, and the standing was slow, because the cold country past his elbow ached in the darks now, the joint Brannoc had read, bearing its weight. "You priced the flats the dark the husks came, and you priced them true. Entered: I am counting your truth, and what the truth buys you. A balanced Red that reads the Cold's war better than the boy does — that is a finding too. It goes in the book."

The warmth against the door did not move. But it went quiet in the way of a thing that has been counted, and the boy climbed to the rim with the ache in his arm and the moon's whole ledger on his back, and the count ran down the tiers beneath his hand, and the world — the flats, the lanterns, the far water, the gathering hulls — counted back.

---

He went down to the third gallery once more before the thaw of that dark, alone, against the whole floor's counted advice, because the floor's advice had been given before Ennin knelt and the advice of a thing could be priced wrong by a moon.

The tallow lamp threw its small circle on the stone of the fourth turning, and the white stroke lay where it had lain since the walls held: his own mark, cut shallow by a hand not his, face up, stopping in the middle of the line. A promissory note laid in a ledger-line. He had read it a hundred darks and it had not changed, and the not-changing had been the change.

It had changed.

He saw it before the lamp settled, and the body took it before the count could dress it — as the palm had taken the missing cold under the stone — a flinch of the whole skin, and then the stillness. There was a second line.

Beside his unfinished stroke, in the stone, in the white — thin, deliberate, cut shallow as his was shallow — a second mark. Not a copy. A reply. His line stopped in the middle, the stroke of a man interrupted; the new line began where his had stopped, one stroke's length of it, and then it too stopped, mid-line, unfinished, face up, in a hand that was not his hand but had studied his hand until the study was a kind of writing.

The boy stood over it a long time with the lamp, and the tallow burned down, and he read the mark as he read all marks, whole shape first, price after. The shape was: an entry answered in the entry's own form. He had left a line unfinished in the Cold's floor. The Cold had taken up the line, and written a little of it, and left it unfinished in its turn, and the leaving was the sentence. The mark said what the dock said and what the balanced Red said and what Grist's turned question had said from under the black water of the sump, all of it in one stroke of white in a stone floor:

*Your move, counter. The ledger is open. I have read you twice, and I am writing you back.*

The dock was not coming for the city. That finding went through him like cold water through a seam. The dock, the rows, the kneeling, the dark season — those were entries. The Cold had gone to its books, and the books were a ledger with two hands in them now, and it had set the second line beside his to teach him the form: entry for entry. Mark for mark. Cost for cost.

It was not besieging him. It was corresponding.

*Findings,* he entered, on the slate, in the flat voice, in the dark of the fourth turning, and the hand held level, and the level was bought and the price entered. *The white has answered my mark in my own hand's form. Two lines now in the floor of the third gallery, both unfinished, both face up. Entered plain: the Cold and I keep the same book now. It wrote the second line to show me the book. A thing shows you the book when the thing wants the book kept.*

He climbed. The city slept its leaning sleep around him, nine tiers of it, mouths and stores and rows, and out past the rim two hundred and one knelt in the mud in his spacing, and past the lanterns a fleet stacked in old harbors under a name his runt had said in the old sound-speech, and behind a door on the fifth tier a balanced Red lay along the wood and waited to be asked again.

At the rim the gray light came off the water, and Marrow was there, at the shoulder, where Marrow was, the counted breaths running their line, one spent, one owed.

"The white wrote back," the boy said.

The runt watched the flats a while, the kneeling rows, the long breath, the lanterns standing. Then, small, and certain, and aimed, the way only Marrow said things:

"Then somebody's got to write next."

The boy put his hand on the warm stone. Under the stone, in the deep, the quiet held — patient, kept, unspent, reading him as he read it.

"Yes," he said. "Entered."
