# Book Three, Chapter 3: The Tide-Gate

The forty came up the stair at the gray light to be made into a roll.

They came in their spacing, because the spacing was what they had, and they carried the roll's clay between them in a fired pot one of the seventh gallery's kilns had thrown for the count — red clay, wet, heavy as a held breath — and Maren met them at the rim with the slates, and the slates held the roll's shape already entered: names, tiers, ages, the debt of each and the debt's release, the terms of the passage, the terms of the return. First over, first back. Witnessed by the count's own clay. Nothing on the slate that was not on the board at the tide-gate, and nothing on the board that was not on the slate. The boy had checked the two against each other three times in the dark, because a roll with two shapes was a door with two hinges, and doors with two hinges swung the way the stronger hand pushed.

Serl pressed her thumb first.

She did it before the floor had fully gathered, before Asha-kel's flat had come up from the houses, before the under-priest's ruined hands had settled in their lap — she stepped to the pot, and set her thumb in the clay, and drew it out, and looked at the mark while it was still sharp. A keeper, checking the keeping. Then she stood back and let the rest come, and the boy watched her watch them, and the watching was the finding of the gray light: she counted them onto the roll with her eyes the way a woman counts her own jars going into a cellar for the winter, each one set, each one counted, each one hers to answer for.

There was a boy among the forty who rowed his shoulders like a man and was not one. Tev. The seventh gallery knew the name before Serl's mouth finished it — sister's son, the tiers said, the sister dead in the coughing dark of two winters back, the son kept since by the gallery whole, as galleries kept. He was fifteen by the count of the moon-feasts and seventeen by the set of his jaw, and the boy read both ages whole in the way the young one's thumb hovered over the clay a breath too long, the breath of a body asking its own price. And behind the young one's shoulder, Serl's face did a thing the boy had never seen a flat do: it went thin. One breath of thin, gone by the next, folded back under the row-voice before it could be witnessed by anyone but a boy who had spent a life reading faces that did not want reading.

She had not asked him excused. She had not moved him down the roll. She had thumbed first, so that his thumb would come second, so that the whole stair could see the order and price it as the order it was: kin after keeper, and neither one off the hook.

The boy entered it where only he read. *The faction's woman binds her own first. That is not cruelty. That is the row. Entered plain: I could not have taught her better, and I taught her nothing. That frightens me more than the board.*

The beast sat the whole swearing still. That was new. Under the ribs, where the pacing had worn its track all moon, the old thing had folded itself down and sat with its weight forward, the way it sat in the green when the herd was coming to water — not hunting. Attending. It watched the thumbs go into the clay one after another, forty small sounds the body heard as one long sound, and when the last thumb came out the beast breathed once, deep, satisfied, a sound the boy knew from nowhere he wanted to know it from.

It liked the roll. It liked the binding. Forty of the counted, entered, marked, set apart from the nine thousand and sent toward the water, and the beast read the setting-apart the way the old blood read everything: as meat divided from the herd.

*Entered apart,* the boy wrote, on the slate, in the dark of his own hand. *Watch it. It counts divisions now.*

---

The tide-gate stood at the city's foot, where the rim's stone went down into the black water and became a mouth, and the Cordon's longboat came to the mouth at slack water with the herald standing in the bow like a ledger set upright.

The boy came down to witness the first over in person, which the floor had counted against, and the counting was entered and overruled: the count had sworn these forty by its own clay, and a count that swore a thing from the rim and sent clerks to see it done was a count teaching the water it could be answered by anyone. He stood on the gate-stone with Marrow at the shoulder and the ache riding the cold seam of his arm down to the knuckles, and the longboat's crew shipped oars in one motion, and the herald came up the stone with the passage-book in his inkless-looking hands, and behind him, out past the mouth, the Cordon's lanterns stood on the water in their patient rows, hulls dark under them, and beyond the lanterns the sea ran out flat and colorless to where the world kept its harbors.

The book was read aloud, both columns, the trade-tongue slow so the stair's speakers could carry it up. Grain by the season's measure. Salt-fish, cured. Oil. Payment in metal, in salt-work, in winter-berth rights at the tide-gate, entered, witnessed. Rower's terms: the count's own, sworn, paid, rotated, returned at the passage's end or the passage's price forfeit to the count. The herald read it all in the seller's cadence, flat and exact, and the boy listened with the body and let the body do its finding, and the body's finding was that the read was true and clean down to the last entry, and the cleanness itself sat wrong, the way a floor swept too well sits wrong in a house where nothing has been swept for nine hundred years.

The herald closed the book. And then he opened it again, one thumb keeping the place, and turned to the final leaf, and the leaf held a rider, cut small, entered in the ledger-hand: *Further terms for the keeping of rowers through any delay of the thaw-wind, as the season shall require.*

The stone under the boy's feet did not move. The water did not move. The rider sat on its leaf and was small, and the small was its whole shape.

"No," the boy said.

The herald waited. It was the speaker's stillness, and under it the boy read the true thing: the man had expected the no, had been sent to collect the no, and the collecting of it had a price the Cordon had already entered somewhere the book did not hold.

"The rider goes," the boy said, flat, carrying, so the gate and the stair behind the gate could hold it, "or the count reads the board's third column aloud on the rim, every tier, every dark, until the thaw — the discount row for the small ones first. The Cordon built that column to be priced in the dark. The count will price it in the gray light, at no charge, and the sea can hear what your grain is rowed on." He let it stand. He had learned the letting from the water. "Your masters want the berth. The backs were never the cargo. Entered: a seller who prices a thing he does not want is measuring the buyer. I have been measured. The rider goes."

For one breath the herald's face did the thing faces did at the fourth turning when the counting came for them: the stillness held, and under the stillness something moved, and what moved was not anger. It was relief.

The leaf came out of the book. Cut, whole, with a small knife the man kept for the wax, folded once, and held out — not to the count, not to Maren's slate. To the fire-basket the gate-watch kept at the stone. The herald fed it himself and stood while it burned, and the burning was unhurried, and the boy watched a man make sure his own masters' paper could not be pieced back out of the ash.

"The Cordon enters the rider withdrawn," the herald said, level, trade-tongue, for the record the water kept. And then, quieter, in the register the boy had heard exactly once before, under the wool, where the man's own voice lived: "Carrier. Unpriced, unfree, and I will deny it was said: my masters are not the only buyers on this water this season. There is a stacking in the old harbors past the far light. Hulls under no name the Cordon's readers can buy. Sixty darks, and the count of them grows, and they do not fish, and they do not trade, and they burn nothing for warmth that a fleet would burn." The herald's hands folded, soft as a book closing. "The Cordon sells distance, carrier. Distance needs to know what is standing at the far end of it. We would price that stacking cheap, whatever it is, and we cannot, because it will not price back. My masters find that... unusual." A pause, and the seller's cadence came back down over the voice like a hood. "The grain rows with the next tide's slack. Your forty will be fed at the Cordon's own tables — entered, and entered because a starving rower is a slow one, and my masters do not buy slow."

He went down to the longboat. The oars came out. The black water took the hull the way it took everything, without comment, and the boy stood on the gate-stone with the ash of the rider still gray in the basket and the name the runt had said in the old sound-speech lying in him like a stone in a boot.

Sixty darks. A stacking that would not price back. Voss-Marr, if the old sound-speech held what it had held at the walls. And the fleet that had come for a boy once had learned, since, what a city was.

---

The forty went over at the slack.

They went in the count's own boats, four to a bench, and Serl set the stroke from the bow of the lead hull with the row-voice turned to its oldest use — not words now, the count, the called number, one, and two, and the oars took it and the boats came alive off the stone and moved. The boy stood at the gate and watched the spacing of them go out on the flat water, forty backs in their bent rows, gray light on the oar-blades, and the body did what the body did at water: it went back into the hull.

The dark belly of it. The bilge-stink. The chain of the wrist and the roll of the deck and the darks without number where the only count was the count of breaths you could hold before the next wave hit the side. The gap behind the teeth went cold at the memory the way it had gone cold at the thin smell of the starving city, and the boy stood on his own stone with his own water under him and let the two holdings sit in him at once: he had gone out of a tide-gate as cargo, in the dark, counted by another man's column, and he was standing at this one free, watching counted men row toward a wage with their keeper in the bow calling the stroke. The same water. The same hulls, near enough. The difference was the whole difference, and the difference was a book, and the book was his.

The beast rose under his ribs and leaned — not at the forty. At the water. It leaned toward the water the way it had leaned toward nothing since the green: the wide country, the far country, the country with no tiers and no count and no clay, where a body was fast or meat. The lean was enormous. The boy priced it, and the price was this: the beast had hated the ledger all moon, and now, at the sight of the open sea, it had stopped hating — because the sea was the one book it could read. Hulls on it. Meat on the hulls. No walls anywhere.

"You were cargo," Marrow said, at the shoulder, small, watching the boats go. The counted breaths held their line. "Cargo goes where it's rowed. You're the one standing on the stone deciding where the rowing is. Don't envy them the bench. The bench hasn't improved."

The boy breathed. The lean subsided, sulking, to the floor of him.

"Entered," he said.

The boats reached the lanterns and were taken in, one by one, by the dark hulls, and the water closed over the wake, and the count stood at the tide-gate with its ache and its roll and its sixty-dark stacking and did the only thing a count could do with a thing this size.

It carried it up the stair.

---

The Red was awake when he came to the door, and it had been awake, he understood, for the whole of the gray light and the whole of the slack — it had lain along the wood and listened to the tide-gate the way it lay and listened to the floor, and when the boy sat down across from it on the cold stone the warmth spoke before he had settled his back.

*You sent them,* the ledger-voice said, warm, exact, unhurried. *I want that entered before anything else. Nine darks ago you would have gone with them — do not enter a denial, boy, I felt the lean too. It leaned with yours. That is a finding for both books.* A page turned along the grain of the wood. *Forty of your counted, on open water, in hulls that belong to a power that prices everything and holds nothing. And here you sit, on stone, with the seam bearing, entering your own wanting in the dark where the tiers cannot read it. That is the cost they never wrote on the leash, boy. Any man can be tied. You tied yourself, and called it a count, and now the count has parts of it on the water you cannot watch.*

The boy set his back against the stone and let the cold come up out of it, because the cold was the city's own temperature and the door's warmth was not, and a body that forgot the difference was a body already half across.

"The roll is witnessed," he said. "The terms are cut. The Cordon fed its own greed to the fire to keep the berth. Entered: the crossing runs on metal and salt-work, and the forty come back bound by clay. What do you see that I have not entered."

*The stacking,* the Red said, and the ledger-voice went slow, the way it went when it was reading as it spoke, a thing the boy had learned to count as the truest register it had. *Sixty darks of hulls that burn nothing a fleet would burn. Your herald gave you that free because his masters want your eyes on it. Enter why. The Cordon sells distance, and distance is dying on this water, and a seller whose ware is dying buys the one thing that does not die.* The warmth lay flat, level as a ruled line. *Fear. They want your fear of the stacking, boy, because fear makes buyers who do not count. And the stacking itself — I will price what I can price. A fleet that does not fish, does not trade, does not warm itself, is not a fleet. It is a patience. Something is keeping sixty darks of hulls still on cold water, and keeping is the one art your enemy has mastered, and it is the one art you have mastered, and the sea does not keep. Things that do not keep come to the water anyway, when what they are keeping is out of the water's reach.* A stillness. *Or whom. You wrote a stroke to it, boy. Three unfinished lines. Say the rest in your own book: what does a keeper do when the kept writes back?*

The boy sat with it. The door's warmth waited, balanced, and under the ribs the beast had gone still at the word *stacking* — the flattened stillness, the old one, the one from before the apex print, and the boy marked that too: the old blood feared the Cold, and it did not fear Voss-Marr, and it had gone flat at a fleet on the water.

"You think the stacking and the white are one book," he said.

*I think nothing,* the Red said. *I enter. And the entry is this: you now have two correspondences open, one in stone and one on water, and you can afford to answer slowly in stone. The stone is patient with you.* The warmth drew in, and the last of it came quiet, the way the true entries came. *The water is not. Whatever holds those hulls still is paying for the stillness dark by dark, and things that pay want the paying done. Go and read your gallery, boy. I have watched you ration that reading for three darks. The third stroke has been answered since the gray light of the swearing, and you have known it in the arm since then, and you have been walking away from the knowing the way the third tier walks away from its cellar.*

The warmth left the wood. The door was only a door.

The boy stood in the dark of the fifth tier a long time before he took the lamp down.

---

The fourth turning was cold as the Cold's side of the book, and the lamp's circle found the three unfinished lines where they lay, and the boy knelt on the stone with the ache going down the seam of the arm at the kneeling, Brannoc's seam, and read.

The third line was finished.

The body took it first, one tightening from crown to heel, and then the count dressed it: the Cold had gone back to the third stroke, the stroke the boy had cut in the Cold's own hand, one length, shallow, promissory, stopped mid-line — and the Cold had completed it. Not in the boy's hand. In its own. The two hands lay in the one line now, joined at the stopping-point the way joined entries join in a ledger, and the line ran on, deliberate, shallow as a note of hand, to its end. To *an* end. The boy measured it against the first line, his own, the oldest, the one the Cold had begun answering moons ago, and the finding came up through the cold of the arm and set itself in the book without asking leave:

The third line, whole, was exactly as long as the first line, unfinished, would have been. The Cold had finished a line of his in its hand, and made a line of its own in the doing, and sized the finished thing to the shape of the thing he had left incomplete. It had read his unfinished entry down to its missing length, and supplied the length, and stopped there, and not one cut further.

Four lines in the white stone. Three unfinished, face up, waiting, as they had waited. And one whole.

*Findings,* the boy entered, and the flat voice held level in the dark, and the level was bought. *The white has closed the third line. First closed entry in the book. Closed in its own hand, over the join of mine, sized to my unfinished first. Entered plain: in the count-form, between counters, a closed line is not an answer. It is an acceptance. Entry matched to entry, cost to cost, and the column footed and ruled off. We have terms.*

He stayed kneeling, because the knees did not lift, and made himself go on reading, whole shape first, no flinching, as he read the season.

*Terms bind two ways. That is what terms are for. The white has signed its keeping to my keeping — and a thing nine hundred years patient does not sign to end a correspondence. It signs to move it. A closed line is a footing, and a footing is what a column stands on. Entered plain: the book has a foundation now, and foundations are for building. The next line it cuts will not be beside the others.*

The lamp burned small. Under the stone, in the deep, the quiet held — and the holding had changed register, and the boy had the words for the change at last, because the roll had taught them to him that very gray light: the quiet held the way a keeper holds. Not stillness. Custody. A hand under a thing, level, patient, priced.

It was not going to besiege him. It had told him so in three unfinished lines, and now it had told him the other thing, the thing under the telling, in the one finished line, and the boy knelt on the cold stone and made himself hold being answered.

He climbed. The tiers slept their leaning sleep, and the ache climbed with him, dark by dark, tier by tier, and at the rim the gray light was coming off the water, and the Cordon's lanterns stood where they stood, holding the forty, holding the coming grain, and out past the far light the sea lay flat and colorless and kept nothing at all.

Marrow was at the shoulder. The counted breaths ran their line, one spent, one owed, and the runt watched the water a while, the lanterns, the flat beyond the lanterns, before he spoke, and when he spoke it was quiet, and certain, and aimed.

"Closed line," he said. "In my country, a closed line gets collected."

The boy put his hand on the warm stone. Under the stone, in the deep, the custody held.

"Then the count holds the ledger," he said, "and waits for the collection. Entered."
