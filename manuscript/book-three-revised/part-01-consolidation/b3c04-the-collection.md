# Book Three, Chapter 4: The Collection

The grain came on the next tide's slack, and the city heard it before it saw it.

That was the finding of the gray light: sound. The tiers had learned the water's schedule without being taught — the count had entered the crossing, the crossing had entered the story, and the story had gone down the stair in the dark the way stories went — and so the galleries were already leaning out over their rails when the first hull crossed the light, and the sound they made was a sound the boy had never heard from nine thousand mouths at once. Not cheering. The count had not taught cheering. It was the long breath, the held one, two hundred darks of holding, let out tier by tier from the bottom up, so that the sound climbed the stair ahead of the grain the way warmth climbs a wall.

The body did its own finding under the noise. The boy stood the gate-stone with Marrow at his shoulder, the ache riding the arm's cold seam down to the wrist, and under his ribs the beast sat forward, weight on its haunches, and made a sound it had made once before, at the swearing. Satisfaction. He entered it where only he read — *the beast does not hear the breath. It hears the meat on the water* — and priced the finding whole: to the old blood, the hulls were not a delivery. They were a herd, driven to the drinking place, and the noise of the tiers was the noise of the herd smelling water. Nine thousand, and grain, and it counted both. It counted divisions now. It had learned the count's own trick from inside the count's own ribs.

He was still carrying that entry when the small ones came down the stair.

---

The feeding-order ran at the second turning, in the open, on the count's law: small ones first, old ones second, the tiers after, entered, witnessed, the clay-pots set out on the long shelf the freed had built for exactly this. Maren ran the shelf with her slates and two of Serl's remaining gallery-women, and the third tier stood and watched its own children eat before it ate, and the watching was the whole point and the whole cost.

The body found the cost first. The boy stood at the stair-mouth where the small ones were set apart from the line — counted out, lifted over the rail, fed apart from their own tiers in a little crowd of ribs and quick hands — and under his ribs the beast read the shelf with a slow, deep attention he had to price twice before he believed it. It was not watching the children. It was watching the *dividing.* The set-apart. The counted-out. A thing the old blood knew from a country older than the count: the slow ones at the edge of the herd, the small ones in their own track, and something in the deep of him laid that reading against the shelf of clay-pots and found the fit good, and settled, satisfied, warm.

*Entered,* the boy wrote, in the dark of his own hand, and the hand was steady because he made it be. *The feeding-order feeds. The feeding-order also divides. Everything that feeds divides, and the beast reads every division as a setting-apart for the taking. Watch the shelf. Watch it in me.*

The third tier read the shelf differently, and their reading was also true, and he made himself hold both readings at once the way he held the two hungers: an old woman at the rail, third tier, watching a small one eat with the whole of her face, and her face said — plain, in the tongue of faces — *mine, fed, first.* And beside her a man with the flat off, watching the same small one, and his face said — plainer — *fed before me.* Both faces true. Both entered. The count had bought the small ones' ribs with the tiers' patience, dark by dark, and the price was now public, standing at a shelf, wearing faces.

He stood the standing. That was the law he had made for himself at the rim: a carrier reads the whole floor, even the parts that price him.

---

The longboats came in with the slack, and the grain came off them in the Cordon's own sacks, counted twice by the count's own tellers, and behind the sacks, in the second boat, the forty came home.

First over, first back. The clay had held.

The boy read them whole as they came up the gate-stone, wet to the knee, salt in their brows, and the reading came in rows: thirty-nine in their spacing, and the spacing kept, even on stone, even spent; and the fortieth — Tev, sister's son, first thumb after Serl's — the boy found him before he found Serl, because the body found him first. The young one had gone down to the tide-gate with a jaw that was seventeen and shoulders that rowed like a man's argument with his own age, and he came back up the stone with the two finally agreeing. The water had done that. One crossing. The boy knew the size of it — one crossing could write a whole body — and priced it plain: the bench took a boy and returned a rower, and the taking was paid for at the sworn rate, witnessed, and the boy was still glad of it, and the gladness was entered next to the beast's satisfied warmth, and the two entries did not sit together, and he did not make them.

Serl came last. Of course she came last; she had set the stroke going out and she set it coming in, and when her boots found the stone she did not stagger, though the body under her best mended clothes had spent what it had. She stood the roll-call facing the count, straight-backed, and gave it in the row-voice, flat, for the record the stone kept: "Forty over. Forty back. Terms kept. Entered."

Then her face did the thin thing.

One breath of thin — the boy had seen it once before, at the clay — gone by the next, folded under the row-voice. But he had already run the count the body's way, whole shape first, and the shape was wrong by one breath, and the wrong had a name, and the name was in the second boat, and it was not Tev.

Thirty-nine stood. One lay. An older freed-man, Harn, fourth tier, winter-sworn two seasons back — the roll had him, the clay had him, the terms had him — and he lay in the bow of the second longboat with his hands crossed on his chest, and his chest rose, and fell, and rose. The long breath. The boy knew the breath before the boat touched stone. He had stood over two hundred and one of that breath in the mud, and the mud was four tiers down and a wall away, and the breath had crossed the water on its own, in a sworn man, under the Cordon's lanterns, and come home.

Not wounded. Not marked. The tellers had checked; Serl had checked; the boy checked again himself, hands and feet and eyes, because the count did not take another count's word for a thing this size. Harn's eyes were open. They watched the sky over the tide-gate with the patience of the kept, and when the boy put his hand between the man's face and the light, the eyes did not follow and did not flinch. They breathed. That was all they did, and all the Cordon's whole crossing could say about when it had started: sometime in the dark, past the far light.

Serl told it plain, because plain was what she kept. "He stood a watch at the gunwale. His own idea — nobody set it. Past the far light the stacking stands in close now, close enough to count hulls off the oar-blades, and a man looks, carrier. You told us the looking was a road. Harn looked one dark too long, and at the change of watch he wouldn't turn for the stroke, and when we took him off his feet he came off light. Light as a man can be and still be a man. And his breath—" she stopped there. The row-voice had carried every word flat to the stone, and it carried the not-saying too. "His breath runs with the boat now. It ran with the water the whole crossing home. It runs with the stone under your feet, carrier. You can hear it, if the gate goes quiet."

The gate went quiet. Thirty-nine rowers and a count of tellers and a carrier stood still on the tide-gate, and the boy listened down through his own soles, and heard it — or entered that he heard it, which under the circumstances was the same finding: a long, slow, patient holding of breath under the world, and one more voice in it now, a small one, newly entered, from the wrong side of the wall.

*Findings,* he entered, and the flat voice held, and the level was bought. *The custody has taken a rower off open water. No rows. No mud. No dock. The looking is a road and the road runs off the land now. Entered plain: I sent forty over and the water was not empty. Harn priced something kinder than his thaw, and I cannot even enter whose book he is kept in.*

---

The herald came up the stone while they still carried Harn, because the Cordon's timing was a currency and the man spent it.

He came with the passage-book closed under his arm and the seller's cadence on, and he read the settlement aloud for the record the water kept — grain delivered, entered, weighed; rowers returned, forty of forty, terms whole; the winter-berth to begin at the next dark's bell, the Cordon's hulls to lie at the count's gate under the count's law, rates as cut — and every word of it true, and clean, and the cleanness sat the way it had sat before, a floor swept too well. Then he closed the book for the second time that season, and the second closing was different, and the boy's body knew the difference before his count named it: the first time, the man had closed a book he was selling from. This time he closed a book he had been carrying like a wall.

"Carrier." The trade-tongue went slow. "Two entries, off the book. The first is free. The second is for sale, and I will tell you its shape before I name its price, because my masters have entered that you do not buy blind."

The hood came down off the voice the way it had come down once before, and under it the man's own voice lived, and the man's own voice was tired.

"The free entry: the stacking stands inside the far light now. Sixty and ten darks of hulls. They do not fish. They do not trade. They burn nothing. Two darks ago my masters hailed them under the white flag of the sellers' law, the oldest courtesy on any water, and the hail was answered. Once. A lamp was shown and hooded. Showed, and hooded. My masters have entered that as a signal, and the signal is the entry: *we are seen, and we do not care, and the next move is not ours to make — it is being kept for us.*" The inkless-looking hands folded, and the folding was not smooth. "The Cordon has sold distance for nine generations, carrier. We priced your fear of the stacking cheap, whatever it is, and I want that entered against us, because the pricing is now spent. There is no distance on this water anymore. The stacking is standing in the middle of our shelf."

The boy let the words stand on the stone. The water had taught him the letting, and the water lay right there off the gate to watch him spend the lesson.

"The entry for sale." The herald's eyes went past the boy, up the stone, toward the city — the first time the man had ever looked at the city, the boy counted, and the looking was the shape of the ware. "My masters will buy winter-room. Not berth. Room. Hulls laid up inside your gate, under your law, your count on their decks — entered as residents, not as traders, at whatever rate a thing pays when it is no longer selling distance but standing inside the last wall on the water. Name the price, carrier. My masters' instruction is a blank leaf." A pause, and the seller's cadence almost made it back up, and did not. "I will deny the blankness was shown. But it was shown."

There it was, the villain beat the whole water had coming: the factor who had carried a third column of bodies had come up the stone carrying a blank leaf and a tired voice, and the tired voice was the truest thing the Cordon had. The sellers of fear had run out of distance and wanted to buy the one thing the count had never yet sold — *standing inside the wall.* The boy priced it whole, no flinching: the Cordon's fear was worth more to the count than the Cordon's grain, because fear bought with grain was a fleet that would row for the city when the stacking moved, and the stacking had begun, dark by dark, to spend.

"Entered," the boy said, and made the flat voice carry, so the gate took it and the stair took it up tier by tier. "The count will sell winter-room. Rate to be cut in clay, witnessed: hulls inside the gate, the count's law on their decks, their oars on the count's roll if the water makes it needful. The price is this — and it is not metal. When the stacking moves, whatever the stacking is, the Cordon's hulls move with the count. Entered as one fleet or not entered at all." He looked at the man until the man's stillness gave, one breath of it. "Your masters sold me my fear back at a markup, herald. I am selling them theirs at the family rate. Tell them it is arithmetic."

The herald went down to the longboat lighter than he had come up, and the boy watched the lightness go and entered it as the day's second warning, after Harn's breath: a seller light on his feet was a seller who had bought.

---

The Red was awake when he came to the door, and it did not wait for his back to find the stone.

*You carried a long breath up the tide-gate in front of the whole stair,* the ledger-voice said, warm, exact, unhurried. *Sit. That was a spending, boy, whether you entered it or not. Your arm has been bearing cold since the stone — I can read the seam from here. Brannoc read you true and the reading is still running.*

The boy sat. The cold came up out of the stone and the ache came down out of the arm, and between the two holdings he made himself speak, because the door had earned the whole entry, not the rim's version.

"Harn breathes the long breath off open water. The custody took him past the far light, or the stacking did, and I cannot tell which book holds him, and the not-telling is a finding I am carrying badly. The Cordon bought standing inside the wall with a blank leaf. The stacking answered a hail once, with a lamp, and the answer was *the next move is being kept.* Entered: two books on the water and one under the floor, and I keep getting written at and I cannot read the hands apart." He kept the flat voice level, and the level cost what it always cost. "Tell me the true entry."

*The true entry is Harn,* the Red said, and the ledger-voice went slow, the reading-as-it-spoke register, the truest one. *Grain, fleets, blank leaves — I can price all of those; they are my old tongue. What I cannot price is what you already know, so I will only enter it. You watched two hundred and one kneel in mud you could walk to. Now you have watched one lie in a boat you sent, and the one is worse, and the worse is the finding: the mud was its country. The boat was yours. The custody has entered your own crossing, boy. Whatever holds those hulls still — the white or the stacking or the one book under both — it has begun writing in your margins. That is what a correspondent does when the column is closed and the total carried.* A page turned along the grain of the wood. *Which brings me to the third thing, the one you have not said because you have not gone down to read it yet. The fourth turning. You have been walking away from that stair-mouth since the gray light.*

The boy's hand went still on his knee.

"You felt it through the stone," he said.

*I felt you feel it,* the Red said. *There is a difference, and the difference is my leash, and my leash is the price of the leash: I feel what you feel, boy, and I feel it after, and the after is the whole of what I am. So take the finding as secondhand and price it accordingly. Something in the deep of this rock has carried a total. Go and read the page.*

He went down. The lamp threw its small circle, and the ache bore his weight down the turnings, and the fourth turning was cold as the Cold's side of the book, and the three unfinished lines lay where they had lain — three, still, face up, waiting, unchanged in the lamp's circle.

But the wall above them had been read.

Not cut. Not marked. Read — a wear in the white stone, faint as breath on a cold pane, at the height of a kneeling man's eyes, the exact height, the boy measured it against his own knees without wanting to: a patch of stone where the stone was very slightly less than stone. Polished. Not by hands. By *reading,* the way a threshold wears under nine hundred years of feet, except the feet had been attention, and the wearing was on the wall that faced the three lines, and the boy knelt where the wear said a thing had knelt — long, patient, dark by dark, at the exact point from which his unfinished lines were best read — and the finding went through him cold and total, one wave, crown to heel:

It had not been writing from a distance. It came up. It knelt in his turning, at his marks, in the form he had taught it — *kneeling, at the count's own stones, the way the freed kneel to be entered* — and read the book at reading distance, dark after dark, while the city slept its leaning sleep, and had then gone down and done the thing it had come up to do.

The total was not in the fourth turning. He found it where the day said it would be, because the day had been teaching him the page since the gray light: at the tide-gate. On the gate-stone. One line, cut in the white, shallow as a promissory note, whole — closed — beginning where the boy's own boots stood at every slack, and running the width of the gate, and stopping. Not beside the others. At the city's mouth. At the water.

He measured it, because measuring was the count's prayer. The closed line was the length of the three unfinished lines laid end to end. To the finger's width. To the knife's own width. The Cold had read his three open entries in its own kneeling, and carried the column, and footed it, and ruled it off — and cut the ruled total into the threshold where the count crossed the water, so that every crossing, every sack of grain, every sworn rower, every blank leaf, passed over the entry the way a man passes under a lintel. Or a gate. Or a yoke.

*Findings,* the boy entered, in the dark of his own hand, on the gate-stone, under the lanterns, with the sea flat and colorless past the far light. *The white has collected. Three open lines footed and ruled, the total cut in the city's threshold, sized to the sum of my own unfinished entries. Entered plain: in the count-form, a total is not an answer either. A total is what a column is carried to. Somebody is carrying it. The only question a total asks is — to whom.*

He stood on the closed line, because standing on it was the only answer the count had tonight, and the standing bore his weight, and under the stone, in the deep, the custody held — level, patient, priced, one small new voice in it breathing the long breath from a boat he had sworn home.

---

The rim held its gray light coming off the water, and Marrow was at the shoulder, and between them the counted breaths ran their line, one spent, one owed.

The runt had been down to the gate. He had stood on the closed line with the boy, both of them silent the length of a watch, and now at the rim he watched the lanterns — the Cordon's, and past the far light the dark rows of hulls that burned nothing — and when he spoke it was small, and certain, and aimed.

"A closed line gets collected," Marrow said. "You entered that. So here's what my country would enter next: a total that gets cut into a threshold isn't a bill, Rook. It's a notice. The kind a bailiff nails to a door." The counted breaths held their line. "It's telling you the account's been moved. The question isn't to whom."

The boy put his hand on the warm stone. Under the stone, in the deep, the quiet held, and out on the flat water sixty and ten darks of hulls kept their stillness, paying for it dark by dark, and in the fourth turning of his city three of his lines lay unfinished, face up, and had been read at kneeling height.

"Then the count writes next," he said. "Not the white's page. Mine. It closed a line of mine in its hand — entered, sized, collected. Entered back: I have a line to finish."

He took the graver's cold iron from his belt.

"Entered," he said.
