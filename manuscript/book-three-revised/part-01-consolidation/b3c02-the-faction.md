# Book Three, Chapter 2: The Faction

The city began to smell thin.

That was the finding of the second moon, and the body made it before any slate did: the boy stood on the stair between the fifth tier and the fourth at the middle dark, and the air off the sleeping galleries had lost its bottom. A fed city slept under its own weight, smoke and tallow and the sour warmth of too many bodies in held stone. This dark the warmth stood alone. The tallow was rationed to a thumb a gallery. The smoke was gone because the fry-pans were cold, and under what was left ran a thinner smell, the smell of cellars being scraped and scraped again, of water stretched with nothing in it, of bodies burning their own fat in their sleep. The body knew that smell. The body had crossed the water inside it, in the belly of a hull, and the gap behind the teeth went cold at it before the count could say why.

*Findings,* he entered, at the rim, in the flat voice. *The third tier's cellars reach two darks short of the ration the ration was already cut to. The salt holds at nine days against the dark season's forty. Entered plain: the season will outrun the stores unless the stores move, and the stores cannot move, because there is nothing to move them to. The count is faster than the famine. The famine does not care. It has one column and no stair to climb.*

The beast paced under that entry, all dark, its track worn familiar now, and the boy let it pace. The pacing had changed its lean this moon, and he had entered the change and priced it: the lean was no longer only toward the door out. When the fry-pans went cold on the third tier, the old thing under his ribs had stopped at its track's end and turned toward the city — toward the smells, the mouths, the counting — the way a beast turns on a staked line when the meat comes near enough to smell and the line holds. Not wanting out. Wanting *in.* And the boy had sat with that in the dark a long time, because he knew the shape of it. It was the shape from the green, from the first blood, from the pit. The beast did not hate the famine. The famine was a thing with a throat, at last. Everything else this moon was a column. The famine could be bitten.

He entered that too, alone, where the tiers would not hear it: *The beast likes the famine. Entered apart. Watch it.*

---

The Cordon sent terms on the sixth dark.

The herald came up the rim stair again, the same small dry man, the factor's wool, the speaker's stillness, and behind him two porters carried a board of white wood, and on the board the terms were written — in the trade-tongue, in a ledger-hand, in columns. The herald set the board up on the count's floor itself, so the whole council could read it or have it read, and that was the first finding of the dark, before a word was priced: terms set on a board, facing the room — a man's wares, set out for the buying. Not a message. A shop.

"Grain," the herald said, slow, in the trade-tongue the count could take. "Salt-fish, cured. Oil. Quantities entered here against the season your water has told my masters you face — you will note we have not asked how long your stores run; that entry you keep; we have priced its shape from the smell of your smoke." A pause, measured, a seller's pause, letting the room feel the size of the grain before the price came. "Payment is entered in the third column."

The boy read the third column before the herald spoke it, because the boy read columns now as he had once read mud, and because his body had read it first anyway, in one tightening of the whole skin.

The third column was bodies. Not named as bodies. The trade-tongue had a cleaner word — *hands,* entered by count, by tier of origin, by age-band, priced descending from the strong to the old, with a discount row for children under ten, because children stacked. Rowers, the terms said. Season-rowers, owing, counted, delivered at the tide-gate at the Cordon's convenience, to be returned or kept at the Cordon's further terms, which were entered elsewhere, in a book the board did not hold.

A hold built for stacking. His body knew that build. His body had been the cargo that taught him.

The gap behind his teeth stayed cold. The hands lay open at his sides, and the open was bought, and he entered the price of it where only he could read. Then he said, flat, carrying, so the floor could hold it:

"The Cordon said it did not come to buy."

"The Cordon does not buy," the herald said, and the stillness held, and somewhere under the wool the man's own voice knew what it was carrying. "The Cordon carries. What my masters buy is *distance,* carrier — grain grown on a shore your fear never touched, rowed to you by backs your law will never meet. The backs are the boat's price, not the cargo. That distinction is entered." And then, because the Cordon priced information and had already decided this next piece was free, the same free that showed what the priced pieces cost: "You will want to know who told us the shape of your famine. No one did. Your smoke told the water. Hunger is the loudest word a city says, and yours is shouting. My masters only wrote down what the whole sea could hear." The inkless-looking hands folded. "The board stands until the thaw-wind. After the thaw-wind, the third column's prices go down — the grain goes up. That is not threat. That is season. My masters enter that you of all people will understand: it is arithmetic."

The herald was rowed back down. The board stayed, white, columned, facing the room.

---

The faction came on the ninth dark, and the boy had counted it coming, and had entered its dark wrong by two.

He had priced the pressure as a house-move — Asha-kel's arithmetic finding the board and reading profit in it. It came from under instead. It came up the stair from the freed, and a woman brought it.

Serl. He made himself hold her whole before he priced the part. Serl of the seventh gallery, who had stood the second rank at the fourth turning when the standing white came, who had held the row when the man beside her went down and her own breath had run long and gray and come back — Serl the row-holder, the name the third tier said with the flat off, the way people said a thing they had leaned on. A wide woman, gray at the temples, freed-man's mark old and pale on her forearm, and beside her on the stair, when she came, stood forty of the freed in the old spacing, not kneeling, not pressing, just standing, the wall-law's own set of the shoulders, come to be witnessed. She had dressed for the count's floor in her best, and the best was mended, and the mending was even, and the boy read the evenness: a woman who kept her things. A keeper. She had kept the row. She would say she was keeping the tiers now.

"Carrier." She did not kneel; the freed did not kneel to the count, they stood, that was the law he had made. "I have the seventh gallery's words, and the third tier's, and half the second's, and I will say them plain, because plain is what you taught us." Her voice was the row-voice, made for carrying down a gallery's length, and she set nothing soft in it. "Take the third column."

The floor went still. Maren's chalk stopped against the slate and did not click down. Lorren, at the wall, turned — turned his front to the room, and the boy priced the turn whole: Lorren had come to show his back too often lately; the front had too much on it.

"We ran your count," Serl said, and lifted her own slate, a freed slate, chalked in the tally-hand the road had taught them. "Not to argue it. To *agree* with it. The city is more mouths than the city is food — your words, carrier, gone down the stair and back up in our own mouths. We made the same entry you made, and then we made the entry you won't make." She held the slate up, facing him, as the herald's board had faced the room. "Three hundred rowers at the Cordon's third-column prices buys the grain to carry the other nine thousand to the thaw. We ran it three ways. The two-column answer is: send three hundred, or bury eleven hundred before the thaw-wind, and the eleven hundred are not spread even. They are the third tier's small ones and the third tier's old ones. The count knows it. We know the count knows it." The row-voice did not waver. "We are not asking to sell our people, carrier. We are telling you they are already priced. The Cordon priced them in the open, and the famine priced them before the Cordon did, and the only party at this floor who has not entered their price is the one who keeps the book."

There it was. Pride, arithmetic, and fear in one woman, and the boy made himself hold all three, and none of the three false, and that was the terrible part — he had learned to price the houses' flat faces, the callers' arithmetic, and here came a face he had no column for: the woman who had held the row at the fourth turning to save the mouths, come to sell three hundred of them, for the mouths. The same hands. The same keeping. It was not betrayal and it was not hunger talking. It was the row-law run to its end: the row holds what the row can hold, and a row that cannot hold nine thousand holds three hundred *for* nine thousand, and calls it holding still.

The under-priest stood. That alone moved the floor; he had sat through Ennin, through the husks, through the white's second line, saying nothing, his ruined hands in his lap, his nothing carrying the doctrine's whole nine hundred years. Now he stood, and the ruined hands rose with him, and the voice that came out was cracked and quiet and aimed like Marrow's small certainty:

"The tithe fed the pit," he said. "One throat in ten, chosen, entered, lawful, so the rest might live unlooked-at. We ended it. We said the city would never again price its people into the cold's mouth." The hands worked, the ruin of them showing white at the knuckles. "And now the third column stands on this floor, and it is *tidier.* Rows, ages, discounts for the small. Carrier — I buried the tithe with these hands' last work. Do not make me dig it up and find it dressed in wool."

"It is not the tithe," Serl said, flat, and the two of them stood facing across the count's floor, the woman who had held the row and the priest who had ended the rite, and the boy let them hold the facing, because the room needed to see what he could already see: they were the same argument at two ages. The tithe had also been a famine's child. Nine hundred years ago somebody had stood on some floor and said *one in ten, so the nine may live,* and it had been arithmetic too, and it had been true too, and it had lasted nine hundred years because it worked until it didn't.

The beast, under his ribs, watched Serl as it watched the flats. That went in the book too. The beast had no argument with her. That frightened him more than the board.

---

He did not answer her that dark. He entered the finding — *the faction's count is true; the truth is the trap* — and gave the floor the count's law: the board would be read to every tier, whole, third column and all, because a city that had ended a tithe in the open would not now price its people in the dark. Serl took that with a slow nod, the nod of a woman who had expected worse and priced the not-worse, and led her forty down the stair, and the stair carried the spacing of them back into the city like a sentence carried down a gallery's length.

Then, at the middle dark, he went to the door.

The warmth lay along the wood, low, level, and it was awake before his hand found the stone beside it — it had been awake for the whole floor, he understood that; it had lain along the door and listened to Serl run the arithmetic in the row-voice, and it had said nothing, and the nothing was the finding.

"You priced her," the boy said. "Say it."

*Her columns close,* the Red said, in the ledger-voice, warm, exact. *Three hundred against eleven hundred, weighted for the small and the old, discounted for sentiment at the margins. It is good arithmetic, boy. I know the hand.* The warmth moved along the door, slow, a page turning. *So do you. You ran it in your own head on the sixth dark, standing over that board, before the priest's face made you set it down. I felt you run it. You totaled it in nine breaths.*

"Entered," the boy said. "I ran it. The running is not the question."

*No,* the Red said. *The question is what the running makes you. I will give you the true entry, free — you remember what the free ones cost.* A stillness, level as a ruled line. *There are two counts on this rock now, and you keep both of them, and they have begun to disagree. The count that is a law wants the city whole, mouths and all, and pays dark by dark in hunger and risk and the watching of small ribs. The count that is a *ledger* wants the column to balance, and the column balances the moment the third column clears the tide-gate. Serl keeps the law's count with the ledger's arithmetic. So do the houses. So, some dark, will you. It is not a faction, boy. It is a seam, and it runs through every tier, and through the floor of this room, and through the middle of you.* The warmth lay flat. *You asked me a moon ago whose ledger the city is. I have watched you carry the answer since. It is heavier than the famine, and you have been rationing your reading of it the way the third tier rations its cellar.*

The boy sat with his back to the cold stone and the door's warmth against one shoulder, and made himself read the answer whole, as he read the season, no flinching — and the reading was this: that the faction was not wrong, and not the houses, and not the Cordon. The seam was his. He had built the count to end the tithe, and the count had learned to price, and the pricing had come back up the stair in a keeper's row-voice wearing its best mended clothes, and it had called itself *plain,* because he was the one who had taught the city that plain was honest. The arithmetic the Red had put in his hand over six moons of doors — the arithmetic he had caught himself running at the rim, selling the houses their fear back at a markup and calling it craft — that arithmetic did not stop at the tide-gate. It priced children by the row. It was on the white board in the trade-tongue and in his own head, and it was *good,* and good was never again going to be the same as his.

"I will answer her tomorrow," he said. "Not with the seam. With a count."

*Every count you run from now on,* the Red said, and the ledger-voice had gone quiet — a thing that has said its true entry and will not price the silence — *runs on that seam, boy. Entered or not.*

---

He answered the faction at the gray light, from the rim, with the city's own floor called up the stair to hear it — Maren with the slates, Serl and her forty in their spacing at the stair-mouth, the under-priest with his ruined hands, Asha-kel arrived flat from the houses with the houses' arithmetic running under her flat, and behind them, tier below tier, the galleries leaning up the stair as a field leans under wind.

He gave them the seam first. That was the price of the rest, and he paid it in the open, in the flat voice, carrying, as the count said everything that mattered:

*Findings. The faction's count is true. Three hundred against eleven hundred, and the eleven hundred the small and the old. Entered plain: I ran the same count, at the same board, in nine breaths, and my breaths were faster than hers, and that finding goes in the book and stays in it. The arithmetic that stands in the Cordon's third column did not come across the water. We taught it to ourselves, dark by dark, tier by tier, and I taught it hardest. If the city sells three hundred of its counted to buy back its own conscience, the sale will not end at the tide-gate, because a law that prices its people once has priced them forever, and we buried that law in this floor with the tithe, and I did not dig it up this moon to have it shipped home in wool.*

Then he turned the column as he had turned every column the houses had ever handed him — around, into the light, so it worked for them instead of on them.

"But the famine is also true, and the famine does not read. So hear the count's answer. The count crosses water." He let it stand, because the words were not his only — they were the leavings' question come back from under the sump, turned all the way around, and he made himself say them where the whole stair could hear him answer Grist through nine tiers of stone. "The count crosses water — and it does not sell its counted. Grain will come across. Salt-fish will come across. The Cordon's hulls will carry it, and be paid — in metal, in salt-work, in winter-berth rights at the tide-gate, entered, witnessed, terms written. Not in backs. And the rowers who work the passage will be the count's own: sworn, paid, rotated, returned, each one owing nothing but the terms, bound as the road-sworn were bound and the winter-sworn were bound. And the first names on the rower's roll—" and here he looked at Serl, and let the look carry the whole price, giving her the same terms the count had ever given the ones who proposed a spending — "are the ones who proposed the row. Serl of the seventh gallery, and the forty who stood her spacing on my stair. You priced three hundred, and the count heard you. The count prices you forty, first over, first back, your passage witnessed by the count's own clay. If the third column is holding, you will hold it best. If it is a sale, you will be sold first. That is the law this city keeps now. Entered."

It was cruel. He entered that too, where only he read it. It was also the count's oldest move, the move that had bound Dorrik to the road and the winter-sworn to the spur — *the ones who want the spending stand the spending* — and it worked as it had always worked, not because it shamed them but because it priced them. And Serl stood the pricing. She stood it as she had stood the second rank, breath running long, and then she said, in the row-voice, flat, and under the flat was something the boy would enter later as the sound of a seam bearing weight without breaking:

"Then we row. Entered. And, carrier—" the slate came up, her own tally-hand chalked fresh in the dark — "the grain comes to the small ones first. The Cordon's board prices children at a discount. The count will not. First over, first back, first fed. Say that part on the stair, so the third tier hears it from your mouth and not the story's."

He said that part on the stair. And the stair took it down, tier by tier, into the leaning galleries, and Asha-kel watched him do it with no face at all, which was the houses' way of entering that they had just watched a faction be turned into a shipping-concern and a feeding-order, and that the houses' own arithmetic had been run around in the open, again, at no charge.

---

That dark he went down twice.

First to the sump, alone, and stood on the grating over the black water, and put the answer through the stone in the vibration the Skell read, because a question turned all the way around deserved its answer in the shape it was asked: *The count crosses water. Grain, not backs. The count keeps its counted. — And the leavings of the broken Vorth, the ones that will not bow: the count will cross for those too, when the count crosses. Entered down here, where the keeping is.*

The floor held his feet a long time after, the black water still, and when the grist's answer came up at last it came slow, shaped old, and it was not a question this time. It was one shape, laid flat, held: *counted.*

Then up, and past the fifth, and down the fourth turning, with the tallow lamp in his hand and a graver in the other fist — the small iron the freed used to cut tally-marks into doorposts, borrowed at the middle dark from a doorpost that would not miss it before the gray.

The third gallery was cold as the Cold's own books. The white stroke lay where it had lain, and beside it the second line, thin, studied, unfinished in its turn, face up: *your move, counter.*

The boy knelt on the stone, and the cold country of his arm ached down its joint at the kneeling, Brannoc's seam, bearing weight, and he let it bear this. He set the graver's point where the second line stopped. He did not copy and he did not answer in a new hand. He took up the Cold's own line, its angle, its shallow cut, its deliberate spacing, the way it had taken up his — a hand studying a hand until the study was a kind of writing — and he cut one stroke's length of it. Deliberate. Shallow as a promissory note. And he stopped, mid-line, unfinished, face up, and laid the graver down beside the mark like a rule laid beside a ledger-line.

Two lines and a third, all stopping in the middle. The book open. Entry for entry, cost for cost, and the third stroke said what the grain-fleet and the rower's roll and the seam in his own floor could not say to a thing nine hundred years patient:

*The keeping is mine too. I have read your form. My hand holds the pen. — Write when you've priced what that means.*

He stood, and the lamp held its small circle, and under the stone, in the deep, the quiet did not move, did not lean, did not warm or cool. But it held. The way a thing holds a column open for the next entry.

He climbed to the rim with the ache in the arm and the graver's iron still cold in his fist, and behind him in the dark of the fourth turning, three unfinished lines lay in the white stone, side by side, and none of them had a total.

Out past the flats, two hundred and one knelt in his spacing, and past them the Cordon's lanterns stood on the black water, and the sea between here and the thaw-wind — the boy read it whole, at the gray light, hand on the warm stone — was now, entered and priced and witnessed, the widest tier of all.

*The count crosses water,* he entered. *Held breath. — Let it. The count was never the walls.*

Marrow was at the shoulder, counted breaths running their line, watching the lanterns.

"Rowed water before," the runt said, small, and certain, and aimed. "You were cargo. Now you're the manifest."

The boy put the cold graver in his belt, next to the slate, iron against clay, the two hands of what he was now.

"Entered," he said.
