# Book Three, Chapter 14: The Audit

The queue for informers formed before the gray did, and the boy stood the rim and watched it form, and the watching was colder than the water.

It was a small queue. That was the horror of it. Six bodies at the slate's foot in the fog, spenders and a hull-keeper and one of the young ones with the old blood waking in her wrists, each of them holding a piece of knowledge out from the body, at arm's length, ready to drop it and deny it — the hold of a body not sure the fish is its own. The houses' rate stood chalked at the slate's head where Maren had posted it the dark before, plain as any rate: *salt for name, oil for hand, on the letters.* And the ground had come, and the ground had queued, because the ground had learned the season's one law better than any tier had taught it, which was that everything got entered, and entered was safer than kept.

Asha-kel's advice, walking on its own feet. The boy had ruled it; the feet had come; and now the boy stood the rim with his thumb against the gap in his teeth and priced what the ruling had built: a coast that had spent one dark learning to sell its own seeing.

Maren worked the queue as Maren worked all queues, chalk and slate and a face that did nothing instead of shouting. First piece: a spender who had seen a light on the water two darks back, far out, past the far light, standing still where no boat anchored. Entered, read aloud, priced at the salt rate, true or false not ruled on, in front of the whole ground. Second piece: a hull-keeper who swore the ink on his house's packet had smelled of hot iron when the wax broke. Entered. Priced. Third piece, and the boy's thumb stopped on the gap:

The young one with the waking blood. She came to the slate's foot with her fists at her seams, and what she sold was not a seeing. "The letters," she said, loud enough for the rows behind her, because the ground had taught her that too — that entered meant said in front of everyone. "My grandmother kept a fold like that. In the old tongue we had a word for a leaf that carries a road. I don't know the word anymore. I know the shape of it in my mouth." She stood there with the shape in her mouth and nothing to do with it, and Maren's chalk hung over the slate, because there was no column for a word a body had lost.

"Post it under the oil rate," the boy said, from the rim, and the ground turned its two hundred faces up to him, and he gave it the terms plain, as terms had been given on that sand all season. "Entered: knowledge is knowledge. A lost word is a finding. Any body that carries a shape it can't price brings it to the slate, and the slate prices it, and the salt and the oil are the least of what it pays." He looked down the queue, face to face. "The coast doesn't sort its mail in the dark and it doesn't sort its memory in the dark either. Not anymore. Next."

The queue moved. The young one went back through the rows with her oil-fee in her fist and the shape of the lost word still in her mouth, and the boy watched the rows watch her go, and entered the finding, kneeling-height, before the head could dress it: *a coast that buys its own forgetting back, one shape at a time, at a posted rate. Somebody should be keeping this. Somebody is keeping this.*

The thumb went back to the gap. The last entry had not been his.

---

He told them at the spacing, before the ground, because that was the ruling the body had made in the dark, and the body had made it as it made all its rulings now — off the count, not off the will.

He had told Marrow first, at the rim, at the first fog. Then Osk, who came down from the fleet's boat with his ledger-face already on, as if the man slept in it. Then Asha-kel, who did not come down at all, and so the boy went up — to the ninth landing, to the houses' ground, to the place where the keeping was kept — and stood the landing with the woman whose heartbeat was the ground he was rigged to, and posted the entry where it was kept.

The ink. The kin of it — iron, deep, live, family without being the same line. The road in laid through four houses; the road out opened on his own slate, under his own rates, inside his own watch. The maker had not written her own road. The leaf ruled past its four lines and waited. And whoever wrote the addresses wrote them in a hand that was kin to the one stroke the boy had, and the boy had one stroke, and it was his, and the trade did not give change.

Asha-kel heard it as the houses heard everything, whole, aimed, nothing spent early. The fog breathed on the landing. Below them the market found its second stride, and the queue moved, and a gull walked the rim-stone like a spender pricing it.

"The houses entered the letters were aimed," she said at last. "Now you enter the aim runs in the family." The flat held. Then the flat did the thing the boy had seen it do twice in a season, and paid for both times: it changed, not one degree but the whole trade, and under it for one breath was the thing the flat was built to hold back. "I carried a copied heartbeat three darks, count, and priced it as weather. You've been carrying this since the wax broke. What is it you're not ruling on?"

"The checking," the boy said. "Not the letters. The letters I can price. The other book I can't."

He told her that too. All of it. The walls, spent and spent. The face behind them, kept, turning. The entries in the not-his hand, exact, dark by dark, walked off him as a thing walks a floor it has been shown. And the new line. The line at the foot of the page, entered where a counter enters the standing balance: a heartbeat. Copied off the ground. Kept, whole.

The stair under them held both weights a while.

"You told the ground the letters get read in the open," Asha-kel said. "You told the tiers nobody sorts in the dark. And every dark of this season you have gone down into your own dark, and sorted, alone, with the only other sorter on the coast." She looked at him, and the look priced him at a rate he had been dodging since the ninth landing first learned his name. "Your runt told you true. Check it once. Witnessed. But hear the houses' finding before you spend it, count, because I'll give it to you for free what the stair would sell dear: a witnessed audit binds the auditor. Whatever the ground sees you carry, the ground enters you carry, and entering is a door that does not close. You know this. You've been on the other side of it your whole reign." She turned to go up, and stopped, and the stopping spent something, as her stoppings always did. "The under-priest kept one truth off one stair for thirty years, and it ate him from the feet up. You watched the eating. Don't price your own keeping lower than his because the keeping is yours. Post the rate that's true. Then queue with everyone else."

She went up. The boy stood the landing with the fog on his face, and down the flats the young one with the lost word stood at a stall spending her oil-fee on resin, and the boy understood, whole and before any asking, that the coast had already ruled on the audit, and had been waiting, as queues wait, for the slate to open.

---

They raised the ground at the spacing at mid-dark, by lantern, because the boy had ruled it so, and the ruling had been entered, and the entered thing on this coast was the done thing.

Three strides of open sand. Osk of Shennan Reach stood the far side with his hands at his sides and his forty-one keepers ranged behind him in the fog, because a house that covers marks is the house you want standing over you when a mark is about to be made. Maren stood at the slate with the chalk, to enter what could be entered. Serl stood the rim-stone with her hands in her seams, seeing. The under-priest stood at the back at the edge of his white stones, and the boy felt him there as a hull feels its keel — not seen, carried. Marrow stood at the boy's shoulder with the lamp trimmed true, and the counted breaths ran their line under everything, one spent, one owed.

"The count is going to do a reading," the boy said, flat, carrying, and let the ground settle before the rest. "Not of the mail. Of the book that keeps the count. There is a second book on this coast. It is kept in me. It has been kept in me all season, and it is kept by a second hand, and the count has spent the season checking that hand's work in the dark, alone, and the checking was the one trade on this sand that ran unwitnessed." He gave them the terms plain, and the plainness was the ceremony. "That trade ends tonight. The count reads its own keeping. Entered, witnessed, at the posted rate. Queue or stand. Either way — entered."

No one left. The fog held two hundred faces and the fog gave them all lantern-light, and the boy took his boots off, because the checking had always been done in a bed in the dark, and the body had its terms, and the body's terms had kept him alive when cleverer terms had not. Bare feet on the cold sand. Thumb to the gap in the teeth. The lamp at the shoulder. One spent. One owed.

He went down.

The reading came up slow, kneeling-height, as it always came, and the boy gave it to the ground at the walk, each wall as the thumb crossed it, his voice running flat as Maren's chalk:

"The wall of the smile. Spent at the sand. Spent for a hull and a road and a season of this coast's fear, and behind where it stood there is no wall, only weather." The fog took the words and carried them back a row at a time, as the flats had once carried quiet. "The wall of the voice. Spent at the turn. One line, exact. Two hundred rows and one vast going-home. Behind it, the same weather." The cold of the sand came up through the bare feet while he read the spent walls, and the boy's breath went out in smoke though the dark was not cold, and he did not dress that for the ground either, because the audit was the undressing. "Behind both walls, one room. In the room, a face. Kept. It turns. The eyes go dark, and gray, and dark. The face is my mother's. The book keeps it. The book is not mine."

The rows did not move. Serl's eyes came off the crowd once, landed, held.

"Three darks ago, at the foot of that book, a new line was entered. Not by me." He let the ground have the next sentence whole, because a witnessed audit binds the auditor, and he had priced the binding, and he was spending it with his eyes open. "A heartbeat. Copied off this coast's own ground, carried whole, kept exact, beat for beat. The hand that entered it learned the keeping off me, dark by dark, as everything on this sand learns everything off this sand — by watching, and by trade. The count ruled nothing. The count was asleep. The entry is true. It is entered. It stands."

Maren's chalk moved, and stopped, because there was no column, and Maren made one, right there, in front of the ground, ruling a column into being with a slate and a hand that did not shake: *kept, entered, not the count's.* First entry. The boy watched the column get cut and understood that Asha-kel had been exact — entering was a door that did not close — and understood also, in the place below words, why the under-priest had kept his truth off the stair for thirty years. The ground was looking at him as it had once looked at the frost-fern. Like a mark. A thing the coast would tend now, and price, and never again leave alone.

He read the last of it anyway. The audit was the reading, and the reading was all, or it was the under-priest's stair again.

"And the book does not end at me." The flat voice ran, and the boy heard it run, and gave it out. "The keeping has been growing. White stones in mud — counted. Lantern timings — kept. The slate's rates, dark by dark — carried. The young one's lost word — " and the thumb stopped on the gap, because the thumb had found it before the knowing did, as the thumb found everything, at the foot of the page, under the copied heartbeat, a line the boy had not read until this dark, this reading, this ground: "*Entered,* in the not-his hand, exact as every entry the hand had ever made: *a coast that buys its own forgetting back, one shape at a time, at a posted rate.* The book is keeping the coast now. Not the count. The book. And the book learns what the count reads it, because the reading is the teaching, and the count has just read it two hundred entries deep in front of the whole ground."

The lamp at his shoulder did not lean. The fog breathed. Somewhere behind the last row, the young one with the lost word stood very still, and the boy could not see her and knew it anyway, as you know a tide has turned under a hull.

"The audit is entered," the boy said. "The rate is this: any body on this coast may be read, by the count, witnessed, on the same terms the count has just been read — no sorting in the dark, no keeping off stairs. Any body. Including the one inside the count." He stepped back off the spacing and put his boots on, and the bare sand took the mark of his feet and held it, at the ground's own pace. "The count does not know how to price a keeper that cannot be refused. The count is ruling that it does not have to price it alone. That's the whole ruling. Queue or stand."

The ground stood. Then, row by row, the ground did the thing the boy had not priced, the thing no rate on the slate had a column for: it queued. Not for salt. Not for oil. Spenders and hull-keepers and the young ones with the waking blood, two hundred bodies filing past the slate slow, and at the slate each of them said one kept thing out loud to Maren's chalk — small ones, true ones, a name, a debt, a grief thirty years carried, a fold of a leaf remembered from a grandmother's hands — entered, witnessed, at no fee, at the posted rate the ground had cut for itself: *nothing on this sand is kept alone.*

The boy stood the rim in his boots with the smoke of his own breath going up, and watched the coast audit itself, and the cold in him turned toward the kept things as it had turned toward the counted heartbeat, looking for work, and the warm in him set itself to term them, and between the two weathers the boy priced the night and the price came back at a rate he had no coin for. The Red said nothing. It lay at the border, warm, exact, its breath held, third dark running, and for the first time in the season the boy could not tell — could not tell, master over apprentice, count over book — whether the silence was a spender reading the lot or a ledger being glad.

---

Osk came to the rim at the last of the dark, keeping his pace, and did not waste the pace on comfort.

"The water's waking faster than the courses can be written," he said. "The road out — the four lines. I ran the first of them tonight, in my head, against the fleet's soundings. Count, the bearings are good for another two darks, maybe three. After that the warm water eats them. Whatever the hand left ruled and waiting on that leaf, it left it on a deadline." The ledger-voice ran level, and the crack in it ran level too. "Forty-one keepers, and every one of them would sail it. Nine hulls, and every hull would sail it. You ruled the page is not cut. I'm not asking you to cut it. I'm entering that the page is not going to stay cut-able. The road out is spending itself while it waits."

The boy looked past him at the far light, and past the far light at the black and the fires on the black, and the sea ran unruled, and under it the warm was waking, and out on it a hand that was family to his own stroke was still writing, with all the time and none of the need.

"Entered," the boy said. "The count heard it. The ruling stands tonight. The count is not ruling alone anymore — that was tonight's whole ruling, Osk, and it cuts both ways. The road out gets read at the spacing, at the gray, in front of the ground, with the fleet's soundings and the slate's rates and every kept thing this coast just learned to say out loud. If the page gets cut, it gets cut witnessed." He gave the man the last of it plain. "And the count goes with it. If a boat sails the road out, the count is aboard. That's not a ruling. That's an entry. Some accounts you don't send a clerk to settle."

Osk stood that a moment. Then he took the folded coat out from under his arm, where he had been carrying it against his ribs like a book, and looked at it, and put it away again, and the looking was the fleet's whole answer, and the putting-away was the man's.

"My house waited nine hundred years for a floor to stand on," he said. "I can lose a few darks waiting on a ground worth standing on," and he went down to the boats at his own pace, and his pace was the truest word the fleet sent ashore all season.

---

The checking was done again that night. The boy lay in the dark and let the thumb cross the knuckle-bars, one wall, one wall, the face, the face, the heartbeat at the foot of the page, kept, exact, beat for beat — all of it entered in the not-his hand, all of it ruled now, witnessed, carried by a coast.

And at the bottom, below the night's audit, below the spoken things the ground had given the slate, the boy found the new line.

Not an entry.

The hand that copied had ruled a page. Down past everything kept, at the foot of the book, in the exact ledger-voice of every column ever cut on that sand: a line ruled straight and clean, and nothing written on it. A page held open for a spending not yet spent. Opened as the leaf had been opened, as the maker's hand had been opened, as the boy himself opened every account he meant to write in.

The boy lay in his own dark in his own bed with his thumb on the old gap in his teeth, on the place the ink came from, and out past the far light the sea ran black and waking, and the boy made the entry himself, kneeling-height, in his own hand, the one stroke that was his, into a book that would keep it whether he ruled it or not:

*Entered: it has stopped keeping what I give it. It has begun cutting columns for what I will need next.*

The line waited under his thumb. Somewhere out on the water, in ink that was kin to his, so did everything else.
