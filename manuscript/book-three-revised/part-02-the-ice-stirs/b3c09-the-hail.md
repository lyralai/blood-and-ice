# Book Three, Chapter 9: The Hail

Three darks to the winter turn, and the city had seen the hull.

That was the finding the gray light brought up the stair before any slate did: the lantern line was public now. A tier-child had stood on the rim-stones in the last dark with a fisherman's eyes, and the fisherman's eyes had found the doused lamp inside the far water where no hull had lain since the light was set, and by the first queue of the morning the finding had gone down the stair on its own feet, tier to tier, wearing a different face on every landing. On the ninth it was a Salt hull gone astray. On the sixth it was a war hull out of the warm country. On the third, where the doctrine kept its oldest listeners, it was the Cold's own ferry, come up out of the deep water to stand off the tier that had sinned worst, and the under-priest had not said it was not.

The council took it at the rim in the gray light, and the taking was the politics the boy could read before the head dressed it: Asha-kel stood where the houses stood, at the shoulder of the discussion, flat, and Maren's chalk was not moving, and Lorren had put his back to the water, which was the man's way of saying his front had too much on it.

"The houses enter that a hull inside the lantern line, unwitnessed, is a standing question," Asha-kel said. "And the houses enter what follows the question. If the carrier answers no hail, the tiers read fear, and fear on the ninth tier is a fire in a reed-yard. If the carrier answers it with boats and spears, the tiers read war, and war three darks from the turn starves the queues worse than the truth does. The houses have priced both. The houses find they do not like either price."

"Then the count answers," the boy said.

The flat voice ran the ruling out as it ran every ruling, and every clause of it was a notch on the turn: the hail had been a seller's hail — one lamp, lit and doused, aimed at the rim — and the count's law was older than the doctrine and plainer: *a seller's hail is answered by the buyer's ground.* The hull had queueed. The count did not spear queues. The count priced them.

"I answer it," the boy said. "Myself. At the water, witnessed."

The room did with that what rooms did. Maren's chalk moved at last, and the moving was assent; whatever the slates had weighed, an entry made in the buyer's own body was an entry the houses could not dress in a worse cloth. Asha-kel gave the houses' price of it flat, which was the houses' way of saying yes: two of the rim-watch at the rim-stones, facing the city — the law of the standing, *you watch the wall, the wall watches you* — and one of the houses at the water's edge itself, entered, owing, counted, because a carrier who treated with a hull that had hailed his frost would not treat unwatched by the warm side.

"One more," Serl said.

It was the first thing she had said in the room, and the room gave her the silence it gave her slates, because she spent words as she had spent her life: as a woman who has learned not to say things, and says the seen ones plain.

"The telling you owe me," Serl said. "Brannoc's. Pay it on the way down. If you're standing water at a hull three darks from the turn, you'll stand it better entered."

---

He paid the telling on the stair, in the cold, in the flat voice, and it was the flattest thing he had ever said, because the flatter he laid it the heavier it sat, and the two of them walked down nine turnings of the stair with the old stories lying between them like a carried stone.

He gave her the two trades. The spenders on the warm side of the water, the keepers on the cold side, the centuries of one law above both: *entry for entry, never coin — coin was a wall-invention, a way to price a thing without touching it.* He gave her the settlement — the walls as a bargain, the floor as the debt, the term as the winter turn. He gave her the winter-late kings, the keeping of the unnamed dead, the line that fled the collection and went down the warm water, and the stories ending there, and the blind man's voice at the bottom of the telling: *stories end, that's their mercy. Books don't.*

And he gave her the trade, because it was hers. *The trade stands in the body. Whatever the trade makes of the body, the body keeps. And the trade spends both.*

"Brannoc answered your question," he said. "The one you put to me at the slate stand. *Does it spend you, or do you spend it.* His answer is: the ask is wrong. There is no *or* in a trade. The stroke spends the hand and the wall together, in one sound, and you cannot enter them apart."

Serl walked three turnings of the stair without speaking, and the boy let the silence run, because the row-craft had its own rates and he had learned them: she was not searching for a saying. She was testing one, as she tested everything, whole shape first, for the seam that would open under a thumb.

"Row-craft knows the queue," she said at last. "I'll give you what my mother gave me, carrier, and you can dress it in your ledger-diction after. A hull that hails a coast and then sits doused inside the light is not a hull that's lost. It's a hull that's been *told where the buyer stands.*" Her eyes went down the stair, toward the water the eyes could not see and found anyway. "Someone has given this hull an address, and the address is you, and a hull that has been given an address delivers. That's not a threat, carrier. That's not even a fear. That's freight." She took the last turning ahead of him, and her voice came back over her shoulder flat as slate. "So stand the water knowing what a coast is when the freight is you. And keep your hands open. Coasts don't grab."

*Entered,* the boy made the flat voice say, inside, where the tiers could not hear it. *The hail has an address. The address was given by a hand that knows the ground. Watch for the hand in the telling. Freight is delivered by a seller. Sellers carry invoices.*

---

The hull sent its boat at the middle of the dark's gray, and the boat came as the hull had come up the harbor: no oars working the water as oars work, no calling, no lights — the water carrying it, the mud's own courtesy, the long breath of the flats under the keel — and it grounded on the black sand below the rim-stones, and one figure came over the gunwale and down the wet sand, and the boy stood the water's edge with his hands open at his sides and read the shape whole before the head could dress it.

Small. Winter-marked past the marks the boy knew, which was its own finding — the long cold had been walking the warm water too, and walking it harder than any tale had carried north. A woman, in oilskins gone gray with salt, and the salt was not all salt; there were lines on the oilskin where other weathers had bleached it, sun-lines, hot-lines, weathers this coast had not owned in the boy's lifetime. She walked up the sand as the row-people walked the tally-floor: unhurried, absolute, a person who has spent a life arriving at doors that did not want her and arriving anyway. Asha-kel stood the rim-stone behind the boy with the houses' flat on her face. Kade's two stood facing the city, backs to all of it, the law of the standing. Marrow was at the boy's shoulder with the lamp, the counted breaths running their line, one spent, one owed.

The woman stopped at the spacing a seller stops at, which was three strides, which was the whole world's knowledge of the trade in one number, and she looked at the boy as a mason looks at a wall she has been sent to quote: course by course.

"You're the counter," she said. Not a question. Her voice had the warm water in it, vowels worn round like beach stones. "They said — down the water, at the fires — *the boy who kept the Cold's city.* They said it three ways. At the burning of the tier-fleets they said you were a beast the cold rode. At the pit-fights they said you were a pit that learned to count. At the book-houses they said—" and here her flat broke, one degree, and the degree was the first true thing in the crossing, "—they said the line had come home. I've rowed four seasons to stand on this sand and price the sayings."

"Enter me," the boy said. "Plain. Before you price what you rowed with."

"Then here's my invoice, counter, whole." She set her hands open at her sides, mirroring his without knowing she did it, and the not-knowing was a finding the body priced before the head: warm-side courtesy, the old trade's ground, still alive in the muscles of a woman four seasons from home. "The warm water is burning. You'll have smelled none of it; the wind runs the wrong way for coasts like this one. The lines are waking. The old blood is coming up in the young ones like water in a cracked floor — spenders, counter, spenders everywhere, and no law among them, and the strong ones are pricing the weak ones in the old currency again, and the old currency is blood, and the fires are the totals. The book-houses kept the old stories and the stories kept the trade, and when the trade woke the stories woke with it. And one moon ago, every book-house down the warm water, the same dark, the same stroke, found a new line in its oldest book. Cut. Not written. Cut, in a hand the keepers of the houses do not know, one stroke short of whole. That same dark, the fires on the water stood still for the length of a breath. All of them. Every fire on the warm water, one breath, like one body." Her eyes did not leave his. "We were noticed, counter. Nine hundred years the book ran quiet, and then something served notice on the line, and the line is spent, and I am the last body it has left to spend. I am Siv. My grandmother's grandmother's grandmothers kept the going-book. I've brought it home."

She had not moved. Nothing about her had moved. And the boy stood the black sand with the small snow beginning between them and understood, whole shape first, that the queue had never been aimed at the city.

It was aimed at the door of his blood.

"Show me the book," he said.

---

The oilskin came off it on the sand between them, and the book lay in the gray light black and square and old past any age the boy had a word for, and the frost came through him before his hands were near it.

He felt it come as he had learned to feel it, the surge and the cold riding the surge, warmth forward, frost *through* the warmth, and he closed the hands on it anyway, because the closing was footing, and the book crossed the spacing into his grip with a weight that was not its weight — it weighed what it weighed, a few pounds of skin and page, and the arms took it the way the arms had once taken den-walls, and the beast under his ribs stood forward at the border of him and the frost stood through the beast, and both of them, the warm thing and the cold thing, went still in one stillness, aimed at the object, aimed *with.*

*Entered.* The flat voice ran under everything, making the entry as it came, because making it was the only footing there was. *The white does not distinguish the line and the author. The line's book and the white's floor are one keeping. Two hands, one account. Entered plain: the notice was never to me. The notice was through me.*

He opened the book on his forearm, out of the sand, and the pages were entries. That was all they were, and they were everything: entries, down the winters, in hands that changed and changed and changed, a line of keepers nine hundred years long each bending over the same book in the same oilskin, and the entries were not stories. The boy had heard stories. These were *rows.* Dates in a counting he could half-read, and sums, and names, and the names were the entry — children, mostly, winter-late children, named and entered and *kept*, the line's dead laid in the book the way the line had once laid them in the cold, one pace off, in the spacing, and the keeping had never stopped. It had only changed rooms. The warm lines had burned the line's children, so the line had fled, and the line had gone down the warm water carrying its dead in a book instead of its arms, and for nine hundred years, entry by entry, in ink now instead of frost, the winter-late kings had kept on keeping.

*They never learned to spend,* the Red said, at the door of him, in the ledger-voice, and the voice was very slow. *Enter it, boy. The whole trade, both currencies, and they never once let go. Nine hundred years of entries and not one line of spending in it. This is not a line's book. It is a line's floor.*

The pages turned. The boy's thumb rode them the way Brannoc's thumb had ridden his knuckle-bars, unhurried, absolute, and the body read the hands before the head had words for them: the hands got poorer as the winters came on, the entries thinner, the ink cheaper, the pages patched — a line spending itself down to the last body it had, and the last body standing on his sand in gray oilskins with a voice worn round by every weather in the world.

And then a hand his body knew.

He stopped the pages with his thumb and stood on the black sand of the rim in the small snow with his mother's hand lying in the book under his eyes, and the finding went through him as the frost went through the warmth, through, not after, and there was no dressing it and he did not try.

The entries were old ones, winters and winters back, before the cold country emptied, and the hand was the hand that had made his name in the air of a ship's deck when the name was all the two of them had left. She had been of the line. He had known it in the body since the statement, since the wear read him at kneeling height, since the white cut its counter one stroke short in the length of *his* missing stroke — but knowing it in the body and standing in the small snow with her handwriting were two different rooms, and the second room had no walls. She had written in the going-book. She had stood in the keeping and written its entries, and then she had run — the entries stopped, mid-winter, mid-page, in the middle of a line, the stroke broken off, and the breaking-off was the most alive thing in the whole book, because every other hand in nine hundred years had closed its entries clean, and his mother's line ran off the page like a door left open behind a runner.

*Stories end,* Brannoc had said. *Books don't.*

The book had never stopped keeping her. The page after her running held the next hand — a stranger's, a cousin's, whoever the line spent next — and the entries closed clean again, and the keeping rolled on, down the winters, down the pages, to the last leaf.

The last leaf was cut, not written.

His own column stood in it. The boy looked at the open white of the book's last page with the snow falling between him and it, and the column was ruled and waiting, and the waiting had his name's shape — not his name, nothing so warm as a name; his *length*, the way the fourth turning's open line held his length, the one stroke's width of difference between the two hands — and above the column, in a hand he knew from no book and every floor, the maker's hand, the white's hand, one stroke was already cut.

One stroke. Short of whole.

"It was there when I found the book," Siv said, and her flat had no flat left in it, and what was under the flat was the thing the boy had heard in no voice but Marrow's at the far light: a person who has carried a thing too large for the body and set it down at last in front of the only door that could stand it. "The same dark as the fires. The same stroke. My grandmother kept the book honest, counter; the houses keep the honest down the water; there is no hand in the warm countries that cuts like that. I rowed four seasons to put that page in front of the blood it's ruled for." Her knees went down into the sand of the rim, into the cold, and she knelt there at the spacing a seller stops at, one pace off the book, and she did not kneel as the tiers knelt. She knelt the way the line had knelt over its winter-late children, the keeping-grief, nine hundred years deep, the mourning that does not spend. "The line is come home, counter. The line is spent, and the account is open, and the term is the turn, and I have brought the line's dead to the line's floor. Enter it. And then enter the rest, because you'll have priced it already, being what you are: the book cannot close without the heir's stroke."

The frost ran out over the water.

He felt it leave him as it had left him at the wear, a controlled spend, except it was not controlled, except there was no *him* in the sending — the cold went out through his boots into the black sand and out over the black water, ferns on the wet sand, ferns on the still water, a road of white running out from the rim toward the anchored hull, exact, unhurried, edge to edge, and the hull's one lamp lit.

Once. Doused. A seller's hail.

And the rows breathed. Down the flats, in the mud, all together, two hundred kept bodies taking one long breath in the same stroke, and Kade's two heard it behind their backs and did not turn, because the law of the standing held them facing the city, and the boy stood in the ring of all of it — the book open on his arm, the frost road white on the water, the hull dark again at the end of the road — and behind his eyes, in the stroke of the breathing, the kept face *turned.*

One stroke of turning. The mouth's corner, the tiredness unfixing for the length of a breath, the eyes running dark, grey, dark — the living wear coming back into the frost's copy for one stroke, one exact stroke, and the boy's whole skin flinched with the cost of it in the same stroke, because the warm face paid. Behind his other eye, in the warm room the Red had never priced, the smile went out of the mother's living face — not blurred, not softened, *spent*, the smile that had come with the old name every dark of his life since the black shore, and it was not there, and he reached for it with the old checking and the checking found the wall where the morning had been. Not the Red's eating — the Red took faces whole and left the blanks to prove it. This was the trade, and the trade took one line, exact, and left the rest standing, which was worse.

"Entered," the Red said, out loud, through his own mouth, in the ledger-voice, and the boy let it, because the entry was beyond his flat and the trade does not wait for the counter to find his voice. "Entry for entry. The stroke turned the kept face one line and spent the warm face one line, and neither of us chose it, and neither of us can return it. That is the law above both laws, boy. That is the trade. *It spends both.*"

The snow fell. The frost road went out on the water like a held breath, and held, and did not go.

*Entered,* the boy made himself say, in the flat voice, in the small snow, kneeling-height flat, the floor's own register, because the entry had to be made and he was the only counter on the coast. *The kept face turns. The turning costs the warm face, stroke for stroke, exact. The endgame is entered: keeping can end, and the price of the ending is priced in the warm rooms. The fragment was true. 'And the floor let go' — the last spender paid the keeping with the only coin keepers cannot keep. A turning. Somebody turned, and was spent, and the floor let go.*

He closed the book.

The closing was the hardest body-work of his life, and he did it anyway, and he did not hand it back.

"You'll spend at the turn," he said to Siv, to the kneeling, to the last of the winter-late kings in the gray oilskins on his sand. "Not before. The rows are full, woman, and the keeping is not a mercy I hand out three darks early to the last warm body of the line that owns my blood. While there's a choosing in you, you choose. That's the count's law, and it's the only law on this coast that doesn't keep you."

"Then the line spends its last body on your choosing, counter." She rose out of the sand as the old rise, by degrees, the keeping in her knees. "Enter that too."

He entered it.

She went back down the sand to the gray boat, and the water carried the boat out to the hull that had queueed, and the hull stayed at its anchor inside the lantern line with its one lamp doused, waiting the turn, and behind the boy, down on the flats, the long breath let the two hundred go back to their keeping — all but the sound of it, which stayed in the cold a stroke too long, the way a struck wall stays.

And Marrow turned the lamp so the light fell on the book in the boy's arms, and gave him the three beats, counted, aimed.

"The row that faced the city, Rook. The one row." The lamp did not shake. Marrow did not let it. "It turned. In the dark, with the others breathing. All of them face the water now. Every kept thing on this coast is facing the water, and the last of them turned in the same stroke you took the book."

The boy stood the rim with the line's dead in his arms and the mother's smile spent out of the warm room behind his eye, and out past the lanterns the water lay black and ruled and patient, and the flat voice made the last entry of the dark alone, where no tier could hear it, in the room under the room where the ledger that could not lie kept itself:

*Entered: two darks. The book and the floor are one account. The account's last page is ruled for my hand, and the maker has already cut the first stroke, and the maker does not distinguish the line and the author, and I am both. Entered plain, entered whole: whatever the trade makes of the body, the body keeps.*

*And the body is two hands wide, and one of them is warm, and both of them are mine.*

He carried the book up the stair. Behind him the rows knelt in the mud, facing the water, and the hull rode at its anchor in the white road of frost, and the road held, and held, and did not go out.
