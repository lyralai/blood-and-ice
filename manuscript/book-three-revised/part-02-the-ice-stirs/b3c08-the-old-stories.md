# Book Three, Chapter 8: The Old Stories

Three darks to the winter turn, and the boy woke with two faces behind his eyes.

That was the finding the body made before the gray light came: he had always woken with one. The warm one, the worn one — the face he checked each dark the way a man checks a coal he has carried through the night, and the checking had its own small ritual, thumb crossing the knuckle-bars where the whiteness lay, the body buying the face a dark's more turning. But the warm face did not turn alone now. Beside it, at the same distance, held at the same reading height, stood the other: the kept face, exact, planed, the eyes stayed, the mouth's tiredness fixed, coin-true. Two rooms at the back of him. One breathed. One was kept.

And the body had made its own craft of it in the sleep, wordless, an old craft, the craft that had once carried the den: the warm face it turned, each dark, a dark's further turn. The kept face it did not look at. It stood the kept face the way the wall stood weather — facing it, bearing it, and giving it nothing.

*Entered,* the flat voice said, at the gray light, the breath falling small and white toward the floor. *The cold keeps what I keep off the books. It read the one row I stood alone, and its entry stands, and the standing changes nothing and I will not pretend it does not stand. But the warm face turned again this dark. Entered plain: the frost has a copy. It does not have the turning. The turning is mine.*

He went down the stair to Brannoc before the tiers woke, because the answers were arriving before the ask now, and the boy had priced that finding whole in the ring of frost: a thing that answers before you ask is a thing that already has you in its book, and the only footing left was to ask first. To find the oldest reading in the city and put the question to it before the turn put it there for him.

The blind reader kept a cell off the third gallery, below the tally-house, where the walls were oldest. The boy had entered the cell before, in the first darks of the count, when the walls were strange to him and Brannoc's fingers had read the boy's own arm — the seam at the elbow, the ache that rode the joint, *Brannoc's seam*, the whole city called it now, without asking itself why a blind man's name sat in a mason's body. The door stood open. It always stood open. The man had no lamp to burn and no face to hide, and he sat at his stone table in the cold with both hands flat on a slab of wall-stone older than the tiers, reading nothing, waiting, and the boy understood as he came through the door that the waiting had a direction. It was aimed at the stair. The man had heard him from the fourth turning, as he heard everything, and had known the step, and had not moved.

"Sit," Brannoc said. "You've a dark left in you that wasn't there a moon ago. The stair says so. The stair says everything; that's its trade."

The boy sat. The stone bench took his weight, and the ache ran down the arm at the angle of the sitting, and the old man's face turned at that — the small, blind, exact turning, the fingers making the small motion on the stone, the motion he made when a thing wanted reading twice.

"Give me the hand," Brannoc said. "The one you've been carrying like a thing that bleeds."

---

The ferns lay faded in the knuckle-bars, whiteness under the skin, old scar-work that had not been made by any wound. The boy set his hand in the blind man's, palm up, in the circle of the cold, and Brannoc read it the way he read everything: whole shape first, thumb riding the bars, fingers walking the spread of it, unhurried, absolute — a touch with a stroke-rate, row-craft in it, the oldest reading in the city doing its work on the newest wrong thing in the city.

And the frost read him back.

The boy felt it happen before the old man's face made the finding — a gathering, faint, under the skin, the ferns waking to the touch the way a coal wakes to a breath, and he watched Brannoc's thumb stop mid-bar and stand, pressed light, not moving, and he watched the whiteness rise under it and *complete*. One fern, finishing its stroke under the reading finger, edge to edge, exact, unhurried — the missing tip of it closing like frost seals a crack in dressed stone, without hurry — so that when the old man's thumb lifted, the fern lay whole where a breath before it had been cut short.

The blind face did not change. That was the worst of it. The face had made this finding before.

"You can stop your hand from shaking," Brannoc said, level. "It doesn't help. Mine shook for a moon. Here's the entry, boy, and I'll say it in my own trade, stone-wise, and you can dress it in your ledger-diction after: I have felt that finishing once before. Under this city. In a floor. I was nineteen years old and I had eyes, and I was the best graver the third tier had ever bred, and the guild sent me down to the old turning to copy a fault in the white stone before the wall-menders packed it." The fingers left the boy's hand and lay flat on the slab, the small motion going. "The fault was not a fault. It was a hand, in the stone, shallow as a promissory note, and I put my fingers in it to read the cut — pride, boy, plain pride, I wanted to know the tool — and the cut *finished* under my fingers. It wrote one stroke more of itself, using my reading for its graver. And I read the stroke. That was the cost. The reading is the road; you entered that yourself, I'm told, the dark Ennin knelt. The stroke went in through the fingers and it has been going in, dark by dark, for thirty years. I did not go blind, boy. I was read blind. Slow. The stone paid me out one dark of light at a time, and every dark I told myself it was the lamp-smoke."

The cell was very cold. The boy's breath fell white between them, and he watched the old man's ruined eyes, their stillness, their patience, and he priced the whole shape at once, shape first, the reading arriving before the dressing: the council's blind reader was the city's one living man who had stood on the road Ennin knelt on and walked back up the stair. Thirty years the man had sat below the tally-house with his door open and his eyes spent, and the tithe-doctrine upstairs had called him touched, holy, safe — a blind man tells no marks — and the whole time he had been the only book in the city that held the old turning's hand.

"You never told the floor's mark to the council," the boy said.

"I told it singing," Brannoc said. "Thirty years. Every tithe-hymn the under-priest's house ever stood, I stood in it, and I buried the true stories inside the cant, line under line, where only a reader could find them, because the last man who told the old stories plain in this city, the tiers burned him in his own yard for keeping the Cold's company. I was nineteen and I had eyes and a trade and I chose the singing. Enter it against me. I have."

"Entered," the boy said. "Not the burial. The keeping. A man who buries a thing and carries it thirty years has not spent it. It's in your hands yet, and your hands are steady. Sing me the plain version."

---

This is what the blind man gave him, in the trade-voice, the stone-voice, slow, the fingers reading the slab the whole time as if the stories lay in it, which the boy understood, before the telling was done, they did.

Before the walls, the water had two trades. That was the oldest story, older than the tiers, older than the tithe, older than the doctrine's nine hundred years. On the warm side of the water were the spenders: blood-lines that burned hot, that paid and were paid, that could spend warmth out of themselves into a wound or a bargain and take the price back in trade. On the cold side, the keepers: lines that did not spend. Lines that held. What a keeper's blood touched, it kept — kept the shape, kept the leaf, kept the dead exact — and the keeping asked nothing and gave nothing and never once, in all the old stories, in all the winters the stories counted, let go.

"Two trades, two currencies," Brannoc said. "And the stories are plain on this, boy, plain as graver-work: never one body. Not once. The spenders feared the keepers because a price entered against a keeper did not come back. The keepers feared the spenders because a spender could spend *into* the keeping, warm the held thing loose, make a kept thing turn. They fought, down the old winters, when they fought, and they traded when they traded, and the law of the trading was one law, and you have stood on it: entry for entry. Never coin. Coin came later. Coin was a wall-invention, a tithe-invention, a way to price a thing without touching it." The small motion, twice. "That's your entry, boy. The one your ledger cut off. I heard you make it — the stair tells me things, I told you — the dark you knelt in the frost. *The old currency was never the coin.* Say the rest yourself. You've had it since the statement."

The boy sat in the cold with his breath falling white, and the two faces stood at the back of his eyes, one turning, one kept, and the flat voice said it, whole, the entry closing like a seam:

"It was the line."

"It was the line," Brannoc said. "Blood for blood, line for line. A spender's debt runs in the spending. A keeper's debt runs in the keeping. And the walls—" the thumb came down once on the slab, hard, a graver's stroke, "—the walls are a settlement. Not a defense, whatever the doctrine sings. A bargain. Nine hundred years ago the keeper-line that held this water priced a debt against the warm lines that ran the old trade here, and the price was the floor: the turning, the white, the book under the city. A debt entered in the line's own stone, to be collected at the line's own term. The tiers built on the settlement and forgot it, and the tithe made the forgetting a hymn, and the Cold sat under the floor with the account open, keeping the stone, keeping the shape of the bargain, waiting out the term the way keepers wait. It does not want your city, boy. It has never wanted anything. That's the horror the stories carry and the doctrine buries. The Cold does not want. It *holds*. And the holding has a term, and the term is the winter turn, and the statement you took through Harn was notice — not to you. You're the warm side's residue, one boy with a spender squatting in him. The notice was to the line."

"My line," the boy said. Flat. For the record, which was the only way he had ever said a terrible thing.

"The stories call them the winter-late kings," Brannoc said. "A keeping-line that fled the cold country when the settlement cut, rather than stand the collection. Kings of a keeping, boy. What they kept was kin. When a child of theirs died before its naming, they would not burn it — the warm custom — they kept it. Kept it exact. Stood it in the cold and stood with it, one pace off, in the spacing, and mourned it standing, sometimes for years, because a kept child is not spent and a kept child is not gone and the line would not price a child into the fire. The warm lines called it abomination. The doctrine still sings it as abomination." The old man's face turned, blind, exact. "Enter what the rows at your rim are, boy, in your own ledger, and tell me whose spacing they kneel in."

The boy did not enter it aloud. The body made the entry whole and unwilled, a flinch of the whole skin: two hundred kneeling in the mud, breathing the long breath, kept, and Ennin one pace off in the spacing, kept, and the dock built in his own hand's shape, and the whole rim of it saying, in the only language a keeper has ever spoken — not *mine*. Nothing so warm as *mine*. *Kept.*

"The line fled," the boy said. "The line died out."

"The stories say the line went down the warm water," Brannoc said, "and the stories end. Stories end, boy; that's their mercy. Books don't." He stood then, slow, the standing of a man whose body had its own ledger of thirty cold years, and he crossed the cell to the wall, and he set his palm flat on the oldest stone, and the boy came to stand beside him, and the old man took the boy's hand and set it on the stone beside his own, and under both palms, deep, the stone was not cold. It was held. There is a difference, and the body knew it before the head could dress it: the stone was not taking warmth from his hand and it was not giving warmth to it. It was *keeping* his hand's warmth, exactly, the way the frost kept the leaf it killed — the warmth of him entered and held and not returned, and the wall under the tiers of the city lay in the hand of the floor like a ledger under a counting hand, and every warm body that had leaned on it for nine hundred years was in the stone's keeping, a little, entered, and the boy pulled his hand back and the pulling took more strength than the pulling from a beast.

"Spender in a keeper's body," Brannoc said, quietly. "That's you, if the stories price true. First one since before the walls. The trade was never one body because the trades don't fit one body — a spender's whole law is *let go, at a price*; a keeper's whole law is *hold, forever* — and you've got both laws in you, boy, and each of them thinks it's the landlord. The old stories have no ending for you. There's no entry. The book your two hands write is a book the stories don't hold." The blind face turned up at him, and the level voice went one degree off level, and the degree was the finding: "So I'll give you the only thing my thirty years bought me that the stories don't hold, and you may enter it as an old blind man's price or as stone-true, as your count likes. The reading is the road, and the road runs both ways. The stone read me blind, dark by dark, because I read it and did not know I was spent. You knelt in the frost and it read you, and you've been reading it back since the fourth turning — you and the white, entry for entry, mark for mark, and that is a *trade*, boy, the oldest trade, the two-currency trade, and in the old winters, when a spender traded a keeper, there was one law above both laws, and every story agrees on it, and here is the law:

"The trade stands in the body. Whatever the trade makes of the body, the body keeps. Your warm one spends your mother into you a dark at a time — the turning, you called it. The cold one holds her exact, and it does not have the turning, you entered that yourself, and the entering is stone-true. That's the law keeping you whole between them, boy, and it has a price, and the price is Serl's question, and you tell that row-woman I answered it, because the stories answer it: the trade spends *both*. It spends you, and you spend it, at once, in the same stroke, like a hand and a struck wall. The last spender that traded a keeper — there's one story, half a story, a fragment the doctrine didn't find — traded three winters, and what the trade made of the body, the body kept." The fingers made the small motion, once, and stilled. "And the story says the keeper was changed too. Kept warm. One degree. For one winter. And then the story ends, and I have always read that ending, boy, thirty years, as the most frightening line in stone: *and the floor let go.*"

---

He was at the rim when the gray light came, with the old stories lying in him like cargo, ballast-true, distributed, the body walking its rounds under the weight as it had once walked the den-country under den-walls, and Marrow was at the shoulder, where Marrow was, the counted breaths running their line, one spent, one owed.

The runt did not spend words. He turned his face to the water, and the boy read the shape whole before the head had the telling: the flats. The rows. Two hundred kneeling in his spacing in the gray light, breathing the long breath, heads all turned to the city, as they had turned them —

No.

Not all. One row had its faces to the city. The rest — the boy stood at the rim-stone and counted, and the counting ran out of him flat, unwilled, dark by dark since the crown — the rest of the rows, a hundred and ninety and more, knelt facing the *water*. Facing out. And in the offing, inside the lantern line, where no hull had lain since the far light was set, the hull sat at anchor. Come up the harbor in the dark, no oars, no sound, no hail — the water carrying it the way the mud carried the rows — and it lay off the third tier with its one lamp doused, and it was waiting, and the shape of its waiting was a shape the boy had stood all his life on both sides of.

It was queueing.

*Entered,* the flat voice said, at the rim, in the small snow, with the two faces standing at the back of his eyes, one turning, one kept. *The white does not distinguish the line and the author. It does not distinguish the city and the debtor. At the winter turn it collects the account — and the account is not the blood and was never the coin. The account is the line, and the line is a keeping-line, and a keeper collects in kept things, and everything that kneels at this rim is entered.*

*The turn comes in three darks. The queue is forming. And the old stories' fragment — the one trade, three winters, the floor letting go — is the only entry in the world that says the keeping can end.*

He stood the rim, and the beast under his ribs stood with him, aimed *with*, the way the Red had aimed, the body of it turned toward the water like the rows, and out past the lanterns the one lamp of the hull lay doused like a stroke cut one short of whole, and the gray light came up off the harbor the color of a coin no one would ever spend again.
