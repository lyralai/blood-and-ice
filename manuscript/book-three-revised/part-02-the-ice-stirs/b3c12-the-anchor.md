# Book Three, Chapter 12: The Anchor

The dark after the turn was the first dark in a season with nothing due, and the boy lay awake in it anyway, because a body that has stood at attention for a season does not know how to be dismissed.

The waking was the checking. It had been the checking since the sand, and the body did it now before the boy was in it, thumb crossing the knuckle-bars of himself like a keeper walking a floor. The wall of the smile first. Spent at the sand, spent for good, spent for a hull and a road and a season of the coast's fear, and behind where it had stood there was no wall now, only weather, only the draft of a room with a window gone. The wall of the voice second. Spent at the turn, spent for two hundred rows and one vast going-home, one line, exact, and the boy's thumb crossed the place it had stood and found the same draft blowing through it. Two gaps in the same warm house. Behind them both, in the room that was still his, the face turned. The eyes, dark, gray, dark. The wreck of the smile. The living line of her cheek that no stroke had touched and no stroke ever would, because the account was closed, and a closed account strokes nobody again.

The checking priced both walls and found nothing owing, and nothing owing was the new condition, and the boy lay in the dark and understood that he did not know what to do with it. A wall that is spent is a wall that is not holding anything up. All season the trade had run its two lines through him — cold to the keeping, warm to the term — and the tension of those two lines had been his shape, the way rigging is a mast's shape, and last dark, at the spacing, he had cut the rigging himself with his own inked hand, and it had been right, and the mast stood.

But nothing was pulling on it now. And a mast with nothing pulling on it is only a tall thing waiting for weather.

Down the flats, the fog breathed. Ordinary fog, mud-warm, rising off the place two hundred rows had knelt since before any living keeper had rowed, and under the fog the ground was doing the one work the boy had never seen it do in his lifetime: nothing. The wind walked the water. The lantern line stood its rim-to-far-light watch with every flame trimmed and idle, a wall with no war. Out on the road's old reach the sea moved, black and unruled, carrying nine fires that crackled and spat and did not care what they lit. The boy listened to his own heartbeat come and go in his ears, priced it — unheld, ordinary, his — and did not know until Marrow's lamp swung at the tent-mouth that he had been listening to it as a man listens to a clock in a house where a death is expected.

"The houses are up," the runt said. "The market's asking for you. Or near enough asking. They're being polite about it, which from spenders is asking."

The lamp hung steady on its hook, trimmed true, the runt's hand and the runt's habit, and the counted breaths ran their line under the words, one spent, one owed, and the boy rose into them as he had risen into them every dark since the rows — off the count, not off the will, because the will was a currency the body no longer dealt in alone.

"One spent," the boy said, to let the runt hear the count taken.

"One owed," Marrow said back, and the three beats were counted and aimed, and the dark was entered, and the coast was ordinary, and the boy went down to it.

---

The market had come ashore with the gray.

It stood on the buyer's ground in the fog like a thing grown overnight: three rows of stalls where the stalls had no right to be, boats hauled up past the tide-line with their keels cooling, spenders and burned keepers and the young ones with the old blood waking in them, all of them pricing the ground and being priced by it in return. Salt for resin. Resin for lamp-oil. Lamp-oil, at a rate that made Maren's chalk move for the first time in two darks, for work — hull-work, wall-work, the plain labor of a coast that had to learn again what its hands were for.

Maren stood at the tally-slate the houses had raised at the head of the ground, and the chalk went in his fist, and the chalk wrote, and the writing was the coast's whole sermon: nothing was owed here. Everything was for sale at the posted rate. Queue or trade.

Serl stood the rim-stone above it with her hands in her seams, spending nothing, seeing all of it, and the boy felt her seeing land on him as a watchman's lamp lands — not accusing, counting. Asha-kel stood where the houses stood, at the shoulder of the ground, wearing the flat, and the flat was doing the work it always did.

"The tiers want the story before the second queue forms," she said, without greeting, because the houses did not spend on greeting. "The story they have is this: the Cold came, the count answered it, two hundred of the kept went down mud-warm, and the tiers woke to a market on their shore run by the boy who bled for all of it. That story walks on its own. The houses have watched stories walk all season. The ones you don't dress walk wherever the feet want to go." A beat, flat. "The houses enter that this is the one dark the feet should be aimed."

"And the priest?" the boy said.

Because the under-priest was on the flats. In the fog, past the stalls, past the last boat, the gray figure knelt in the mud where the two hundred had knelt, alone, setting something into the ground small by small — river-stones, white ones, one at each place a row had settled — and the boy had entered him there at first sight and entered him now again at the naming: a man who had spent thirty years keeping a lie upright to spare his tiers, standing in the mud doing the dead's tally with his own hands, on his own feet, an hour before any count asked.

"He asked the ground," Asha-kel said. "The houses priced it. A priest set to penance is a cheaper story than a priest set to nothing. Penance aims the feet." The flat did not change, and then it changed one degree, which from Asha-kel was the width of a door opening. "He buried truth for thirty years to keep a riot off the third tier. I have stood in rooms with the man since before you had a name on this coast, count. I never once saw him kneel to the thing he kept. The houses entered his relief last dark, the same as you did. The houses' finding is that relief is the one coin a keeper cannot bank. It spends him."

The boy looked down the flats a long time. The gray figure set another white stone.

"Let him aim his own feet," the boy said. "Entered: the rows were kept. The keeping cost what it cost. If the coast wants a story, the coast can trade for the true one at the posted rate."

Behind him, Maren's chalk moved.

---

Osk of Shennan Reach was waiting at the spacing.

He stood where the maker had stood, three strides off, at the count's own distance, and the boy took the ground between them as he had taken it every dark of the season — flat, open-handed, priced — and stopped, and looked down, because the frost-fern had changed in the dark.

The coat lay over it still, burned cloth on burned sand, and under the hem, where the fog had worked in, the ferns had gone to damp. Not melted. Gone over — frost into wet into ground, the way ice goes over in a thaw, quietly, at its own term, without asking anyone's leave. The boy crouched and lifted the coat's edge, and the ferns under it stood yet, exact, the turned stroke of the face laid into the sand, but the edges of them had begun to weep. It would be gone by the next dark. The ground was already taking the mark back the way ground takes all marks, which is slowly, and all at once at the end, and without malice.

The boy put the coat back as it had lain.

"It's going," Osk said. "You could keep a piece. Frost keeps, they say, if a cold hand wills it. Yours would." The ledger-voice ran level, and the crack in it, the width of one degree, ran level too. "Some in the fleet would want it kept. A relic. The last mark of the thing that kept our houses' dead for nine hundred years. There's a price on that, count, and the price would be high, and the fleet would queue to pay it."

"I don't trade in relics," the boy said. "It kept. That was its work. The work's done. It goes at its own term." He stood. "The coat stays till the frost is done with it. A keeper's mark isn't moved before its term. Your house knew that law better than any house on the water."

"My house," Osk said, and stopped, and started again, and the starting-again was the whole man, as the walking-his-pace had been the whole man: "My house covered marks on a floor for eleven generations, count. It never once got to say the law out loud. The law was just what we did. You say it on this sand in front of nine hulls and a hundred spenders, and it's a ruling now." He looked at the coat. "I don't have a floor to cover anymore. I have a burned coat, a boat's share, and forty-one keepers who rowed here on the strength of my say-so. That's the whole inventory."

"Then trade," the boy said, and said the rest as terms had been given on that sand all season: plain. "A house that covers marks has work on this coast. The flats will want tending. The dead of the rows will want keeping in whatever way mud keeps. The houses will price you and price you wrong and re-price you, and you'll arrive at their doors all your life, and you'll keep your pace anyway, because that's the other law, the one nobody writes." He watched the man's hands, because the hands had told the truth about Osk on the first dark, on the water, before the mouth had. "Queue or trade, Osk. Or stand. The ground has room for a standing house."

The hands did not move for three beats. Then Osk of Shennan Reach took the coat off the frost, folded it once, wet side in, and held it against his chest like a book, and the boy entered him true: not grateful. Gratitude spends and is gone. Claimed. A man who had rowed eleven generations of other people's law across a burning sea, told for the first time that the law would hold land.

"You should know what you bought with that ruling," Osk said, and the ledger-voice came down to the register spenders keep for the news no one has priced yet. "We didn't find you. No chart of this water is worth its ink, and the warm water is waking so hard now that half the old courses are lies. The fleet found you because a packet found us. Forty darks out of Shennan, in a channel no book-house lists — a sealed packet, no sender's mark, and inside it, one leaf. A route. Bearings, currents, the lantern-line's timing to the half-dark, this sand, this count. Written in an ink no keeper on nine hulls could name." He let the coat take his weight a moment, as walls take it. "We compared notes on the water, after the trade woke and every hand on every hull felt it. Three other houses got packets. Different channels, different ports — same hand, same ink, same leaf. Somebody is writing addresses, count. Somebody has been writing them all season. And every packet we ever heard of points the same direction the water points."

He nodded past the boy, past the rim, past the coast, out at the far light and the dark past the far light.

"This one."

The boy stood the spacing and felt the fog move on his face, and the flat voice under everything made the entry before the head could dress it, kneeling-height, in the room under the room:

*Entered: the hull had an address. The fleet had an address. The packets are a hand, and the hand has been at work longer than the season, and everything aimed at this door was aimed by something that knows the door down to the lantern-line's timing. The maker came down the aisle to be let go. The maker did not write its own road. The layer was laying it through me.*

The Red said nothing.

The boy waited for it — waited for the doors in that voice to open, for the pricing to start, *a hand that writes addresses is a hand that can be read, boy, and readers can be* — and nothing came. The border was warm and quiet and held its breath, and had been holding it since the turn, and the quiet lay under the fog heavier than the fog, and the boy entered that too, with the care of a man logging a crack in a wall that had not fallen yet but had stopped being a wall he could swear by.

---

The cost came at the second gray.

It came as the account's closing had promised it would come, which did not make the coming easier. The boy stood the rim at the last of the light, watching the lanterns walk their watch, and inside his chest, in the closed account of his body, the two hands turned toward each other.

They had been turning since the sand-spit of the stroke. The boy had felt it under everything all dark, the way you feel a tide under a hull — not seen, not heard, only the whole floor of you leaning by degrees — and now, with no term on the one and no keeping on the other, the leaning found its feet. The cold rose. It rose with nothing to keep, and it came up through the bone of him looking for work, and the boy's breath went out of him in smoke though the night was not cold. The warm rose. It rose with nothing to price, and it came up under his ribs looking for a term, and his heart ran like a spender's clerk at year-tithe, fast, spend-now, spend-all. Frost feathered the backs of his knuckles. Sweat ran the fronts of them. The body stood between its two weathers and shook, and the boy understood the finding, whole shape first, before the head had cloth for it, and the finding was simple and it was the worst of the season:

They had never been enemies. They had been trades. Two lines run taut through one mast, cold against warm, each holding the other's tension in the shape of a man — and he had cut the rigging himself, and it had been right, and now the lines were free, and free lines in a high wind find each other.

"Breathe," Marrow said, at the shoulder, the lamp steady, the three beats counted. "One spent. One owed."

The boy breathed. It was not enough. It had been enough at the turn, with the coast holding its breath beside him and the whole account yet to close. It was not enough now, alone in the ordinary dark, with nothing due and nothing owed and nothing aimed, and the frost climbed his wrist while his chest burned, and the Red — the Red, which priced everything, which would have bid at this, which had spent a season teaching him that every tearing is a door — the Red held its silence like a second frost, and the boy priced that silence at last, priced it true:

The quiet was not peace. The quiet was a spender at an auction who has already read the lot.

"Count." Asha-kel's voice, from the stair-head, flat, worn round by every weather in the world. "The houses felt it on the ninth landing. Two of the rim-hold felt it in their teeth. You are coming apart at the seams in front of the whole coast's window, and the houses enter that this is not a thing the flats can queue for." She came down the stair, and the flat stayed on, and under the flat — the boy heard it because his whole skin was ears now, everything priced, everything read — under the flat something else was walking. "The houses' price is a ruling. Mine is older. Sit down, count."

He did not sit. His legs were rigging and the rigging was humming. Asha-kel stopped at the spacing — three strides, the count's own distance, the distance the whole trade had kept all season — and then she crossed it. One stride. Two. Three, into the spacing no one had stood in, and she was smaller than the maker had been and warmer than the maker had been, and she took his iced hand off the rim-stone and put it flat against her own chest, over the cloth, over the heartbeat under the cloth.

"Count," she said. "Not the lamp's count. Mine."

The heartbeat came. Steady. Work-worn, aimed, a heart that had climbed nine tiers of stairs in its time and stood in every hard room the houses owned and kept its own time through all of it. The boy counted one beat, and the frost on his wrist held. Two beats, and the burning under his ribs banked itself, as a fire banks when the draft is set — not out, aimed. Three beats.

The cold in him turned toward the count. The boy felt it turn — felt the keeping-less cold, the unemployed frost, come around off its hunt for work and settle against the counted beats like a debt settling, because a keeping was a keeping, and here was a small kept thing, warm and regular, that wanted holding. And the warm in him turned too — turned off its spend-now, spend-all — and set itself to the same count, pricing it, terming it, beat against beat, because a term was a term, and here was a term that renewed itself without spending anybody.

Two weathers. One count. The body stopped shaking by degrees, as the frost was going over under the coat, at its own term, and the boy stood in the ordinary dark with his hand on the houses' flat heart and understood the entry before the flat voice made it:

*Entered: the rigging is not cut. It is re-rigged. The trade held them to their work. The work is done. What holds them now is not owed and is not priced and does not close.* The flat voice made the entry, and for the first time all season the entry was warm at the writing of it. *Entered: an anchor is a thing that does not hold the boat still. It holds the boat to the ground while the weather decides what it is. Entered: the ground is her heartbeat and I am the boat and the weather is coming, because Osk's packets are a hand, and hands that write addresses are writing them for a reason.*

"Entered," the boy said aloud, rough, one line, to the woman and to the coast and to the account that was closed and did not need him to say it and got it anyway. "One spent. One owed."

Asha-kel's face did what faces do when they are kept by keepers: nothing, exactly, one degree. "The houses will enter that the count was stable at the gray," she said, and put his hand back on the rim-stone, and went up the stair as she had come down it, unhurried, and the boy watched her go and priced the going at the old rate, and the old rate was: warm, and not for sale.

---

He slept. Or the body slept, which that dark was the same thing, and near the last of it, in the room under the room, the boy rose to do the checking.

The smile wall: spent. The voice wall: spent. The face, behind them, turning, dark eyes, gray, dark, his, and —

The boy went still.

The checking had already been done.

The walls stood checked. Priced. Entered. Both of them, smile and voice, spent and logged and carried, and the face behind them kept and turning, and the entries lay in the room under the room in a hand that was not the flat voice and was not the boy's knowing — a hand that had watched the thumb cross the knuckle-bars every dark of the season, dark by dark, as it watched everything, and had learned the walk off him, and tonight, in the boy's sleep, had walked the floor itself.

The Red said nothing.

It had not spoken since the turn. It did not speak now. It lay at the border, warm, quiet, keeping his mother's kept face in his own keeping-hand, and the quiet was the thing the boy carried up out of sleep and into the gray, and the gray came up over the water ordinary, wind and fog and the crackle of nine fires, and the boy stood the rim with his thumb pressed to the old gap in his teeth where the ink had come from, and the door was the whole world now, and somewhere past the far light, in an ink no keeper could name, the hand that wrote addresses was still writing.
