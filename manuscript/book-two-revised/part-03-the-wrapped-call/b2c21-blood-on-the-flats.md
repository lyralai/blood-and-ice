# Book Two, Chapter 21: Blood on the Flats

The two lows ran out at the first dark of the twenty-ninth low.

Nobody spoke it. The body knew it the way the body knew a tide turning — not in the head, in the soles, in the skin of the wrists, in the hair standing along the arms before any ear had reason. The bowl sat on the platform stone and the bowl was not heavier than blood. The frost lay thick on the fifth gate's bars. The rim-stones had drunk and asked and drunk and asked, and the asking had not stopped, and above the sump's ceiling the city had done its sealed arithmetic in the old clay, and the sum had come down.

The stair-feet told it first. The watch changed wrong. The boots that came down to the third gallery at first dark were not the watch's boots going up; they were the watch's boots staying, and the staying had weight in it, iron slung low, rim-cloth moving in the dark the way rim-cloth moved when it had been told where it was going.

He stood on the platform stone with the tablet under his hand and listened to the city through the stone the way he had once listened to the green through the soles of his feet. The green had always told him what was coming before it came. The city was the green's child in this one thing. It, too, told you at the bottom of the feet first.

The Skell came out of the ninth cage and stood at his shoulder without being asked. The amber eyes went up the dark of the shaft, tier over tier, and came down again, and the Skell's shoulders did the thing they did before a feeding — dropped and widened, the body making itself low and hard, a shape that had survived by reading what the amber eyes read.

"Stairs," the Skell said. Not a question.

"Stairs," the boy said.

---

Lorren came down at the second dark, and behind her the clerk, and the clerk had never come to the sump at second dark in the whole of the count, and the coming was the message before the mouth opened it.

The clerk set no tablet on the stone. That was the second message. The clerk's hands were empty, and the thin body stood in the tallow light with the shelving eyes and gave the report the way the hook-chief gave the wall's — flat, believing itself.

"The courts shelfed a sealed count at the twenty-seventh low. It came down tonight. A warrant of correction. The sump's floor is named a disordered line. The watch is named the instrument. The masters' gallery holds the bond for the cost, and the bond has been sold, and the buyers are the arena-masters' men, and the men have hired tide-runners to the count of three boats." The dry voice did not hurry. A voice built for columns, reading a column. "The warrant says the disorder is corrected where it stands. It does not say your name. It does not have to. The city has learned your name from both ends, and the sealed clay carries it under the number."

"The hook-chief," the boy said.

"Stands the watch. The warrant makes the watch the instrument, and the hook-chief is the watch's, and the hook-chief serves the heavier answer, and the courts have made theirs the heavier." The clerk paused, and the pause was the closest thing to an apology the dry voice had in it. "He asked me to carry one thing down that is not in the warrant. He said: the gates will be loud. He said: a wall-man feints with the wall's voice, because that is the only loudness a wall-man has."

The boy read it whole. Lorren stood behind the clerk with the fasting sign worn into her wrist, and she had not come down for doctrine and she had not come down for the bled; she had come down because the temple had gone quiet, and the temple's quiet was the third message. The clean knife did not come to a breaking. The clean knife sat above the ceiling and let the breaking do its work, and the grief would come down after, when there were bodies to grieve over and a count to blame for them. Vessa did not need to spend a healer on this. The warrant was her rite, written in other hands.

A feint at the gates. He set it beside everything the stairs had taught him — the third gallery, the fourth gate, the fifth gate with its frost, the crawl-space stairs where the bowl had gone up tier by tier. The watch's loudness, hooked iron on the stairs, bodies the bled knew by their boot-beats, pressing gates that could be held by fifty sworn who knew the landings.

And under the loudness, quiet. Three boats of tide-runners. And a drowned coast, and a shaft that drank the sea twice a low at the bottom, and a drowned gate at the sump's seaward end that the tide worked open and shut, open and shut, and at dead-low breathed out a flat of pale sand and black mud wide as the sump itself.

He had walked the flat at dead-low once, in the first weeks, and read it the way he read all ground: the tide-line of wrack, the pools, the one road of firm sand through the mud, narrow, funneled between two shoulders of rock — the only ground a boat's worth of armed men could cross at speed.

Where the sump was dark, blood was black. On the pale flat, blood showed.

"They will not come down the stairs to you," the boy said. "They will come across the flat at dead-low, through the drowned gate. The gates are the loudness. The flat is the blow."

The clerk's shelving eyes measured him. "The warrant does not say it."

"The hook-chief said it," the boy said. "A wall-man feints with the wall's voice."

---

The count became an order of battle in the low's dark, and the becoming was the strangest work the count had yet done — every tool of the feeding turned to a tool of the holding.

He set it out on the platform stone the way he set out the troughs at a feeding, and the sworn stood in the circle, one hundred and forty-one, and the bled stood behind them at the edge of the tallow light, and Kade's brightness stood among the freed-but-owing with the wrapped hands and the heat coming off him like a banked thing breathing.

"The Skell holds the fourth gate landing with thirty," the boy said. "The five hold the fifth with twenty. Hooks take the stair-feet; the landings are ours; the watch is loud and the watch is honest and the watch will press and not break, because the hook-chief is hers and the hook-chief obeys the heavier answer, and the heavier answer is the warrant, and the warrant says correct, not slaughter." He said it flat and he said it aloud, because the count's law was speak-what-is, and the sworn needed the shape of the enemy's honesty to stand against the loudness without hating it. "Nobody chases. Nobody follows a broken stair press down or up. The gates only have to be gates until the tide turns."

"And the flat?" the Skell said.

"The flat is mine," the boy said. "Sixty of the sworn on the two rock shoulders, slings and stones, out of the mud-light. The firm road between them is the only ground that carries speed, and speed is all a tide-runner has bought. I will stand on the road."

He did not say what would stand on the road with him. The circle heard it anyway. The tallow light moved on one hundred and forty-one faces, and not one of them asked.

Kade pushed half a step out of the freed-but-owing. The brightness had a hunger in it that the low's whole dark could not feed. "The seventh's master runs with the arena-men," Kade said. The voice was low and even and wrong — the evenness of a body that had counted its own floor-cuts and kept the number. "He signed the schedule. Forty-three. He weighed the cuts himself, each low, against the yield. He is in the bond's column. He will be on the flat or at its edge, pricing."

"I know," the boy said.

"He is mine," Kade said.

"He is nobody's," the boy said, and the words came out with the crown's weight in them, flat, older than the low. "You hold the right shoulder with the slings. You do not leave the shoulder. Whatever crosses the road, whatever the road does, whatever you see — the shoulder holds, and the holding is yours, and the seventh's master is a column in a count, not a debt in your hands. The count does not drop what it carries. That is the whole law. Say it back."

Kade's brightness flickered. Devotion rebuilding a refusal into a rite — he could see it happen, the eyes taking the order and re-cutting it into faith, the way Kade had rebuilt every standing he had ever been given. "The shoulder holds," Kade said. "The holding is mine."

"And the seventh's master?"

"Yours," Kade said, and the word cost him a cut somewhere the tallow light could not reach.

The boy set the answer on the shelf with the season's sums, and the shelf cut it a notch and gave nothing back.

---

He went to the cages before the dead-low.

Marrow's hand was already out through the bars. The curled fingers found his wrist and closed, and the hold was the old hold — no numbers, no asking, the runt's oldest answer, *still here* — and under it, new, the pressure that counted what the holding cost.

"You will stand on the road," Marrow said. Not a question. The runt had read the circle's silence from inside the cage, the way he read everything, off bodies, off the moving of feet, off the tallow burning down faster than the sump's breath explained.

"On the road."

"Halfway," Marrow said.

The hand tightened once. Not the counting press. The other one. The grip of a body that had held a thing through the cage and the green and the tribe and the pit and the crossing and the hold and the crown, and knew, tonight, what it was holding the door against.

"Listen," Marrow said, and the voice was low and level and had the runt's whole weight in it. "The thing wins fights. It has never once won the morning after. Every time it comes up, it wins, and the morning after is bigger than the time before. You count what everything costs. Count the morning after first. Before the door. Not after."

He crouched by the cage in the tallow light with the runt's hand on his wrist and the crown's sinew thin-cutting both temples, and he counted the morning after first, and the counting did not help, and he did it anyway, because that was the whole of what he was now: the body that counted the morning after first.

"The morning after has a column in it with no number yet," he said. "I have seen it since the twenty-sixth low. It is waiting for tonight to fill it."

Marrow's thumb moved once over the pulse it could not reach and had never needed to. "Then fill it standing up," Marrow said. "Whatever crosses the road — fill it standing up. The bled have to watch you choose it. Not watch the thing choose. Watch you."

The hand let go of his wrist at the first sound of the gates.

---

The loudness came at the third dark-watch, and it was everything the hook-chief's voice had promised.

Torches went up the shaft tier by tier, a climbing of orange that made the frost on the fifth's bars shine like the rim-stones' teeth. Iron came down the stairs in the rhythm of men who had been told where they were going, and the fourth gate landing took the first press, and the press was real — hooked iron on shields of lashed timber, the Skell's thirty giving ground a hand's width at a time and not a finger more, the landings running with the wet dark of the sump's breath and the first blood going black on the stone where you could not see it.

He heard it all from the flat and did not turn to it. The loudness was the wall's voice. The wall's voice was honest. The blow was in the quiet, and the quiet was coming in on the tide's last foot of water.

Dead-low came like the green's dawn used to come: not light, a graying, a letting-go. The drowned gate breathed out. The water sheeted off the shoulders of rock and the flat came up out of the sea a piece at a time — pale sand, black mud, the wrack-line, the pools going still — and the firm road between the shoulders lay open to the gate's dark throat like a laid cord.

The tide-runners came out of the throat at the graying's edge, and they were good, and the goodness was the horror of it. They were counted bodies — hired flesh, rim-cloth gone gray with salt, men who ran the drowned coast for the arena-boats and paid their own shares of blood at the rim-stones each season like every line the city owned. The masters had not spent their own on this crossing. They had bought the owned to break the owned. The front rank of the blow was itself a shorted share with hooks.

The Red came up off the floor of him before he asked it, and he caught it at the half-door, and the crown bit.

The sinew cut. Both temples, the thin lines running warm, the three knots' law made physical, the wearer bleeding first. The pain was a hand on a rope and he held the rope and the door stood at half, and through the half-door the flat changed.

Blood showed.

Every pool, every wrack-line, the dark at the gate's throat — the flat lay open to him like the tablet, like the opened books, like a column waiting to be read. He could see the price of every body on the road. The front rank's iron was tired iron, poorly kept, a season from the melting-pot. The second rank's footing was the wrong footing, mud-weight in the boot-cuts. The man at the column's head had a fused knee that the cold had been working on for ten seasons. He did not want to know it. The knowing came anyway, poured in through the half-door, and the wanting and the not-wanting were both his, and he could not always tell which one was the older.

*First,* said the thing behind the door. Not a push. A word. The hinge-word, offered now, on the flat, in the graying, patient as arithmetic. *The stairs are loud. This is quiet. The count holds gates. The first holds flats. Later for the city. First for this.*

He let it be first.

He went down off the rock shoulder onto the road, and the fight that followed was won before the tide-runners understood what they had met, and the winning was the worst thing the season had shown him, because he was there for all of it. The old risings had been storms — the body taken, the world gone red, the boy returned to a field of finished sums. The half-door was not a storm. The half-door was a clarity. He stood inside the choosing and watched himself choose, blow by blow, and every blow was clean.

He chose the fused knee and the tired iron, not the throats. He chose the front rank's shoulders and wrists, the maimings that make a hook-hand into a none-hand, and the choosing was merciful and the mercy was a horror, because he could feel the arithmetic of it — how few he killed, how many he merely ended as fighting men, how exactly the ledger balanced mercy against use — and the arithmetic was not the pit's red arithmetic. It was the count's. The thing had learned the count so well that even its killing came back as administration.

The Red laughed. Low, in him, through him — not sound on the air, sound in the blood, and the sound was joy, and the joy was clean.

The column broke. It broke the way a shorted line breaks — from the middle first, where the bought men were, the ones with shares to lose; the arena-masters' own bond-men at the rear held longest and went down hardest, because they were spending the masters' name and knew what going back empty cost. He read that too. Even the enemy's holding was a count. Everyone in the city was owned, and everyone's courage was the shape of what owned them, and the thing behind the half-door read it all with him and turned page after page and said, at the end, in the tone of a body closing a ledger it had found in good order: *See what the choosing does, when the choosing is not flinched from. You built this. I am only holding the door.*

On the tier's edge above the flat, far up in the failing dark, a figure stood where the houses stood, and the figure's hands held a thing wrapped in oiled sinew, unwrapped at one end, held low along the stone. Asha-kel. Come down from the alcove. The leash, carried to the wall of its own accord, shown to the whole waking sump without being asked, without being needed. He saw it through the half-door's reach and the seeing went into him with the rest: the houses had watched the half-door open, and the houses had put a hand on the kill-switch, and the hand had stayed in its cloth.

The leash had not been needed. That was the morning-after column, filling.

---

And then the shoulder emptied.

Afterward he rebuilt it in the still place a hundred times, and it was always the same shape. A body came off the mud at the column's ragged rear, awkward in the run, half-falling in the boot-cuts — a gallery-body, soft with floors, an old man young — and the graying light took the face, and from the right shoulder a brightness went off like a signal-fire.

Kade.

He heard his own order from across the flat — *the shoulder holds* — arrive where Kade had been standing, and find the standing empty. Kade went down the shoulder through the sling-line and the line bent after him, because the freed-but-owing followed brightness the way water follows a crack, and the right shoulder opened a gap the width of three bodies at the road's narrowest stone, and the column's rear-guard saw the gap with the desperate reading of men who had just watched their front become arithmetic.

They went through it. Bond-men, spending the masters' name, four of them, into the gap, up onto the shoulder among the slings, and the slings were distance-tools in a hand's-breadth fight, and the shoulder that had been the trap's jaw became the meat.

He crossed the flat at a speed that left prints cracked in the mud, and the half-door's clarity came with him, and it was worth nothing. Clarity reads; it does not mend. He took the first bond-man through the gap and put him down without choosing — the choosing gone, the storm's old red at the edges of the half, the thing behind the door rising to full voice, *First, first, let it be all the way first* — and he held the half. He held it with the crown biting and the sinew in the meat of his temples and Marrow's voice in the counting-place: *the morning after, the morning after.*

He held the half, and the cost of the holding was the seconds, and the seconds were where the bled died.

Two. It came to two, and each one was a column.

One of the five — the one who had survived the temple's rites and could not stop counting the cost, the one whose flat patience had measured every body the tiers had priced — opened by a bond-man's hook under the chin, going down in the mud with the counting face still on, and it did not have time to change, and that was the whole of the death: it finished mid-count.

And a young one of the freed-but-owing, a wrong-blood from the working tier, six seasons in the city, who had sworn under the count two lows after the cords came down — opened across the chest by the second bond-man, and the boy was there before the body finished falling, and the bond-man looked at what stood over him in the half-door's gray and set his own hook down in the mud and knelt, and the kneeling did not matter and the boy knew it did not matter and he did not kill him. The mercy was arithmetic. He could feel it being arithmetic. He filed the kneeling man with the maimed and moved on because the ledger said move on, and the ledger's voice and his voice were one voice at the half-door, and the not-telling was the door.

The last of it went into the mud and the flat went still.

Kade stood thirty paces up the shoulder with the seventh's master under his hands, and the kill was finished. It had been finished before the gap opened — the old man had run with the column's rear the way soft men run, badly, and Kade had crossed the flat's edge like a thrown spear, and the master had died on the sand between the road and the wrack-line with his scheduler's hands up, and Kade had cut him down through the hands, forty-three's worth, and the forty-three had not been counted aloud and the killing had not been quick.

Kade stood up out of the kill and turned to find the boy's eyes across the sand where the blood showed, and the brightness in him was total, and under the brightness, arriving late, second by second, the way light arrives from a burning thing after the burning — the seeing of the gap. The three-body gap. The counting face in the mud. The young one opened across the chest with the oath not six lows old.

Kade came and knelt down in the blood-showing sand between his kill and his dead, and knelt was the wrong word for it: the body arrived at kneeling the way a wave arrives at a shore, having no other shape left.

"Permission," Kade said. The voice was the seventh cage's voice, low, counted, even. "After. I know it is after. I ask it after."

*Followers spend your name and ask permission after.* The sentence formed in him whole, and he could not tell which mouth had built it, and the half-door stood open a hand's width still, and through it the thing looked at Kade kneeling in the sand and offered, gently, reasonably, in the count's own grammar: *A column for a column. He broke the shoulder. The shoulder cost two. The arithmetic is clean. Spoken aloud. Witnessed.*

The arithmetic was clean. That was the horror. He stood on the pale road at dead-low with the tide beginning its long turn behind him and the blood of sixty men showing on the sand like a column written in red where the whole sump could read it, and the cleanest sentence in the city was kneeling at his feet asking for itself.

Above, on the tier's edge, Asha-kel's wrapped hand had not moved. The gates had gone quiet — the hook-chief's loudness spent, the wall's honest feint folded away, the warrant half-executed and lying in the mud. The bled were coming down onto the flat in the graying light, and their faces did the sum the way he had taught them, and the sum was the same in every pair of eyes, and the sum was not grief and it was not fear. It was the reading he had seen first in the throat's galleries and had been running from since: *like the answer. Like the largest thing that will let them come near.*

The thing had won the fight. The thing was winning it still, quietly, at the half-door, in the count's own grammar.

The morning after stood up out of the flat with the tide, and it was his, and it did not flinch, and neither did he.

He set his hand over the gap where the three teeth were gone, and the cold behind the ribs did not stop for the palm, and the choosing waited for the light.

*End of Chapter 21*
