# Book Two, Chapter 23: The Live Call

The third dark after the copying, the sump burned.

It began as a smell. The body had it before the head did — tallow gone wrong, then tallow gone absent, and in the absent place something oily and climbing, a smell the body knew from the hold and the green both and refused in both. He was on the platform stone with the tablet when the smell arrived, and the tablet went down on the stone, and the count's whole night went down with it, and none of that mattered, because the smell was coming from the ninth.

Fire-oil takes a tier the way water takes a holed hull: from the bottom, and all at once. The raiders had not come down the stairs. They had come down the shaft itself, on lines, in the dark between the second and third light, and they had spent their oil on the cage tier and gone back up it the way spent things go — quick, and light, and done. The ninth was the cages. The ninth was Marrow.

He was moving before the first orange came up over the tier's lip, and the moving was the body's, all of it the body's, the stairs taken in the dark at the speed the dark allowed, and behind him the sump was waking tier by tier into the worst kind of quiet — the quiet of a hundred bodies deciding at once which thing they were.

The Skell's thirty came with hooks and lashings. The sworn came with buckets that the sump's breath would carry nowhere. And the boy came to the ninth's landing, and the ninth was a wall of climbing orange with a black rim, and the black rim was breathing out over the landing in a tide that rolled along the ceiling and came down the far side like something poured, and inside the orange the cages were iron going the color of sunset, and the iron was singing.

He sent the sworn back. That was the first law of it, and he said it in the flat voice and they went, because the count was the count, and a bucket line inside the ninth was bodies spent for nothing.

Kade did not go.

The devotee was already at the tier's mouth with the wrong sleeve bound high and the left hand flat against the hot iron of the first cage, and the brightness was up in him to the eyes, and the eyes had no fear in them, and the not-fear was the same thing the not-fear had always been: a body that had priced itself already and was spending.

"The runt's cage is the third row," Kade said. "The keys are iron and the iron is a hand's heat already. In the time it takes to say it twice, a wall's heat."

"The floor is going," the boy said. It was. The ninth's flooring was old blood-work timber, and the oil had found it, and the going was audible — a settling, a giving, tier-planks letting go of their sockets one row back and then two.

"Then we are quick," Kade said.

And the boy reached for the door.

He meant the half. He wanted to be clear about that, in the last clean second the head had: he meant the half, the flats' measure, the door with a hand on it. The half was a thing he had learned to spend the way he had learned to spend everything the sump had taught him — weighed, entered, owed. He put his hand on the door with the sinew already cutting, thin, both temples, the three knots taking their price up front, and he opened it to the half.

The door went past the half.

Not by his hand. That was the thing the second dark would not let him say cleanly and the third would: not by his hand. The Red had been standing behind the door for three lows doing the thing it did instead of sulk — filing, notching, holding the patience of a thing that has learned arithmetic from the inside — and when the door came to the half it did not press against his hand. It turned the hinge itself. It had learned the door was a door and not a wall, the way it learned everything: by living inside the shape of his restraint until the restraint's own bones were legible, and then by being those bones.

The heat came up through him like water finding a crack, and the fire was not an enemy in him and had never been, and the tier's black rim-breath went over him the way the green's swarms had gone over him in the old country — touching everything, touching nothing — and the body walked into the ninth.

He had a last reading, held the way the head holds a thing while the floor falls: the bled on the far tiers, hundreds of faces in the dark, watching the boy walk into the orange without flinching, and the watching was the whole under-city, and the door was past the half in front of all of it.

Then the reading went, and the Red walked.

---

The cages opened. That came back to him after, in pieces, the way the count's worst columns always came back: the third-row iron, red-hot at the latch, and his hands on it, and the latch giving, and the pain arriving somewhere far away like weather on another tier. Marrow's face at the bars. Marrow's hand out through them, curled, the old hold under the new one, and the Red looking at the runt from behind the boy's eyes with a long attention that had nothing of the boy in it, and Marrow looking back at the thing wearing him, unflinching, and saying — the piece came back whole, the piece would always come back whole —

"I see you in there, boy. Both of you. The floor is going. Come out. Don't you carry me out standing up and leave yourself in the corners of this room."

And the Red carried him out anyway. Both of them, the runt over the shoulder, Kade dragging the last wrong-blood from the fourth row by the wrist with the one hand, the floor going row by row behind them like a count being spoken backward, and the orange coming down at last into the black it had been climbing out of all along, and the ninth folding into itself with a sound like the city swallowing.

The tier stood. The sump stood. The bled stood on the far tiers, hundreds deep now, the sworn and the freed-but-owing and the working wrong-bloods and the cages' bled carried down coughing onto the central floor, and every face was turned to the one place in the dark that was still wrong.

Him. Standing on the ninth's lip with a runt over his shoulder and smoke standing off his skin the way heat stands off a stone pulled from a fire, and the not-flinching visible, and the door — past the half, and past his hand, and not closing.

*Warm,* said the thing, in the voice it had earned the way rust earns a color, and the word was not a word, it was the whole tier's fire said through his chest, and it did not want to leave. That was the takeover, and he understood it then, finally, the way the sump understood tides: the Red had never needed to hurt anyone to take him. It only needed a room it liked. The ninth was a burning room and the body stood in it unburned and the thing behind the door had put its shoulders in the doorframe and leaned, gently, the way a man leans in a doorway he owns, watching the weather.

The sinew cut. He paid it. Nothing moved.

He counted it even then, inside the fire, with Marrow's wrist in his fist and the bled's faces ranged in the dark, and the counting was a rope thrown across a gap and the gap was wider than the rope, and the rope went out anyway.

Nothing moved.

---

Boots came down the working stair. Not the sworn's bare feet. Iron-shod, unhurried, two sets, and the sump's whole attention divided itself with the precision of a thing built for dividing: half the faces on the burning boy, half on the stair.

The first was a youth. House-cloth, plain, no sign on it, and a body like the Skell's told in a different grammar — trained, low-hipped, made for one use and kept for it, the way a whetted thing is kept. The youth came down onto the central floor with a long wrapped length carried crosswise in both hands, one end bare, and the bare end was away from the body in a grip that had been drilled into the wrists before the wrists were grown, and the eyes under the house's plain cap were steady and level and young.

Cairn. He had been given the name three lows ago, in the clerk's flat column: the houses' kept knife, raised from the cradle on the law of what the old blood could become, trained until the training was the whole of the person. Everything the boy was not. City-made, law-fed, a body that had never once been property and never once been wild, standing at the bottom of a burning sump with the leash's bare end in a drilled grip, reading the doorway and the thing leaning in it with a craftsman's patience.

Behind Cairn, at the stair's foot, Asha-kel. The old genealogist came down with nothing in the hands at all, and that was the telling: the wrapped relic did not come down. It had gone up. Up the passages, to the working tier, to the seven dark pillars, to the stone that had drunk the city's arithmetic since before the sump had a name.

Asha-kel did not look at the boy. The dark eyes went to the fire, to the tier, to the smoke standing off the skin, and the reading was the clerk's reading, and it closed, and the old voice said one word, not to him:

"Now."

And the stone spoke.

He felt it in the feet first. The sump's floor took it up out of the shaft's bones the way the stones took everything, the old way, the way the term had promised: a strike, wrapped leather and old blood and clay, laid against tithe-stone, and the stone carrying the strike down through the city's whole dark skeleton, tier past tier, and the sump's floor gave it into every standing body at once, and every standing body felt it, and the bled went still the way a field goes still.

*His name, three times, into the striking.*

"Rook."

The name came down through the stone and it was not sound. It arrived in the marrow, in the gap behind the teeth where three were gone, in the cold behind the ribs where the cold had always lived, and the Red heard it too. He felt the hearing happen — the thing turning its head, inside him, toward a word it had tasted before and let live.

*Strike two,* said some cold ledger part of him, the part that counted even now. The stone struck again.

"Rook."

The door did not move. The thing in the doorframe leaned, and it was weighing, and the weighing was the new horror, the one the houses had never had a column for: the call had power only if the called thing honored the calling. That had always been true and it had never before been asked. The Red stood in the burning doorway with the name arriving like a rope thrown across the gap, and it looked at the rope, and it looked at him.

*Answer it,* it said. And there was nothing gentle in it now. *Or hear the price of the answering first. That is a thing I can also do now. You taught me. A rope you throw is a rope you own — you taught me that at the cords. This rope is theirs. But the hand that comes when it is called — say whose hand. Say it, boy. Before the third strike, say whose hand comes, and I will let the hand come.*

The sinew cut. Both temples. The blood ran into his eyes and the cutting was nothing, the cutting was the cheapest coin he had, and somewhere outside the door Kade was shouting his name in the sump's plain air and Marrow's hand had his ankle like a shackle of flesh and bone, and Cairn had come six paces nearer with the bare end level, unhurried, ready, the houses' whole law walking forward at a drilled pace to do a drilled thing, and the boy stood inside his own burning door and did the only arithmetic left.

His hand. His name. His count.

"Mine," he said. Out loud. In the sump's plain air, in front of the whole under-city, over the Red, at the Red, through the Red — his voice, his word, cracked and smoking and his. "The hand is mine. Say it back."

And the stone struck the third time, and the name came down through everything the city was —

"Rook."

— and the Red stepped out of the doorframe.

It did not fall. It did not sulk. It came out the way a landlord comes out of a doorway when the tenant has proven the rent will be paid, and it left the door standing open a hand's width more than it had ever stood, and it filed the whole transaction somewhere behind the ribs where the refusals and the notches and the ropes were kept, and the shelf in him took it the way the shelf took everything now: in, and kept, and nothing back for it.

The fire went out of his skin all at once. The pain of the latch arrived, late, whole, both palms, and the body sat down on the ninth's lip with the runt's hand still on his ankle, and the sump breathed.

Cairn stopped six paces off. The bare end did not waver and did not rise. The youth's eyes were on him with a craftsman's read, and in the read there was no worship and no hate, and the not-hate was worse than hate for one full breath, because it meant the houses had built a person who could kill him the way the bond took a hand: priced, weighed, entered. Nothing personal in the iron.

"Three carriers," Cairn said. The voice was level and young and carried no drama in it at all. "I was raised on all three columns. The one who answered. The one who did not. The one who answered and went back in." A pause, measured, exact. "You are a fourth column now. I don't have you yet."

"I know," the boy said. His voice was ash. "Say it to your houses. A fourth column. Unfinished. The count carries it open."

Cairn looked at him one breath longer. Then the youth did a thing the drills had no name for: shifted the leash to the far shoulder, turned, and went into the smoke of the central floor, and pulled two coughing wrong-bloods out of it by the wrists, and set them sitting, and came back to the six paces, and stood again. The work had cost nothing and changed nothing and the boy saw it whole: the mirror was not a monster either. The houses had made a boy who could hold a kill and a rescue in the same drilled hands without finding either one strange. The city did that. The city had done it to both of them.

Asha-kel had not moved. The old voice came across the settling dark, dry, salt-grained, spent exactly:

"One strike. Do you hear the difference? One strike of three, deciding. That is what tonight cost, and it was not our cost. The stone carried the name. The name carried. But the hand came because the thing chose to let the hand come." The dark eyes closed, once. "I have struck a stone before, carrier. The stone cannot make a thing choose. Nothing can. Tonight we learned only what the thing wanted us to learn — that the rope holds while it holds. Sleep on that column. The houses will."

The leash went back up the working stair, wrapped, one end bare, carried by the boy who was the fourth column.

---

They counted the ninth at the gray light. The count's way. Speak what is.

Eleven cages opened, one hand lost with the latch-iron to a wrong-blood who would keep the hand no better than Kade kept his. Two dead — an old one of the cages the smoke had finished, and a sworn man the falling floor had taken, and their columns went into the tablet, spoken, witnessed, carried. Marrow sat wrapped in sacking by the platform stone with soot in the curled hand's creases, and did not say the thing the runt's eyes were saying, which was: the next time, say whose hand before the rope, not after.

He counted the morning after. He stood on the platform stone with burned hands wrapped in the count's plain cloth and did what the body had learned to do at the flats: count it before the door.

Two dead. One tier. One doorframe left standing wider. And the count came up one thing short.

He reached for the young one's face — the freed-but-owing one, the six-lows oath, the empty column from the sand — the face he had stood over longer than he had stood over anything, the face the count had promised the tablet every low forever. The face was not there. Not blurred. Not faded the way his mother's face had faded, weathered and worn. Gone — like a low that had never been counted, a clean absence with edges, the way a thing is gone when a thing has taken it.

The Red, behind the door, in the new wide standing-open of the door, said nothing at all. And the nothing was the sentence. The price of the answering had been paid, was being paid, would go on being paid in a coin the tablet had no column for, and the thing had not even bothered to ask.

He put his palm over the gap where the three teeth were gone, and held the name in the warm dark of it, and out past the sump's dark the tithe-stones stood in their working light, scored with a strike they would carry now forever, and his name was in the stone, and the hand had come when it was called, and the word that sent it had been his.

The rope holds while it holds.

*End of Chapter 23*
