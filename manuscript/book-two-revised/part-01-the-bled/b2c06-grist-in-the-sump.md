# Book Two, Chapter 6: Grist in the Sump

The exhibit tier had a door the guards did not use.

He found it the way he found all things — by the body, by the shift of air that said *this space goes somewhere else.* The room the city had given him held the window, the platform, the water-jug. The room was the city's architecture. But the wall behind the platform was not flat. The stone changed color at the floor — a darker cut, the seam older, the mortar crumbled to sand in the join. The wall had been built against something, and the something was a passage.

He did not go through on the first low. Or the second. He counted the guards' rounds — the shift-change, the water-jug's slot, the clerk's visit — and between the last clerk's leaving and the first guard's return lay a gap. The gap was narrow. His body could fit through a gap that narrow. The question was what waited on the other side.

On the third low, he pressed the stone. The stone moved — not on hinges, not on a track. On weight. It tilted inward at the base, and the opening was a crawl-space, low enough that his shoulders scraped the sides. The air that came through was different. Wetter. Colder. The smell of the sumps.

He went.

---

The passage descended. Not stairs — a rough-cut slope, the stone slippery with moisture, the walls narrowing until his hands touched both sides and the ceiling brushed his scalp. The tallow-smell of the upper tiers thinned. What replaced it was the smell he knew from the hold — the bilge-smell, the under-smell, the reek of water that had been sitting in stone for longer than the city had been counting.

The passage opened onto a gallery. He went to his knee at the edge and read.

Below the exhibit tier. Below the cells. Below the Mure-cages he had walked past on the first walking-through, the tubes and the clay jars and the slick-dry gray skin. This was the bottom. The sump-floor. The place the tall one had named in the bleeding-books: *the finite things that run dry.*

Cages. Rows of them, the iron rougher than the upper tiers, the bars set close, the floors layered with straw gone to black muck. Some were empty. Some held shapes. The boy's eyes adjusted to the dark — the only light came from tallow-candles set in wall-niches, the flames low and smoky, the light the color of old bone.

The shapes were not all human.

His root read the floor before his eyes sorted the cages. The vibration came up through his knees — the deep-layer hum of the old blood in the stone, the same frequency the throat's floor had carried. But here the hum was different. Thicker. Layered with things the upper floor did not carry. The root tasted the layers the way it had tasted the throat's earth — *below, same rung, above, unknown* — and the tasting was crowded. Many kinds. Many bloods. All of them soaked into the same stone, all of them feeding the same hum.

His eyes sorted the cages.

The first row held men. The boy's own shape — upright, bare, the skin going gray in the cold. Some standing. Some sitting. One lying in the muck with the stillness the boy recognized from the hold, the stillness of a body that had decided to stop. Tubes ran from the arms of two of them to clay jars on the floor. The jars were a quarter-full. The men with the tubes were the ones sitting. The standing men had no tubes — wrong-bloods whose yield had dropped below the price of the needle. The standing men were waiting to be thrown to the throat. The sitting men were still paying.

The second row held the island things.

The boy's body went cold. Not the Red's cold — the cold of recognition, the same cold that had gone through him in the upper tier when the Mure's cage had passed and the root had said *same rung.* The cold of seeing a thing from home in a place that was not home.

A Mure. Two of them, in two cages, the same cages the boy had read on the walking-through with the tall one. But down here the cages were smaller. The Mure in the first cage could not stand. The wide mouth was open, the jaw slack, the eyes — the eyes that had seen tide-water — dry and flat. The slick skin had gone to cracking. The web-fingered hands lay palm-up on the muck, and the tubes ran from the inner elbows to the clay jars. The jars were half-full. The Mure was the horror the boy had read in the upper tier — the Beast rung, the Red gone all the way, the thing the boy could become — and down here, at the bottom, in a cage too small to stand in, the horror was not abstract. The horror was the body in front of him.

The second Mure was smaller. The boy read it — her — the way the green had taught him to read shape and weight and condition. Female. The body thinner than the first, the gray skin pulled tight over the wide ribs. The eyes were not dry. The eyes were open and moving, and the moving was the thing the boy read last: *she is still reading back.* The first Mure had stopped. The second Mure had not.

The boy filed the reading. *The one still reading is the one still alive.*

Past the Mure-cages: a Vorth. One — a male, young, the heavy shoulders folded inward because the cage would not let them spread. The dark matted hide was dull. The eyes were the eyes of a thing taken from the high stones and put in a hole. The Vorth's hands — the hands that had kept fire on the ridge, the hands that had scooped up small ones and held them against the warm — lay flat on the cage floor, the fingers slack. No tubes. The Vorth's yield had not been priced yet. The Vorth was new.

The boy's root went still. The stillness it went into when it read a thing it recognized — not *same rung* like the Mure, not *below* like the pit-thing. The root read the Vorth the way it read the tribe. *Above.* The Vorth was the rung above — the people, the keepers, the ones whose fire did not go out. The root said *above* and the saying was the thing the boy felt in the far hunger, the hunger that had been thinned but was not gone.

The far hunger stirred. Not the Red's lean — his own. The pull toward the high stones, toward the fire-tending, toward the sound thrown across the green that said *you are still ours, come back up.* It reached for the Vorth the way it had reached for the tribe's fire-light on the ridge. *As if belonging were a meal.*

The boy pressed the far hunger down. The lean was thinned. The lean was not gone. But this was not the ridge, and the Vorth in the cage was not the tribe. The Vorth was a thing taken, broken, put in a hole. The boy knew what a hole did to a thing that had kept fire.

---

Past the Vorth, past the last row of cages, against the far wall where the tallow-light did not reach: a shape the boy almost missed.

Low. Lean. The body curled tight in the corner of a cage that was too small for any of the island things — a cage built for a dog, or for a thing the city had decided was less than a dog. The shape did not move. The ribs went and came too fast. The matted fur — not hide, fur, the short coarse fur of a thing that lived on scree and kill-sites — was patchy. The patches were not disease. The patches were grind-marks. Places where the skin had been rubbed away by something external and the fur had grown back thin.

A Skell.

His root read the Skell and the reading was the one he had expected least. Not *below.* Not *same rung.* Not *above.* Not *unknown.* The root read the Skell and said nothing. It tasted the shape of it through the floor-vibration and the taste was — *grist.* The word he did not have for the thing the strong ground off the bone and left behind. The root had no rung for grist. Grist was the thing that fell between rungs. Grist was the thing the ladder was made of — the stuff ground down to build the thing that climbed.

The Skell's eyes opened. Not the slow opening of a thing waking. The fast opening of a thing that had been awake the whole time, that had been reading the boy the way the boy was reading it. The eyes were amber. Bright. The brightness was wrong in the sump — too alert, too alive, the brightness of a thing that survived by reading the order faster than the order could kill it.

The Skell's nostrils flared. The lean body shifted — not moving, *adjusting.* The adjustment was the thing the boy recognized: the Skell was reading the boy's smell the way the boy read the green's exits. The Skell was reading what the boy carried. The root, the wrongness, the warm, the brands, the disc at the throat. The Skell was doing what Skell did — reading the order to survive it.

The Skell's mouth opened. Not to speak — the Skell did not speak the hold-tongue, did not speak any tongue the boy had. The mouth shaped a sound. Low. Guttural. The sound a scavenger makes at the edge of a kill when a bigger thing arrives — not challenge, not submission. Recognition. The sound said: *I know what you are. I read you on the stones. You are the one who counted what the strong wasted.*

The boy went still.

The Skell had read him on the stones. Not these stones — the other stones. The island. The Skell carried the island in the way it shaped the sound, in the way the amber eyes tracked the boy's brands the way the tribe had tracked the brands. The Skell had been there. Not at the ridge — the Skell did not come to the ridge. At the margins. At the kill-sites. At the squaring-ground's edge, where the leavings fell. The Skell had read the boy from the edge of things, the way Skell always read — from the place where the strong did not look.

The boy crouched beside the cage. The Skell did not flinch. The amber eyes held.

"You," the boy said. The word was the hold-tongue but the sound was the sound the tribe used for *the leavings* — the low sound, the grist-sound, the sound that named a thing by what was left when the strong had finished.

The Skell's mouth closed. The amber eyes blinked once — the slow blink of a thing that has been read and knows it has been read and is deciding what the reading means. The Skell adjusted again — the lean body shifting in the corner, the patchy fur settling — and the shifting was the shifting of a thing that had made a decision. The Skell pressed itself against the bars closest to the boy. Not reaching. Pressing. The way a dog presses against a hand it has decided to trust, not because the hand has earned it but because the alternative is the muck.

---

The boy counted the cages on the sump-floor. Eleven. Six held men — the boy's own shape, the wrong-bloods whose blood did things the city wanted to price. Two held the Mure. One held the Vorth. One held the Skell. One was empty, the muck on the floor still shaped like the body that had been there — dragged out, taken up, spent on the throat's floor or bled to the last jar and discarded. The empty cage's muck-shape was small. A young one's shape. The boy did not look away from the shape. The boy read the shape the way he read all things — whole, steady, the body taking the reading in through the eyes and setting it in the still place where the things that mattered stayed.

A young one. In a cage. In the sump. Bled to the last jar.

The city's shelf. The boy had been shelved on the exhibit tier — the city's property, the thing the courts could not agree on. But the sump-floor was a different shelf. The sump-floor was the shelf where the things the city had *finished agreeing on* sat waiting to be spent. The boy's shelf held a history. The sump-floor's shelf held a body-count.

The Red stirred behind the breastbone. Not the press, not the lean. The Red's appetite-filing — the thing the Red did when it found a new category. The boy felt the Red file the sump-floor the way it had filed Asha-kel's three positions: *the sump under feeding. The empty cage under waste. The cages under store.* The Red was building its map of the city's architecture, and the map was organized by hunger, and the sump-floor was where the city kept its food.

The boy pressed the Red down. The door stayed open but the Red went behind it. The Red's map was not the boy's map. The boy's map was the still place's map — the count, the name, the terms with Asha-kel, the anchor-counted-breathing of Marrow somewhere above. The boy's map had room for the sump-floor. The boy's map did not organize by hunger.

But the Red had filed the sump-floor, and the filing was the thing the boy could not un-file. The Red had tasted the floor — the layers of blood, the many kinds, the crowded hum — and the taste had been *feeding* the way the throat's floor had been *feeding.* The sump-floor was an extension of the throat. A pantry. The place where the food was kept before the food was spent.

The boy filed the Red's filing. The cost: the Red had a map of the city now, and the map was appetite. The boy had a map of the city now, and the map was the count. The two maps covered the same ground. The two maps did not agree on what the ground was for.

---

The boy went back the way he had come — the slope, the crawl-space, the stone that tilted back into place behind him. The exhibit-tier room was the same. The window. The platform. The water-jug. The city had not noticed the boy's going and the boy's return. The gap in the guards' rounds was still the gap.

The boy sat on the platform. The still place took the sump-floor and set it beside the Asha-kel fragment and the three theories and the cost-ledger and the count and the name and the Red's appetite-map. The shelf was very full. The shelf had never been this full. The boy was carrying the city's architecture in the still place — the four powers, the tiers, the throat, the exhibit stone, the sump, and now the cages. The boy was carrying the city the way the green had carried him — whole, in the body, the weight of it in the bones.

The boy pressed the Red down. The Red went behind the door. The door stayed open.

But the Red had tasted the sump-floor, and the tasting had been interest, and the interest was the thing the boy filed beside the Asha-kel meeting and the *bled to order* and the appetite-shelves. The Red was building its room. The Red's room was larger today than yesterday. The Red's room had a pantry now.

---

The boy went back on the fifth low.

Not through the crawl-space — the boy had mapped the sump-floor's layout from the inside, and the inside said there was another way down. The exhibit tier had stairs. The stairs went up to the blood-courts and down to the working tiers, and the working tiers had access to the sumps through the bleeding-houses' back passages. The passages were the route the blood-jars took — carried up from the sump by the pit-guards, through the bleeding-houses, into the counting-rooms where the brokers priced what the jars held. The boy had watched the jars move on the first walking-through with the tall one. The boy had counted the route.

The route was guarded. The boy counted the guards — two at the exhibit-tier stair-foot, two at the bleeding-house door, one walking the back-passage with the slack step of a body doing a thing it had done a thousand times and no longer read. Five guards. The boy could not fight five guards. The boy did not need to fight five guards. The boy needed the gap between the walking-guard's rounds — the slack step, the thousand-times step, the step that did not look up.

The boy timed the gap. The gap was wide enough.

The bleeding-house passage was narrow, torch-lit, the walls sweating moisture. The smell was the iron-smell of blood — not the Red's iron taste, the literal iron of blood drawn and jarred and carried. Clay jars lined the passage walls, stacked on stone shelves, each jar stoppered with wax. Some were dark. Some were lighter — the brown-red of thin blood, the blood of a body running dry. The boy read the jars the way the tall one read the bleeding-books: each jar was a body, each color was a yield, each weight was a life measured by what it gave.

The passage opened onto the sump-floor through a door that did not lock. The sump was below the passage. The boy descended three stone steps and was on the floor.

The cages were the same. The shapes were the same. But the boy had come from a different direction, and the different direction gave him the floor's shape: the cages were arranged in a circle. Not a grid, not rows — a circle, the iron bars forming a ring around a central space. The central space was empty. The floor of the central space was the darkest stone the boy had seen — darker than the cage-muck, darker than the passage walls. The boy's root read it from the steps: the central floor was old blood. Not layers. Not history. A saturation — the stone was *full* of blood the way a sponge is full of water, and the fullness was not dry. The central floor was wet. The wet was blood, and the blood was fresh enough to smell, and the smell was the iron-smell that filled the sump.

The central floor was a smaller version of the throat's floor. The city had two floors: the big one — the throat, where the rites happened, where the blood went down in public — and this one. The small one. The private one. The sump-floor where the blood went down without an audience.

The boy understood. The throat was the spectacle. The sump was the *engine.* The throat fed the floor for the crowd. The sump fed the floor for the city. Every cage on the sump-floor was arranged around the central floor the way the tiers were arranged around the throat — the same architecture, smaller, quieter, and without the galleries. The sump was the throat without the show.

---

The Skell watched the boy cross the floor.

The amber eyes tracked him — not the tracking of a threat, not the tracking of food. The tracking of a thing that read the order and survived. The Skell had read the boy on the first visit. The Skell had made its decision. The Skell was waiting for the boy to do what the Skell had decided the boy would do, because the Skell had been reading the order longer than the boy had been in the city, and the reading said: *this one changes things.*

The boy crouched beside the Skell's cage. The Skell pressed against the bars. The amber eyes were close — close enough for the boy to see the film of sump-water over them, the slight clouding that said the Skell's body was taking on the wet it needed to survive. The Skell was adapting. The Skell was reading the order and adjusting, the way Skell always adjusted — at the margin, at the edge, at the place where the strong did not look.

"You read me," the boy said. Not a question. The boy's body knew what the amber eyes were doing.

The Skell made the low sound again. The guttural recognition-sound — but different this time. Longer. Shaped. The sound had a rhythm the boy recognized from the tribe — not the tribe's tongue, not the gentling or the still-sound. A simpler thing. The sound a scavenger makes when the kill has changed hands and the new hand lets the leavings fall.

The Skell was saying: *the kill has changed hands.*

The boy read the Skell's body the way he read all bodies. The grind-marks. The patchy fur. The ribs — the going-and-coming ribs, too fast, the breathing of a body that was burning itself. The Skell was dying. Not fast — the Skell did not die fast, the Skell died the way Skell lived, slowly, at the margin, the body eking out the last of what the strong had left. But dying. The clay jars beside the Skell's cage were nearly empty. The yield had dropped below the price of the needle. The Skell was in the last cage on the sump-floor — the cage built for a dog, the cage that said *this thing is not worth the iron of a bigger cage.* The Skell was next. The Skell was the empty-cage-shape the boy had read on the first visit, the small body pressed into the muck, and the Skell knew it.

The Skell knew it and the Skell was not afraid. The amber eyes held the boy's eyes the way the old male's eyes had held the boy's eyes on the ridge — the look of a thing that had read the order whole and was waiting for the order to finish doing what the order did. Not surrender. Comprehension. The Skell comprehended the sump-floor the way the boy comprehended the cage. The difference was the boy had survived his cage. The Skell was going to die in this one.

The boy's hand went to the bars. The Skell did not flinch. The boy's fingers wrapped around the iron — the iron that was warm, the iron that hummed with the central floor's frequency, the iron that carried the same vibration the boy's root carried. The boy's hand and the Skell's body were separated by the width of a bar. The warmth of the boy's skin and the warmth of the Skell's fur. Two warm things in the cold.

The Red pressed. Not hard — a nudge. The Red was interested in the Skell the way it had been interested in the Mure and the Vorth. The Red was building its map of the sump's stock. The Skell was *grist* — the thing between rungs — and the Red did not have a shelf for grist, and the not-having was the interest. The Red wanted to know what the Skell's blood tasted like. The Red wanted to file the Skell under its own hunger.

The boy pressed the Red back. Hard. The still place locked — name, count, the Kala's teaching, the terms with Asha-kel. The Red went behind the door. The boy's hand stayed on the iron.

---

"You are not nothing," the boy said to the Skell.

The words were the hold-tongue. The Skell did not speak the hold-tongue. But the boy's body said the words with the weight the tribe had given *the leavings* — the low sound, the grist-sound, the sound that named a thing by what was left. The boy's body said the words the way the Kala had said the boy's name in the dark. Not a command. A naming.

The Skell's eyes changed. The amber deepened. The clouding — the sump-water film — shifted, and for a moment the boy saw the thing the Skell had been before the cage. Lean, low, fast. A thing that ran the scree at the kill-site's edge. A thing that read the order of every kill it had ever fed on and survived. A thing whose survival was not strength but *reading* — the cold arithmetic of the discarded, the knowledge that the leavings always read the order first because the leavings were the things the order was going to kill next, and the only way to stay alive was to read the order faster than it killed.

The Skell was the boy. Not the same body, not the same shape, not the same rung. The same *condition.* The condition of being the thing the strong ground off the bone and left behind. The condition of surviving by reading. The condition of being valued by a power that counted what the strong wasted.

The boy had been the Skell on the island. The boy had been the grist in the cage. The boy had been the thing in the small cage at the edge of the sump, reading the order, surviving the order, waiting for the order to finish doing what the order did. The boy had survived because the green had burned him to the root and the root had been strong enough. The Skell was surviving because the Skell read the order and adjusted, and the adjusting was the only thing keeping the lean body breathing.

But the Skell was going to die. The yield had dropped. The jars were nearly empty. The cage was the last cage. The city did not keep things that did not yield.

The boy set his hand on the iron. The iron was warm. The Skell pressed against the bars. The boy counted the Skell's breathing — the going-and-coming ribs, the too-fast rhythm, the body burning itself.

The boy could not open the cage. The boy could not fight five guards. The boy could not carry a Skell up the slope and through the crawl-space and into the exhibit-tier room. The boy was the city's property. The boy was shelved. The boy's hands were tied by the courts' ruling and the four powers' claims and the Red's expanding room.

But the boy could count.

The boy counted the Skell. The Skell was the first of the bled. Not the first body on the sump-floor — the first body the boy *named.* The boy had named himself in the gallery (*I am Rook*), and the naming had been the thing the city could not price. The boy named the Skell now — not with a word, not with a sound. With the count. The boy set the Skell in the still place beside Marrow's breathing. The boy counted the Skell the way he counted Marrow: *you are here. You are real. The distance between us is real. I am counting the distance because the counting is how I know you are still there.*

The Skell's breathing slowed. Not much — a fraction, the ribs going and coming a half-beat slower. The Skell had felt the count. The way Marrow felt the still-sound across the cell-dark. The way the Kala had felt the boy's name spoken in the firelight. The Skell had felt the boy count it, and the feeling was the thing the sump could not take, because the sump took blood and yield and body-weight. The sump did not take the count. The count was the boy's own invention. The count was the human thing — the thing the Red could not eat because the Red did not understand counting.

---

The boy stood. His knees were wet from the sump-floor. His hands smelled of iron — the cage iron and the blood iron and the iron of the Red's taste behind the teeth. The boy looked at the circle of cages. The wrong-bloods in the first row, the tubes running to the jars. The Mure in the second row, the one still reading, the one stopped. The Vorth in the third, the folded shoulders, the dull hide, the fire-hands slack on the muck. The Skell in the last cage, the amber eyes, the grind-marks, the going-and-coming ribs.

The island was here. Not the green — the green was across the water, the green was the thing the boy had been sent to, the green that could not kill its own. But the island's things were here. The Vorth who kept fire. The Mure who were the warning. The Skell who were the leavings. All of them hauled across the water and caged in stone and bled to jars and priced by the yield and spent on the floor when the yield ran dry.

The far hunger came up off the floor of the boy. Not the Red's lean — the boy's own. The far hunger reached for the cages the way it had reached for the tribe's fire-light, hungering at it as at meat. But the reaching was different here. On the ridge, the reaching had been for the tribe's warmth, the tribe's sound, the tribe's keeping. Here, the reaching was for the discarded. The wrong-made. The things the city had finished with. The far hunger reached for the cages the way the boy's hand had reached for the Skell's bars — not to take but to *hold.* To hold the distance. To count the distance. To say *you are here and I am here and the distance is real and I am counting it.*

The far hunger had been thinned. The Red had taken the bite out of the belonging-need. But the count was not the far hunger. The count was the rope. The count was the thing the Red could not eat. And the count was expanding — not the appetite, not the Red's territorial filing. The *count.* The human thing. The thing that said *I see you, I name you, you are not nothing, you are not grist, you are counted.*

The boy counted the cages. Eleven. He counted the bodies. Ten. He counted the empty cage. One. He counted the jars. He counted the tubes. He counted the guards. He counted the steps from the passage to the floor. He counted the steps from the floor to the exhibit tier. He counted the distance between his room and the Skell's cage. He counted the distance between the Skell's cage and Marrow's cell. He counted the distance between Marrow's cell and the throat. He counted the distance between the throat and the blood-courts.

The boy was counting the city.

The boy had been counting the city since the first walking-through with the tall one. But the count had been the tall one's count — the brokers' count, the yield-count, the price-count. The count the tall one used to price what the boy's body gave. Now the count was the boy's. The boy was not counting yield. The boy was not counting price. The boy was counting *people.* The boy was counting the finite things the city ran dry and discarded. The boy was counting the way the green had taught him to count — not numbers but *presences.* Things that were there. Things that were real. Things that had weight in the still place.

Eleven cages. Ten bodies. One empty. The empty was a young one's shape in the muck.

The boy set the count in the still place. The count sat beside the name and the brands and the disc and the three theories and the fragment and the cost-ledger and the Red's appetite-map and Marrow's breathing and the Asha-kel terms. The count was the newest thing on the shelf. The count was the heaviest.

---

The boy went back up. The bleeding-house passage. The exhibit-tier stairs. The gap in the guards' rounds. The room with the window and the platform and the water-jug.

The boy sat on the platform. The grey sky showed through the window — the iron-colored sky, the sky that did not care. The boy looked at the sky the way he had looked at the sky on the first day on the exhibit tier. The sky that was above the city. The sky that was above the sump. The sky that was above the island across the water. The same sky.

The boy's root hummed in the stone. The Red breathed behind the breastbone. The door was open. The Red was building its room. The Red had a pantry now, and the pantry was the sump-floor, and the sump-floor was organized by hunger, and the hunger was the thing the boy pressed down and the pressing was getting harder.

But the count held.

The boy reached for Marrow's breathing. The exhibit tier to the cells — the bled distance, the gap the city put between the boy and the anchor. The still-sound did not cross. The gentling did not come. But the count crossed. The count did not need the sound. The count was the boy's own frequency, and the frequency was human, and the human thing was the thing the Red could not eat.

*I am here. You are there. The distance is real. I am counting.*

And below — below the cells, below the exhibit tier, below the bleeding-houses, at the bottom of the city where the stone was saturated with blood and the cages circled the smaller floor and the amber eyes watched from the last cage — the Skell breathed. And the boy counted the Skell too.

*I am here. You are there. The distance is real. I am counting.*

The rope held.

And the Red, behind the breastbone, the door open, the room expanding, the pantry stocked and filed by appetite — the Red counted too.

The Red did not count the way the boy counted. The Red counted the way a thing counts the things it intends to eat. The Red counted mouths and yield and the weight of blood in a body and the time it would take for the body to give that blood up. The Red's count was not the boy's count. The Red's count was the appetite's count — the thing that looked at the sump-floor and saw a pantry, the thing that looked at the cages and saw jars, the thing that looked at the Skell in the last cage and saw *grist* and filed it under *the thing between rungs that I want to taste.*

The boy pressed the Red down. The Red went behind the door.

But the Red had learned to count.

And the boy had learned that the Red could learn. The two of them breathed the same air behind the ribs, and the counting was the same counting — and that was the worst of it.

The boy closed his eyes. The eleven and the ten and the one. The amber eyes, the empty muck-shape, the jars, the tubes, the iron that hummed.

The boy was Rook. The boy was the count. The count was not a yield or a meaning or a ground — it was a boy in a room with a window, counting the distance to every body the city was running dry, and the counting was the thing the city could not price because the city did not understand counting.

The city understood blood. The city understood price. The city understood yield and spectacle and doctrine and line.

The city did not understand a boy who counted the discarded.

The boy counted the discarded. And the counting was the rope. And the rope was getting longer.

And somewhere in the sump, in a cage built for a dog, a Skell with amber eyes pressed against the bars and breathed — a half-beat slower than it had been before the boy came. The half-beat was the thing the boy had given, and the giving was the count.

The beginning of what, the boy did not know yet.

But the count had started, and the count did not stop.
