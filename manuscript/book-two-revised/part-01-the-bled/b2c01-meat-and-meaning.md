# Book Two, Chapter 1: Meat and Meaning

The cell had no morning. The dark did not thin. The torch-glow from the shaft above came and went on the guards' schedule, not the sun's, and the boy had stopped reading the dark for dawn after the third shift-change and started reading it for the feed-slot.

The feed-slot opened. A bowl came through the iron — broth, thin, the surface showing a skin where it had cooled in the passage. A hand followed and set a hard thing beside it. The slot closed.

He took the broth first. The hands closed on the clay, the mouth found the rim, the broth went down warm and salt and wrong. Not wrong the way the green's water had been wrong — not poison, not rot. Wrong the way all food was wrong down here. The taste of a thing made to keep meat alive, and nothing more. No fat. No care. Measured to a body the way water is measured to a root-cutting in a jar: enough to keep it, not enough to grow it.

The bread-shaped thing was bread. He ate it in three slow bites, working each one against the side that still had teeth.

From two cells down: the still-sound. Marrow, checking. The gentling — two falls and a catch, the rasp of it where the three teeth were gone. The boy made it back. The breathing settled into the rhythm that meant *I am here. I ate. The dark is the same.*

---

He had counted six feed-slots since the pit. Six mornings. The shoulder where the thing's teeth had gone in had closed in two — the warm doing what the warm did, the edges pulling. By the third morning it was a ridge of new skin. By the sixth it was a scar — thin, white, the shape of a thing that had been in his shoulder and was not anymore.

The warm had retreated behind the breastbone. It slept under the wrongness — under the root, deeper, the layer he had not known was there until the teeth went in and the blood came out warm. He could not reach it. The pressing found the wrongness — the fist, the root, bedded down in the blood-stone dark — and under the wrongness, deeper, the faint pulse of the warm. But the warm did not answer.

The memories the warm had burned as fuel were still gone. The sister's hair. The Old One's page-sound. The fruit taste from the first fire-night. He searched for them the way the tongue searches the gaps where teeth were pulled — the shape of what was missing, the ache of the place where the thing had been. The shapes were there. The things themselves were not.

The wrongness stirred in the dark behind the ribs. The root, bedded in its country. It did not press up. It did not press down. It turned over in the blood-stone dark the way an ember turns in spent ash — slow, patient, the movement of something that had all the time and none of the need.

The boy bore down on the root. The still place kept — name, count, the Kala's teaching, the after-things. The line held.

But the count had a new number in it, and the number was six. Six mornings. Six feed-slots. Six broth-bowls and six bread-shaped things. And no second trip to the pit.

The patron had said *more.* The patron's mouth had shaped the word in the gallery, the pale eyes bright with the number the boy had given them. And then: nothing. Six mornings of nothing. No cage. No chain. No running-chain. No gallery. No pit.

The nothing was a thing the boy read. The cage had taught him the language of waiting — the way the men who owned other men used time as a leash longer than chain. You chained a thing once and let the hours do the rest. The hours taught the body that the dark was the shape of the world.

The patron was letting the hours teach.

---

The boy did not let the hours teach. He counted them — the body counted, the way the body counted a crossing or a fever or a low — and he set the count in the still place beside the name and the brands and the Kala and the crack and the warm.

The cell was stone. Three walls. A fourth wall of iron bars — the cell door — set into a frame of older stone, the stone of a thing that had been built before the bars and cut to hold them. The ceiling was the same stone, vaulted, the shape of a tunnel that had been closed and lined. The floor was stone covered with straw gone to damp mat. A sleeping-stone jutted from the back wall — a shelf, waist-high, wide enough for a body to curl on. The feed-slot was in the door, low, the width of a bowl. The running-chain ring was in the wall beside the door, empty now, the chain taken after the pit.

He had read the cell the way he read a den on the island — exits, materials, temperatures, threats. The exits were the door, which locked from outside, and the shaft above, which was reached only by the cage. The temperature was the cool of a thing underground — not the green's wet heat, not the ship's salted wind. A dry cool, steady, the cold of stone that had been stone for a long time. The threats were the pit, the patron, the warm, and the hours.

He set each threat in the still place and gave it a weight. The hours were the one he could fight, because the hours were the one thing in the cell he could count.

He counted.

---

On the seventh morning — the seventh feed-slot — the guards came.

Not the pit-guards. Different men. The boy read them through the bars the way he read everything — hands, weight, the way they held the iron.

These were not fighters. The hands were soft — not the cracked-nail, hard-palmed hands of the men who worked the chain and the cage. These hands had never closed on a running-chain. They held ledgers.

Two men. One tall, narrow, the face going to fat at the jowls though the body was thin — a body that sat and counted and did not move enough to burn what it ate. The other shorter, broader, the hands ink-stained, the eyes quick and moving. Both wore the eye-and-thorn at the throat — not carved, not branded. Stitched into the collar. The sigil of the city, the mark on every wall, the thing the boy wore on his chest without knowing what it meant.

The tall one looked at the boy through the bars. The eyes did the thing eyes did when they found the boy in a cell — the quick inventory, the reckoning. The eyes went to the shoulder scar first, then the hands, then the gap where the teeth were gone. The reckoning was different from the patron's pricing. The patron priced the way a man prices a thing he owns. This one priced the way a man prices a thing he might buy.

The short one wrote in the ledger. The scratching of the nib was the sound of counting.

The tall one spoke. The words were the language of the hold — the slaver's tongue, the words the boy had first learned behind the teeth and then unlearned on the island and then learned again in the camp and the hold and the crossing. But the accent was different. A rounder mouth. A city mouth. The words came out shaped by stone walls and the habit of speaking to things in cages.

"Open it," the tall one said.

The guards — the pit-guards, the hard-handed ones — opened the door. The iron swung out. The tall one did not enter. He stood at the threshold and looked at the boy the way the boy looked at a thing he was reading: whole shape first, then the parts.

"How long since the pit?" the tall one asked. Not the boy. The short one with the ledger.

"Seven lows," the short one said. A *low* — the word the boy knew. The day-unit the hold used, the slaver's word, carried here. Seven lows. Seven mornings. The count was right.

The tall one looked at the shoulder scar. "Healed?"

The short one looked up from the ledger. "The report says closed in two."

The tall one's mouth did a thing — not the patron's bright-eyed *more,* not the slaver's possessive *mine.* Something quieter. A mouth working around a number it must re-shelve. The number did not fit the shelf where it was put. The number needed a new shelf.

"Bring him," the tall one said.

---

The chain went on the wrists — not the running-chain, the shorter chain, the walking-chain, the one that bound the hands in front and gave the guards a lead. The boy went.

The shaft outside the cell was the vertical stone throat he had been carried through before. But this time he was walking, not caged, and the walking showed him the city.

The shaft went up and the shaft went down, the stone cut in tiers, each tier a gallery rimmed with timber and iron, and each gallery was full.

The lower tiers were cages. Not all men. The boy read the shapes as he passed: a heavy-bodied thing, gray-skinned, the build of a creature hauled across water and caged in stone. A Mure. The deep-one shape — the wide mouth, the slick skin gone dry in the cell air, the eyes that had seen tide-water and now saw torch-light. The Mure was in a cage the size of its body, and tubes ran from its arms to clay jars on the floor. The jars were dark with what they held.

The boy's root read the Mure before his eyes settled. The wrongness — the fist, the survival-core — went still the way it went still when it read a thing it recognized. Not above, not below. *On.* The same rung. The wrongness read the Mure the way a hand reads a face it has touched before.

The boy filed the reading — *the root knows the Mure* — and kept walking.

Above the Mure, more cages. Men — the boy's own kind — in the same iron, some standing, some sitting, one lying with the stillness of a thing that had decided to stop.

Above the men: the working tier. Open galleries, not cages — counting-rooms, their walls hung with strips of marked hide and rows of clay tokens. Men and women moved in the counting-rooms with the quick, bent step of people who served a thing larger than themselves and knew the serving was the only safety. The smell of the working tier was ink and sweat and the sour undertone of bodies that did not leave the stone.

The eye-and-thorn was carved on every door-frame — cut into the stone, old, the edges worn. The sigil the boy carried on his chest, the one the dead expedition's bones had worn, the one the seer's finger had found — here it was the city's throat and walls and door-frames, the mark of the thing that ate.

The tall one walked ahead. The short one walked behind, the nib still scratching. The pit-guards walked at the boy's sides, the professional grip on the chain.

They went up.

---

The tier they brought him to was higher — not the top, not the galleries where the faces had watched the pit, but a middle tier, wider, the ceilings cut taller. The air was different. Drier. The stone was cleaner. The torches were spaced wider and the light they gave was steadier, the light of tallow fed on purpose, not scraps.

The rooms on this tier were not cages but rooms — doors of iron-banded wood, floors covered with woven mat. The smell was tallow and dried herb and the faint, constant undertone of old blood, the way the island's green had carried the undertone of rot — not fresh, just *there,* the baseline smell of the place.

They put him in a room. The walking-chain came off. The door closed — not locked from outside with iron but barred with wood, the bar on the outside, the door solid. The room had a sleeping-platform, a clay jug of water, a bowl of the same broth, and a window.

The window was a slit in the stone, the width of a hand, cut at an angle that showed the sky. The body went to it the way the body went to the tide-line on the island — the first measure of where the world began and the den ended.

The sky was grey. Not the green's white-hot sky, not the hold's salt-bright square of open hatch. A grey sky, low, the color of old iron. Below the sky: walls — the rim of the city, cut against the sky, and beyond the rim, nothing he could read. Fog.

The air through the slit was cold — not the green's rain or the hold's bilge. A dry cold, steady, the cold of a place that did not care whether the bodies in it were warm.

The cold found the scar on the shoulder. The new skin tightened. The warm pulsed once behind the breastbone — faint, answering, the banked thing turning over — and went quiet.

He stepped back from the window. The room was the room. The sleeping-platform was stone covered with matting. The water was clean — he tasted it, the mouth testing for poison and rot the way the mouth tested all water, and the water was clean. The broth was the same broth: thin, salt, measured.

He sat on the platform. The still place set the new count: seven mornings in the cell, one walking through the city, one room above the cells. The eye-and-thorn on every wall. The Mure in the cage below, tubes running to clay jars. The men in the counting-rooms, the bent step, the ink-stained hands. The tall one's mouth tightening around a number that did not fit.

And Marrow. Two cells down, seven mornings of the still-sound crossing the dark. The boy had counted Marrow's breathing the way he counted his own — each fall a stone set in the still place, each settling a proof that the thing the boy had made on the island had crossed the water intact. Marrow was here. Marrow was below, in the cells, in the dark. The walking-chain had not gone on Marrow this morning. They had taken the boy alone.

The patron's calculation was simple, and the boy could read it the way he read the green: *separate the thing that counts from the thing it counts.* On the island the patron had kept them together — two cells, the same dark, the still-sound crossing between. Now the patron moved the boy up and left Marrow below. The distance was the leash. The boy would do what the patron wanted because the patron held the thing the boy counted.

The patron had miscalculated. The boy counted Marrow because Marrow was his — not because the patron put Marrow in a cell. The count depended on the still-sound, and the still-sound crossed the dark the way it had crossed the pit.

But the boy filed the miscalculation without trusting it. A patron who miscalculated once might miscalculate again, or might be doing the thing the green did — the thing that looked like mistake and was not. The green let the boy find the fruit tree so the boy would stay near it, and the thing that wanted the boy could find him there. The patron might be letting the boy count Marrow so the boy would hold the count where the patron could reach it.

---

The door opened. Not the tall one. Not the short one with the ledger.

A woman. She came in the way a thing comes that has done this before — the step certain, the hands empty, the body carrying no threat and no welcome. She was past the middle of her years, the face lined, the hands broad, the nails cut short and clean. She wore the eye-and-thorn at the collar, stitched, the same mark the tall one wore. Her eyes were the color of wet stone and they did the thing eyes did when they found the boy: the pricing. But hers was slower. The eyes went to the scar on the shoulder and stayed there.

She crossed the room. She set a cloth bundle on the sleeping-platform — unfolded it, and inside: a strip of dried meat, a clay pot of paste that smelled of rendered fat and something bitter, a roll of clean bandage. She looked at the shoulder scar. She looked at the boy's hands — the cracked-nail palms, the chain-calluses, the healing cuts from the pit. She looked at the gap where the teeth were gone.

She did not speak. She sat on the edge of the platform and waited — the patience of a body that had sat beside many wounded things in rooms like this and knew that the body would show what the body needed before the mouth found the words.

The boy read her the way he read all things. The hands said: *I tend.* The eyes said: *I count.* The collar said: *I belong to the thing that owns this city.*

She was not the patron. She was not the tall one who priced. She was the hand the city used on the bodies it kept.

He let her look at the scar. She rose and came close — not touching — and the eyes went over the ridge of new skin the way the seer's eyes had gone over the wrongness on the island: reading, not diagnosing. But the seer's reading had been wonder and a question folded inside it. This woman's reading was flatter. A thing that had seen fast healing before and had a shelf for it and did not wonder.

"Two days," she said. Not to the boy. To the room. The way a person says a thing to remember it — the mouth confirming what the ledger would hold.

She opened the clay pot. The paste smelled wrong — the fat and the bitter thing under it, an herb or a mineral, the smell of a medicine made by people who knew what closed a wound and did not care what opened one. She dipped two fingers and reached for the shoulder.

The boy caught her wrist.

The root moved before the boy did — not the warm, but the wrongness, the survival-core, the fist that had kept him alive on the island by not letting hands touch him when the hands had not earned the touching.

The still place bore down. The boy loosened the grip — the wrist stayed in his hand, the bones of it small and dry in his palm — but enough that the woman could pull free if she chose.

She did not pull free.

She looked at his hand on her wrist. She looked at his face. The wet-stone eyes did not widen. They did the tally one more time — the shoulder, the hands, the gap — and then they did a thing the boy had not seen eyes do in this city.

They waited for him.

The waiting of a thing that understood the hand on the wrist was the boy's law, and the boy's law was older than the city's law.

The boy let go.

She put the paste on the scar. The paste was cold and the cold stung — the bitter thing in it biting the new skin the way the green's plants bit when you brushed them wrong. The scar reddened. The woman watched the reddening the way a person watches a fire they set — confirming the thing was doing what it was meant to do.

She bandaged the shoulder. The bandage went over the scar and around the chest and the hands were quick and did not linger. The strip of dried meat she left on the platform beside the bandage roll, and the meat was the first real food — not broth, not bread-shaped grain, but *meat* — the boy had held since the hold.

He did not eat it. Things given in this city came with a weight. The broth was measured to keep him alive. The meat was measured to do something else.

The woman watched him hold the meat and not eat it. The wet-stone eyes did the pricing one more time, and the reckoning ended in a place the boy could read: the boy was not stupid. The boy read gifts the way the boy read the green — a thing given was a thing with a cost, and the cost was the thing the giver took.

She gathered her bundle. She went to the door. She paused — the hand on the door-frame, the body half-turned — and spoke for the first time to the boy and not to the room.

"It heals fast," she said. The wet-stone eyes on the shoulder. "They will want to know how fast."

She left. The door barred behind her.

---

The boy sat on the platform with the meat in one hand and the count running.

The city priced his blood. The patron priced the show. The brokers priced the yield. The woman priced the healing. And the eye-and-thorn on every wall was not decoration — the eye watched, the thorn drew. The Mure in the cage below, the tubes running to clay jars, the blood drawn and jarred and carried up through the tiers. The city was a machine built to take blood and count it and weigh it and move it through the tiers the way water moved through the green's root-lines.

On the island the wrongness had been a thing the boy kept on a shelf at the back of himself. Here the city kept *him* on a shelf — a cell, a room, a broth-bowl, a meat-strip. The city shelved him with care, because the thing on the shelf was valuable, and with intention, because the thing on the shelf was going to be used.

He ate the meat. The body needed it — three slow bites, the taste of dried meat the first taste in seven mornings that did not taste of a thing made to keep meat alive.

The wrongness stirred. The root, deep in its country, stirred. The root had read the Mure below — the same rung, the thing the root recognized — and the reading had not gone away. The root wanted to go down through the stone and taste the tiers the way it had tasted the thing in the pit.

The boy held the root down. The still place kept. But the holding found the gap — the warm's gap, where the sister's hair and the Old One's page-sound and the fruit taste had been burned as fuel. And the absence was the thing the patron would use. The patron was not weighing the weapon. The patron was weighing the *cost* — how many fights before the warm burned the after-things away and left nothing but the root.

And the root would fight forever.

---

The door opened again. The tall one. Alone — no short one, no ledger, no pit-guards. He carried a stool, low, three-legged, and he set it inside the door and sat on it the way a man sits who has decided that the sitting is the first move and the move takes priority over the standing.

He looked at the boy. The boy looked at the tall one.

"You are Drevan Sool's sale," the tall one said.

The name landed in the still place. Drevan Sool — the slaver-captain, the patient one, the hand made flesh. The man who had bait-staked the Kala and taken Rook netted and sold the reading along with the body. *This one does not die — watch.* The captain's sale had reached this city the way all sales reached all cities — through the hands that took the price and the mouths that carried the word.

"Sool is dead," the tall one said. "The fever he carried. He died on the crossing or after — the report is not clear. But the sale outlives the trader."

The boy did not speak. The tall one read the not-speaking — the way the green read the boy, the whole shape before the parts.

"Sool sold a reading," the tall one said. "Not a body. *This one does not die. Watch it not die.* Those were his words, or close enough. The reading came through three hands before it reached this floor. By the time it reached me, it said: *wrong-blood. Does not die in the pit. Blood does something.*"

The tall one's mouth worked again. The number not fitting the shelf.

"I have read the arena report," the tall one said. "I have read the healer's count. Two days for a wound that should take twenty. And I have read — " He paused. The pause was not for the boy. The pause was for the tall one himself — the pause of a man deciding how much of his own shelf to show.

"I have read the bleeding-books," the tall one said. "The Mure on the third tier yields six jars a low and weakens. The Vorth on the fifth yields four and sickens. The Skell yield less and die faster. Every wrong-blood on this floor is priced by the yield and the weakening, and the yield always goes down, because the body is a finite thing and the blood is a finite thing, and what is drawn is not replaced."

He looked at the boy.

"Your wound closed in two days. Your blood did something on the arena floor that the pit-guards described in words I do not have. And you ate this morning and the food is already in your muscles, because the body that heals in two days does not waste the broth."

The tall one leaned forward on the stool. The elbows on the knees. The hands together, the fingers laced — the posture of a man about to say a thing he had not said to the thing he was saying it to.

"You are not a finite thing," the tall one said. "Or you have not been one yet. And the floor below me is full of finite things that are dying because they *are* finite. Do you understand what I am saying?"

The boy understood. The tall one was not pricing the boy as a fighter. He was pricing the boy as a *yield* — a thing that gave blood and did not weaken. The Mure yielded six jars and sickened. The boy yielded something the pit had never seen and did not sicken.

The boy was not a fighter. The boy was a well.

The tall one read the understanding in the boy's face — or read enough of it — and the jaw set again, and the setting was the thing the boy had come to recognize in this city: a man who had received a number and must put it somewhere and the shelf was not built yet.

"I am Maren's function," the tall one said — not his name, his function, the word the city used for the thing a person did. "I price what the floor yields. I price what the floor costs. I have priced every wrong-blood on this floor for eleven years, and I have never priced a thing that does not run dry."

He stood. The stool stayed.

"I will price you," he said. "The pricing will take time. The patron wants the pit again. The brokers want the blood. The temple wants the meaning. And I — "

He stopped. The jaw worked one more time, and this time the working was not the number. This time it was the man — the man inside the function, the face behind the pricing.

"I want to know if the thing Sool sold is the thing I am pricing, or if the thing I am pricing is something Sool did not know he had."

He left. The door barred.

---

The boy sat on the platform. The meat was eaten. The bandage was on the shoulder. The window-slit showed the grey sky going darker — not the green's sudden dark, not the hold's constant dark. A slow dark. The dark of a sky that had its own schedule and did not hurry.

Not the words — the words were weighed the same way the broth was weighed. What the tall one had given him was the shape of the city.

Four powers. The patron owned the ground and the ring. The brokers priced the yield. The temple wanted the meaning — would read the boy's blood the way the seer had read it on the island, with wonder and a question folded inside. And below all of them, the floor: the cages, the Mure, the jars, the finite bodies that ran dry.

The floor was the thing the boy would become if the city had its way. The Mure on the third tier — tubes in the arms, jars filling, the body sickening. The corruption ladder made into a city. The warm would walk him toward it if he let it, and the city would weigh every step.

He set the shape in the still place. The count had a new shape now — not the island count or the tribe count or the hold count. The city count. The tiers, the yield, the price. And the boy in the middle, counting the only thing the city could not weigh: the name behind the teeth, the still-sound crossing the dark, the hand that would open.

The cage had opened on the ship, and the green had made him.

The cage had opened on the island, and the tribe had made him.

The cage would open here too.

He closed his eyes. The gap behind the teeth was warm with the name. The shoulder was whole under the bandage. The cost was a gap he would not search for.

Below, in the cells, Marrow breathed. The still-sound crossed the stone — Marrow's gentling — the two-fall rasp, the catch where the three teeth were gone — and found the boy on the sleeping-platform, and the boy made it back, and the breathing settled into the rhythm that meant *I am here. The dark is the same. We count.*

The boy counted.

And the city, above and around and below, the eye-and-thorn on every wall, counted back.
