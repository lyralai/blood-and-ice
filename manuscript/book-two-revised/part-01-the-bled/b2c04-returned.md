# Book Two, Chapter 4: Returned

The chamber healed him from below, and the healing was wrong.

Not wrong the way the green's healing had been wrong — the fever-air, the bites that did not swell, the body rebuilding itself from the wrong fuel. This healing was wrong because it was *thorough.* The ribs knitted in the low and a half. The torn calf muscle wove itself shut in two. The scoring on his side — the three rake-lines the usher's teeth had opened — closed to white ridges in three lows, and by the fourth low the ridges were gone. Smooth skin. The body the green had built and the cage had trained and the pit had tested, restored whole.

But the body was not the body the pit had been given.

The Red had rearranged it. Not in ways the eye could read — the scars were the same, the brands were the same, the gap where the three teeth were gone was the same warm dark. But the way the body *moved* was different. The legs carried the weight low, the way the Hesh carried weight — coiled, the tendons loaded. The arms hung with a looseness that was not relaxation. The looseness of a thing that has struck fast and knows the speed it carries. When the boy rolled on the platform, the body turned *toward* the door, not away — the orientation of a body that has learned to face what comes through.

The boy catalogued the changes the way he catalogued all things: practically, the body counting what the body had become. The Red had re-tensioned the muscles. The Red had re-set the skeleton's default from *survive* to *hunt.* The boy could feel the difference in his hands — how the fingers curled when he was not thinking about them. Not the cage-flinch, the closing-around-nothing, the ghost of the bar. The fingers curled *in.* Toward the palm. Toward the grip. The hand of a body that expects to close on something.

The Red was behind the breastbone. The door was open — not all the way, not the full room of the third throat, but the door the Red had walked through and the door that had not closed behind it. The boy could feel the Red the way he felt the root — always there, always part of the architecture. But the root had been a fist. The Red was a *voice.* Low, steady, speaking in the frequency the floor had spoken in. Not words. Not yet. But the pressure of words — the shape of meaning pressing against the inside of the ribs the way the name pressed against the gap behind the teeth.

The Red was learning to talk.

---

On the fourth low, the tall one came.

The stone door opened. The three-legged stool. The sitting-as-first-move. But the tall one's step was different — quicker, the professional distance compressed. The tall one sat and his eyes did the thing they did, but faster. The pricing was under pressure.

"The blood-courts have convened," the tall one said. Not to the boy — to the room, the way the healer woman spoke to the room. Confirming for the ledger. "Every chair. Every house. The reading came in at the third low and the courts have not recessed."

The boy set the words in the still place. *Blood-courts. Every chair.* The topmost gallery — the one the boy had barely seen in the throat, the chairs with the backs. Those were the blood-courts. And they were sitting. And they had not stopped sitting.

"The patron wants you on the floor again," the tall one said. "Not the rite-floor. The open floor. The exhibit floor — the one where the yield is shown, not spent."

The boy read the tall one as he read the green. The tall one's hands were together, the fingers laced — the posture the tall one used when the number did not fit the shelf. But the lacing was tighter. The knuckles white. The tall one was holding his own hands the way a man holds a thing shut.

"The temple has filed a claim of jurisdiction," the tall one said. "The rite was temple-authorized. The floor's response occurred during a temple rite. The temple says the floor's answer belongs to the temple."

The pause. The working jaw. The man surfacing behind the function.

"The arena-masters say the floor was their floor. The floor is maintained by the masters' labor. The masters' guards run the throat. The floor's response occurred on the masters' ground. The masters say the floor's answer belongs to the masters."

Another pause. Longer. The jaw working harder.

"The brokers say — " The tall one stopped. The stopping was the thing the boy read: the tall one did not want to say what the brokers said. The tall one was Maren's function — the broker's function — and the thing the brokers had said was the thing the tall one's own function had produced, and the producing was the thing the tall one could not set down.

"The brokers say the boy is a market event," the tall one said. "The yield, the healing rate, the floor's response — every price in the city is now indexed to what happened on that floor. The brokers say the boy belongs to the market, because the market is what the city *is,* and the city will tear itself apart over a thing that has no price. The brokers say: *price him, and the tearing stops.*"

The boy understood. Four powers. Four claims. The temple wanted the meaning. The masters wanted the ground. The brokers wanted the price. And the blood-courts — the houses — the tall one had not said what the houses wanted. The houses had convened and had not recessed, and the not-recessing was the thing the boy could not read.

"What do the blood-courts want?" the boy said.

The tall one looked at him. The first time the tall one had looked at the boy without pricing — the eyes bare, the function down, the man behind the function looking at the boy the way a man looks at a thing he is afraid of.

"The blood-courts have not said," the tall one said. "They have convened and they have not spoken. In eleven years on this floor I have seen the courts speak four times. Each time, the thing they spoke about was not seen again. Two were wrong-bloods. One was a Mure queen — the only one taken alive. One was a priest who bled from the eyes."

The tall one's voice went flat. The flatness of a body reciting a thing it has made peace with by making the peace small.

"None of them were seen again," the tall one said. "The courts do not speak to keep. The courts speak to *resolve.* And the resolution is always the same: the thing is removed from the city's argument by becoming the city's property — not the masters', not the temple's, not the brokers'. The courts'. The city's. The whole."

The boy filed the shape of it. *Removed from the argument. The whole.* The courts did not want the boy for the yield or the meaning or the ground. The courts wanted the boy because the boy was the argument, and the argument was tearing the city, and the city could not tear.

---

They came for him at the shift-change. Four guards — not the cell-guards, not the pit-guards. Different men. The boy read them through the doorway: heavy cloaks over the eye-and-thorn stitched at the collar, the hands gloved, the grips careful. These were the courts' hands. The men the blood-courts used when the city's property needed moving.

The walking-chain did not go on. The boy filed the absence — the walking-chain was the city's standard for wrong-bloods, the lead that said *property being transported.* No chain meant a different classification. Not wrong-blood. Not property-being-moved. Something else.

The boy went.

The corridor went up. The stone changed as they climbed — rougher cut below, cleaner above, the tiers the boy had walked before with the tall one. But they passed the middle tier — passed the room with the window-slit, passed the counting-rooms — and kept climbing. Higher than the boy had been. The air changed. Drier. Warmer. The tallow-smell thinning, replaced by something cleaner — the smell of air that had been near the sky.

The corridor opened onto a gallery the boy had not seen.

Not the throat. Not a counting-room. A broad shelf of stone, open on one side to the shaft — the vertical shaft the throat was cut into, but here, high up, the shaft was wider, the walls gentler, the opening to the sky larger. The boy could see the sky. The grey sky, the iron-colored sky, the sky that did not care. But he could see it, and the seeing was the thing the body did — the face turning toward the open air the way the face turned toward water, automatic, the body's oldest reading.

The gallery was full. Not the packed-body fullness of the throat's tiers — the fullness of people who had *places.* Chairs. Mats. Low tables with clay instruments the boy did not recognize. The gallery was arranged in quarters — four sections, each section facing the open side of the shelf, each section occupied by a different kind of body.

The temple quarter: the robes, the burned-on eye-and-thorn, the bronze rods and the marked clay. The temple was here in force — a dozen or more, seated, the faces composed into the stillness the boy recognized from the seer on the island. The stillness of bodies that had practiced being still.

The masters' quarter: the pit-guards, the hard-handed men, but not in their working clothes. Cleaned. The hair oiled. The eye-and-thorn on leather bands, not stitched cloth. The masters had dressed for this. The masters were showing the city what the masters looked like when the masters were a *power,* not a function.

The broker quarter: ledgers on laps, the ink-stained hands, the quick eyes. Maren's function was here — not the tall one but three others, the same narrow build, the same quick look, the same profession written in the posture. The brokers had brought the books. The brokers were ready to *price.*

And the fourth quarter — the blood-courts. The houses. The chairs with the backs. Here, not at the top of the throat, but on the gallery floor, present, the chairs arranged in a shallow arc. The people in the chairs were older, heavier, dressed in cloth the boy had not seen in the city — not the rough weave of the tiers, not the robes of the temple. A finer cloth, darker, the eye-and-thorn woven into the fabric in a thread that was almost the same color, so the mark was felt, not seen. The houses did not *show* the sigil. The houses *were* the sigil.

The boy stood at the gallery's edge. Four quarters. Four powers. Each one facing the open side of the shelf, where the sky showed through the shaft, where the stone floor met the air.

Between the quarters, in the center of the gallery floor: a stone. Not the packed earth of the throat. A cut stone, square, waist-high, the top surface dark and stained. The boy's root read it before his eyes settled — the stone was old blood. Old blood soaked into cut stone the way it had soaked into the throat's earth. A smaller version of the floor. A *portable* floor. The city had cut a piece of the throat's earth and set it here, on this gallery, the way the green's people might carry a piece of a sacred grove.

The exhibit stone. The place where the yield was shown.

The boy understood the shape. The four powers had convened around the exhibit stone, and the boy was to be placed on the stone, and the four powers would argue over what the boy was. Not a trial. A *pricing.* The largest pricing the tall one had ever seen, and the tall one had been pricing for eleven years.

---

The guards brought him to the stone. The boy stood beside it. The stone was warm — the blood in it was not dead, not inert. The root felt it through his soles, a low hum, the floor's smaller cousin. The Red stirred low in the chest — not rising, not pressing. *Listening.* The way it had listened to the floor. The Red was interested.

The boy pressed the Red down. The door stayed open but the Red went behind it — patient, watchful, the thing that had learned the boy's crack and was willing to wait.

A voice from the temple quarter. A woman — not young, not old, the voice carrying the trained projection of a body that had learned to fill stone rooms. The words were the old language, the chanting-language, but spoken here as speech, not song. The drone stripped out. What remained was the cadence of a body reciting law.

"The rite was temple-authorized. The floor's response — the blood-awakening, the memory-bleed, the floor speaking — occurred within the temple's jurisdiction. The temple claims the carrier. The temple claims the meaning."

The words were for the gallery. The boy was the subject, not the audience. The boy stood beside the exhibit stone and the temple spoke about him the way the slavers had spoken about him in the hold — the body present, the meaning elsewhere.

The masters' quarter answered. A man — broad, scarred, the hands of a body that had worked the floor. The voice was not trained. The voice was the voice of a body that had yelled over crowds and was now yelling over a gallery.

"The floor is our floor. The throat is our throat. The masters' guards run the tiers. The masters' blood feeds the stone. The temple filed the rite but the masters *paid* for the rite — the labor, the guards, the usher. The floor's answer belongs to the hands that work it."

The broker quarter did not speak. The broker quarter wrote. The nibs scratching in the silence after the masters' voice — the sound of counting, the sound of a thing being priced while the other powers argued over who owned it. The brokers did not need to claim. The brokers needed to *wait.* Every argument the temple and the masters made was a number in the brokers' books, and the number was the thing the brokers would use when the arguing stopped.

The blood-courts said nothing. The chairs with the backs, the fine dark cloth, the faces that did not move. The courts listened. The courts had convened and had not spoken, and the not-speaking was the thing the boy could not read. The courts were waiting for something. The boy could not tell what.

---

A figure moved in the gallery. Not from the quarters — from the side, from a doorway the boy had not seen. A man, thin, the body built for speed like the usher's body had been built for speed, but this was a human speed. A runner's speed. The man crossed the gallery floor with the quick, bent step of a body that served and knew the serving's shape.

He carried a drum. Small, round, the skin stretched tight. He sat cross-legged beside the exhibit stone, and he set the drum on his knee, and his hand touched the skin — once, twice — and the sound was the sound of a heartbeat made louder.

The pit-singer.

The boy had not seen him before. The boy had not heard the word. But the gallery knew. The temple quarter stiffened — the robes drawing back, the composed faces breaking for a half-beat. The masters' quarter leaned *in.* The broker quarter's nibs stopped.

The pit-singer was the broadcaster. The man whose function was to *name the thing* for a city that could not read.

He began.

Not the old language. Not the chanting. The pit-singer spoke in the tongue the tiers spoke — the hold-tongue, the slaver's tongue, the language of the cell and the counting-room. The common tongue. And the speaking was not recitation. The speaking was *song* — the cadence of a body trained to hold a crowd's ear the way the torch-holders held the torches. The rhythm was the thing. The words rode the rhythm.

He named the floor first. The old earth. The blood-soaked ground that had been drinking since before the city had a name. He named it as the green named a thing — by what it did, not what it was called. *The earth that eats.* The gallery heard it. The gallery knew the floor, but the pit-singer made the floor *new* — gave it the tongue's weight, the rhythm that said *this thing is older than you and it matters.*

Then he named the boy.

Not the boy's name. The boy's *deed.* The pit-singer described the third throat the way the boy had lived it — the drugged man, the opening rite, the blood. But the pit-singer's version was not the boy's version. The pit-singer's version had *shape.* The boy had bled. The boy's blood had touched the floor. The floor had *answered.* And the answer was not a vibration or a frequency or a root-taste. The answer was a *voice.* The pit-singer gave the floor a voice, and the floor's voice said: *this is the one.*

The boy felt the gallery shift. The temple quarter's faces went still — the practiced stillness, but strained. The masters' quarter's eyes went bright — the patron's bright, the *more.* The broker quarter wrote faster. And the blood-courts — the chairs with the backs — the courts did not move, but the not-moving changed quality. The courts were *listening differently.*

The pit-singer was doing the thing the temple had not done in time. The temple should have named the event — interpreted it, contained it, given it meaning within the doctrine. The temple had convened the blood-courts and prepared the argument and filed the jurisdiction claim. But the pit-singer had *sung.* And the song had outrun the doctrine. And the song said the boy was not a wrong-blood or a yield or a market event. The song said the boy was *the floor's answer.* The floor had been waiting, and the boy had come, and the floor had said *here,* and the *here* was sacred.

Sacred. The word the boy did not have. The pit-singer had it. And the gallery heard it. And the hearing was the thing the temple could not take back.

---

The temple quarter rose. The woman — the speaker, the trained projection — stood and her voice cut across the gallery, and the voice was sharp, and the sharpness was the thing the boy recognized: the sound of a power losing control of the story.

"The carrier is a *vessel of the rite,* not the rite's answer. The temple interprets the floor's response. The temple has *always* interpreted the floor's response. The pit-singer speaks for the masters, not the doctrine, and the doctrine is — "

The pit-singer's drum touched once. A single beat. The woman's voice stopped — not because the drum was louder but because the beat was the sound the gallery *listened for,* and the voice was not. The pit-singer's hand rested on the skin. The gallery waited.

"The earth that eats has eaten," the pit-singer said. Quiet. The trained voice, but the trained voice *whispering.* The whisper that carries further than the yell. "And the earth has given back. The earth gives back the ones it does not want. The earth gives back the ones it cannot hold." The pause. The hand on the drum. "The earth did not give this one back."

The gallery breathed. The boy felt it — the many-bodied breath, the sound a body makes when a thing has been said that cannot be unsaid.

The earth did not give this one back.

The pit-singer had made the boy the floor's chosen. Not the temple's rite. Not the masters' ground. Not the brokers' price. The *floor's.* And the floor was older than the temple and older than the masters and older than the brokers' books. The floor was the thing the city was built on. The floor was the thing the eye-and-thorn pointed at. And the floor had chosen.

The boy stood beside the exhibit stone and the Red stirred under the ribs — not rising, not pressing. The Red was *listening* to the pit-singer the way it had listened to the floor. The Red was interested in the song.

The boy drove the Red back. Hard. The still place locked — name, count, the Kala's teaching, Marrow's breathing. The anchors held. But the holding was harder now. The Red was not just the fist, the survival-core, the root. The Red was the thing the pit-singer was singing about. The Red was the floor's answer. And the Red *agreed.*

---

The blood-courts moved.

Not all of them. One chair — the center chair, the largest, the back carved with the full symbol that the boy had seen on the chamber walls. The body in the chair was old. Not the years-old of the tall one or the worn-old of the healer woman. The old of a thing that had been in the city longer than the city had been the city. The face was dry, the skin drawn tight over the bones, the eyes the color of the exhibit stone — dark, stained, the iris and the pupil almost one.

The old body did not stand. The old body spoke from the chair, and the voice was the voice of a body that did not need to be loud because the room had been built to hear it.

"The carrier will be placed," the old body said. "Not in the temple's rite. Not on the masters' floor. Not in the brokers' books. The carrier will be placed where the city places the things it cannot agree on. The middle. The counting-floor. The exhibit tier. The carrier will be shown — visible, accessible, counted — and the city will price what it sees, and the pricing will be the pricing, and the argument will stop."

The temple quarter stood. The masters' quarter stood. The broker quarter did not stand — the broker quarter wrote. And the blood-courts — the old body in the center chair — the old body did not stand because the old body had already said the thing and the saying was done.

*The middle.* The boy understood. Not the bottom, not the top. The exhibit tier — the counting-floor, the place where the yield was shown and the city could see. The courts were not giving the boy to any faction. The courts were giving the boy to the *city.* The whole. The thing the tall one had described: *removed from the argument by becoming the city's property.*

But the boy was not property. The boy had never been property, not since the island, not since the green, not since the name had gone into the gap behind the teeth. The cage had owned his body. The slavers had owned his transport. The patron had owned his placement. But the name was his. The count was his. The still place was his.

The gallery was looking at him. Not the pricing-look. Not the reckoning. The *other* look — the look the throat's crowd had not had. In the throat, the galleries had looked at the boy like a question. Here, on the exhibit tier, with the pit-singer's song still in the air and the blood-courts' ruling still settling, the gallery looked at the boy like an *answer.*

The boy felt the look as he felt the green's humidity — a pressure, a weight, the air thicker than the air should be. The look said: *you are the thing.* The look said: *you are the floor's chosen.* The look said: *you are sacred.*

The Red surged. Not the boy's choice — the Red heard the look and the Red *answered,* the way the Red had answered the floor. The Red pressed up into the boy's throat and the warmth went through his chest and the boy's body wanted to *stand straighter,* to *square the shoulders,* to show the gallery the thing the pit-singer had named. The Red wanted to be *seen.*

---

The vomiting came fast.

The boy bent — the body bending, the stomach seizing — and the vomit hit the exhibit stone. The broth, the meat, the water. The contents of the body on the stone that was the floor's cousin, and the vomit was the body's answer to the Red's surge, the body rejecting the thing the Red wanted.

The Red recoiled.

Not the pressing-down — not the boy's will shoving the Red behind the door. The Red *itself* recoiled. The Red pulled back behind the breastbone like a hand from a flame, and the flame was the shame. The boy's body had rejected the worship, and the Red — the hunger that fed on blood and memory and the floor's deep song — the Red could not hold the shame. The Red was strong enough to walk the boy's body, strong enough to drink through the boy's hands, strong enough to learn the boy's crack. But the Red was not strong enough to hold the body's vomiting. The Red was not strong enough to hold the shame.

The shame was the boy's. The shame was the human thing. The shame was the count and the name and the Kala's teaching and Marrow's breathing and all the things the Red could not eat because the Red did not understand them. The Red understood hunger. The Red understood power. The Red understood the floor's frequency. The Red did not understand a body that vomited when the body was worshiped.

The boy straightened. The gallery was silent. The pit-singer's hand was on the drum, still. The temple quarter's faces were the practiced stillness — but the eyes, the eyes were moving. The eyes had seen the vomiting. The eyes had seen the body reject the worship. And the reading the eyes were doing was the reading the boy could not stop: *the carrier is not what we said. The carrier is a boy who vomits when we name him sacred. The carrier is human. The carrier is ours.*

The boy understood what he had done. The vomit on the exhibit stone was the most honest thing the body had produced since the blood on the throat's floor. The blood had been the Red's. The vomit was the boy's.

---

The silence held. The gallery waited.

The boy opened his mouth. The gap where the three teeth were gone — the warm dark gap that held two names now, the Kala's name and the floor's old name — the gap opened, and the boy spoke.

"Rook."

One word. The name the Kala had given him. Not the old name. Not the floor's name. Not the name the pit-singer had sung or the name the blood-courts had ruled on or the name the temple wanted to own. The boy's name. The small name. The name that had lived behind the teeth through the cage and the green and the tribe and the hold and the pit and the throat and the floor and the Red.

The word went across the gallery. The stone walls carried it as the throat's walls had carried the chanting — but this was one voice, one word, and the word was not a song. The word was a body standing beside a stone with vomit on it and saying: *this is what I am called.*

The gallery did not breathe.

The boy said it again. "Rook." Not louder. The same. The flat statement of a thing the boy had been carrying behind his teeth the way the cage had carried him — locked, barred, kept. The name was out now. The name was in the air. The name was in the gallery alongside the pit-singer's song, but the name was not a song. The name was a sound made by a body with a gap in its teeth and vomit on its chin and hands that had killed and a thing behind the ribs that wanted to be worshiped.

"I am Rook," the boy said.

The third time. The count of three — the count the island had taught him, the count that said *this is real, this is set, this is stone.* Three times, the way the seer's finger had touched the wrongness three times, the way the Kala had said the name three times in the dark.

The gallery heard it. The gallery could not un-hear it. The boy had named himself, and the naming was not the pit-singer's naming, and the naming was not the temple's naming, and the naming was not the blood-courts' naming. The naming was the boy's. And the boy's naming said: *I am not the floor's answer. I am not the temple's rite. I am not the masters' ground. I am not the brokers' price. I am a boy with a name, and the name is Rook, and the name is mine.*

The blood-courts' center chair — the old body, the dark eyes — the old body's mouth moved. Not speaking. Something else. The mouth shaped a word the boy could not hear, and the shaping was the thing the boy recognized from the floor's memory — the mother by the hearth, the mouth shaping a name through the blood-talk. The old body knew the name. Not *Rook* — the other name. The old name. The name the floor had given him.

The old body knew.

---

The gallery broke into sound. The temple quarter conferring — the robes leaning together, the voices low and sharp. The masters' quarter arguing — the scarred hands gesturing, the voices not low. The broker quarter writing — the nibs working, the numbers being set. And the blood-courts sitting in their chairs with their fine dark cloth and their carved backs, and the courts were done.

The courts had ruled. The boy would be placed on the exhibit tier — the middle, the counting-floor, the city's ground. The argument would stop. The boy would be shown, and the city would price what it saw, and the pricing would be the law.

But the boy had named himself. And the naming was the thing the city could not price, because the naming was not a yield or a meaning or a ground. The naming was a boy standing in his own vomit saying *I am Rook,* and the boy was not for sale.

The guards — the courts' hands, the heavy cloaks — brought the boy down from the gallery. Not to the chamber. Not to the cell. To the exhibit tier — the middle, the counting-floor, the wide gallery where the yield was shown. They put him in a room on the exhibit tier. The room had a door of iron-banded wood. The door had a bar. The bar went across.

But the room had a window — wider than the slit in the middle-tier room. Wide enough to put a hand through. Wide enough to put a face through. The boy put his face through and the air came in — the grey air, the iron-sky air, the air that smelled of stone and distance and the thing beyond the rim the boy could not read.

The exhibit tier was above the cells. Above the Mure-cages. Above the bleeding-houses and the sumps. But below the blood-courts. Below the temple. The middle. The place the city put things it could not agree on.

The boy pulled his face from the window. The room had a sleeping-platform, a water-jug, a basin. The bare necessities of a body the city was keeping. No broth-bowl. No feed-slot. No measured meal. The city had moved the boy from the cell to the chamber to the exhibit tier, and each move had added a thing — the chamber had added the carved walls, the exhibit tier added the window, the room — and each move had taken a thing — the cell took the dark, the chamber took the cell's iron bars, the exhibit tier took the feed-slot. The city was not starving the boy. The city was *reclassifying* the boy.

The boy sat on the platform. The Red was banked behind the ribs. The Red was quiet — not the patience of the root, not the patience of the thing that had all the time and none of the need. The quiet of a thing that had been *shamed.* The Red had wanted the worship. The boy's body had rejected the worship. The Red had pulled back. And the pulling back was the thing the boy filed — the first limitation the Red had shown that the boy had not put there himself.

The Red could not hold shame.

The boy set the count. The blood-courts had ruled. The pit-singer had sung. The temple had lost the story. The masters had claimed the ground. The brokers had priced the event. And the boy had said his name three times in a gallery that wanted to call him something else.

Below — somewhere below, through stone and old blood and the tiers the city had built to separate the thing it harvested from the thing it counted — Marrow breathed. The boy could not hear it. The exhibit tier was too far from the cells. The still-sound did not cross the distance as it had crossed the cell-dark.

But the count crossed. The count did not need the sound. The count was the still place's own architecture, and the still place held Marrow the way it held the name — set in stone, counted, real. Marrow was below. Marrow was breathing. The boy did not need the sound to know it.

The Red shifted in the hollow of the chest. Not rising. Settling. The thing that had been shamed was finding its footing — not charging, not pressing. Finding. The way a body finds a new den. The Red was looking for the place inside the boy that the shame had opened, and the Red was *moving in.*

The boy pressed down. The still place held. The name held. The count held.

But the Red had learned the thing the vomiting had taught it. The Red had learned that the shame was the boy's, not the Red's — and the Red had learned that the shame was a *crack.* The worship the boy rejected was the worship the Red wanted. And the wanting was the crack the Red had already found: *you want this too.*

Not the memories this time. Not the mother and the father and the mark. The *worship.* The boy wanted to be seen. The boy wanted to be named. The boy wanted the gallery to look at him and know what he was. The cage had made him invisible. The green had made him the clean space. The tribe had seen him and the seeing had been the thing the far hunger fed on — *as if belonging were a meal.* The boy wanted the belonging. The boy wanted the name spoken by other mouths.

The Red wanted the worship.

The boy wanted the belonging.

The Red had found the crack where the two wants met.

---

The boy lay on the platform. The exhibit tier was quiet. The city's schedule moved around him — the guards' shift-change, the distant sound of the counting-rooms, the scrape of the brokers' nibs. The eye-and-thorn on the door-frame watched.

The boy had been the offering. The floor had taken. The city had argued. The pit-singer had sung. The courts had ruled. And the boy had said his name.

But the name was in the air now. The name was in the city. And the city — the four powers, the tiers, the eye-and-thorn — the city would do what the city did with things it could not agree on.

The city would count.

And the boy would count back.

And the Red, behind the breastbone, the door open, the shame settling into the crack like water into stone — the Red would wait.

The Red had learned the boy's crack. The boy had learned the Red's. And between them, in the warm space behind the ribs where the door stood open, the two of them breathed the same air.

The boy was Rook. The boy was the floor's answer. The boy was the name behind the teeth and the name the floor had given and the thing the city could not price.

The boy was all of these things.

The boy did not know yet which one would survive.
