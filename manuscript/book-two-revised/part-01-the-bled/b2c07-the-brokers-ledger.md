# Book Two, Chapter 7: The Broker's Ledger

The tall one came on the fourth low.

He did not come with guards. He came with a clerk who carried a board and a string of bone beads, and a man who walked as if the city's weight had already been tallied and the tally was not his to carry. The man was narrow at the hips and broad across the chest — the shape of a body that counted more than it lifted. His hands were clean. Not the clean of the courts. The clean of a man who washed the ink off before the ink could stain.

The tall one stayed at the door. The narrow man came in alone.

"The courts have not decided what you are," the narrow man said. The voice was flat. Not cold — flat, as if warmth were a line item he had already struck through. "They will decide when the Red has shown them more than one face. Until then you are property that cannot be priced."

The boy sat on the platform. The words went into the still place beside the sump-floor and the cages and the Skell's amber eyes. The still place did not answer. The body answered. The body sat still the way it had sat in the hold when the slavers walked the aisle and read which ones would fetch a price.

The narrow man studied the boy the way a man studies a column that will not reconcile. "You have a name the city does not own. That is a problem. Names that are not owned are names that can be taken. I am here to own the name before someone else does."

The boy said nothing.

"My name is Maren," the narrow man said. "I count what the routes bring in and what the routes take out. The routes are the city's blood. The courts drink it. The arena spends it. The bled are the price the routes pay. You are a new price. I am here to decide whether the price is worth the cost."

He set the board on the platform. The clerk had already marked it — lines of bone beads, each bead a different size, each size a different weight. The boy read the board as he read the green. The lines were not marks on wood. They were the city's shape made small.

"You eat," Maren said. "You drink. You sleep on stone that does not freeze. That is the cost of keeping you alive. The courts pay it because they do not yet know what you are worth. I pay it because I do not yet know what you will cost me."

The boy looked at the board. The lines and the beads and the flat voice went into the room the Red had built. The Red's room had a pantry now. The Red's room had a ledger. The Red shelved the narrow man where it had shelved the sump-floor: *the one who counts before he spends.*

Maren saw the boy's eyes on the board. "You read the lines." Not a question. A reading. "Good. The courts think the Red is a thing that answers to hunger. I think the Red answers to numbers. Hunger can be fed. Numbers can be made to lie. I want the numbers that do not lie."

He tapped one line on the board. The bead on that line was larger than the others. "This is the cost of keeping the bled alive. The bled in the sump. The ones the courts have finished arguing over — whose blood still has a price but whose bodies do not. The courts want to spend them on the throat. I want to spend them on the routes."

The boy looked up.

Maren's mouth moved — not a smile, a tightening. "The routes need guards. The guards need to eat. The bled need to eat. The courts will not pay for both. The courts will pay for the throat because the throat makes a story the city can tell itself. The routes make no story. The routes make the city possible."

He tapped the board again. The bead shifted under his finger. "You counted the cages. Eleven. Ten bodies. One empty. The empty one was a young one. The young one died because the count did not reach the sump in time. The count reached the exhibit tier because the courts could not agree on what you were. The count did not reach the sump because the sump has no story the courts want to hear."

The far hunger stirred. Not the lean — the other reach, the one that had gone out to the Vorth in the cage and to the tribe's fire on the ridge. The one that wanted the count to reach the places the courts had forgotten.

Maren saw the change in the boy's face. "You want the count to reach the sump. That is the first thing we agree on. The second thing is the price."

He took a second board from the clerk. This one had fewer lines. The beads were smaller, the weights lighter. "This is the price of the routes — the routes that run past the inlet, the routes the arena-masters cannot work without the bled, the routes the courts do not see because the courts do not walk the stone."

He set the second board beside the first. "You give me the routes. I give you the count that reaches the sump. The bled eat. The guards eat. The routes run. The courts do not know the price because the courts do not count what they cannot see."

The boy looked at the two boards. The name and the terms and the Asha-kel fragment and the three theories — all of it shifted to make room. The still place had never carried two maps of the same ground. The Red's map was appetite. Maren's map was routes. The boy's map was the count.

Maren watched the boy's eyes move between the boards. "You are bad at this." The flat voice did not change. "You are bad at counting what a thing is worth before you spend it. That is why the courts still own you. That is why the Red still owns you. I am not here to own you. I am here to make the numbers lie in the same direction."

He stood. The clerk gathered the boards. The narrow man looked at the boy one last time.

"The gap in the guards' rounds is narrow," he said. "The passage behind the platform is older than the city. The sump-floor is where the city keeps what it has finished counting. You have until the next low to decide whether the count reaches the sump or the sump reaches the count."

He turned. The clerk followed. The tall one stayed at the door until the narrow man was gone, then closed the stone behind him.

---

The boy stayed on the platform. The two boards and the flat voice and the weight of a man who counted before he spent settled into the bones. The Red shelved the narrow man a second time: *the one who makes the numbers lie.* The boy weighed him differently: *the one who counted the young one dead.*

The gap was still the gap. The passage was still the passage. The sump-floor was still the sump-floor. The boy had until the next low to decide whether the count could be made to lie in the direction of the bled.

The still place had never carried a choice that was also a ledger. The body took the weight whole — into the hollows the cage had left, into the places where the marrow waited.

The boy pressed the Red down. The Red went behind the door. The door stayed open.

But the Red had taken the narrow man's measure, and the taking was interest, and the interest sat beside the sump-floor and the Skell's amber eyes and the count that had not reached the young one in time.

The city had given him a room with a window. The narrow man had given him a room with two maps. The boy was carrying both. The boy was carrying the city the way he carried all things — whole, in the body, the weight of it settling into bone.

The gap narrowed. The boy stood. The root already knew the passage.

The boy went through the wall. The stone tilted inward. The passage descended. The sump-floor waited below.

The count had not reached the sump. The boy carried it now — the count, and the ledger, and the two maps that did not agree on what the ground was for.

The boy went down.
