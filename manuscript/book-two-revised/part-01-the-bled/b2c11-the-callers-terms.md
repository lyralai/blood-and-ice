# Book Two, Chapter 11: The Caller's Terms

The sitting at the tithe-stones had changed the sump.

Not the stone. Not the cages. Not the hum in the central floor or the tallow-dishes guttering in their niches. The sump changed because the bled had watched the boy sit on the count's ground, hold the Red behind the door, and say his name three times in the working light. The watching turned the sump from a place where bodies were kept into a place where bodies waited.

The bled watched him differently now.

Kade's devotion had hardened into architecture. The brightness in the seventh cage rebuilt the sitting as mastery—the vessel proving the Red was his by holding it—and the rebuilding spread. The freed-but-owing on the working tier had seen the shaking. The masters' gallery-faces had seen the sweat. The pit-singer made a sound of it—two lines carried from mouth to mouth through the working tier and down into the bled: *the carrier sat and the Red sat and neither moved.* The sound traveled faster than the clerk's clay record. It needed no courts' authority. It was the city's reading, and the city's reading was that the boy had done a thing no carrier had done.

He felt the change as weight on the chest.

The weight belonged to Kade's reading, not his own. He had held. The city saw the holding as strength. The holding was cost—the membrane thinning, the Red learning his reserves, the door that opened and closed at his deciding. The city saw mastery. He felt the spending. The two readings did not agree on what the holding was, and the not-agreeing was the danger. The city's reading made him a thing worth following. His reading made him a thing worth keeping behind a door.

---

The bled came on the eleventh low.

Not all. Only the ones whose cages opened for working-tier labor, whose owing-counts let them walk the brokers' routes under guard. Six bodies. They came down the crawl-space stairs in the tallow-light and stood at the sump's edge. The standing was not the standing of broken things.

The Skell was among them. The ninth cage's door had opened for the bleeding-house route—the Skell's yield had dropped below the needle's price, and the masters reclassified it from yield to labor. The amber eyes held him across the circle. The recognition-sound came low and guttural, the scavenger's read, carrying no worship. The Skell had survived the island and the city. The surviving had burned worship out of the body the way the green burned the root. What remained was the reading: this one is large, the large draw the small, the small are safer beside the large than under the needle. The reading was honest. The Skell did not need it to be faith.

Kade watched from the seventh cage. The conditional release did not extend to the sump's circle. But Kade's devotion reached the circle the way the temple's chanting reached the floor—through stone, through iron, through bodies that carried the brightness without knowing they carried it.

The six stood.

He sat in the sump's circle. Marrow behind the iron. The Red behind the door. The count in the still place.

The Skell spoke first. Not words—the Skell had none the boy could speak. The recognition-sound shifted into a cadence. A question-sound. The scavenger asking the larger body: where is the kill, and is there room at it.

He read it as he read the green—whole shape first, then the parts. The shape was: lead us.

He did not answer the shape. He answered the part.

"Not yet," he said.

The Skell's amber eyes held the reading. It had heard *not yet* and not *no.* The distinction was the scavenger's whole education—the difference between the thing delayed and the thing refused. The Skell sat at the sump's edge. The sitting was its answer: we wait. We are good at waiting.

The other five read the Skell's sitting and sat. The sitting was not acolytes' devotion. It was the bled's patience—the patience of bodies that had survived cages and needles and rites and had learned that the large body's reading was better than the small body's hunger.

One of the five sat wrong.

He read it the way he read the green—whole shape first. Four bodies faced the circle. The fifth faced the stairs. The eyes went to the crawl-space entrance and came back and went again, the counting of exits that a body does when the body belongs to another ground. The masters opened that cage for working-tier labor. The working tier had its own owing, and the owing was not all to the count. He did not name the wrongness. He filed it, the way the green filed the wind-change: not a threat yet. A direction a threat could come from.

Five at the sump's edge. One between. The count stayed true because the count held the between.

---

Marrow's voice came through the iron.

"They'll follow you."

He did not move from the circle.

"They'll follow the Red," he said.

The distinction carried the chapter's weight. The bled had not come to the boy. They had come to the boy who held the Red. The holding was the thing the city saw, the thing the bled followed, and the holding was the Red's as much as the boy's—the two *mine*s overlapping at the door, the two readings not agreeing on what the holding was.

He could not lead the bled without leading the Red. He could not lead the Red without the Red's leading being his own. The two were in the same ribs. The city saw one body. He felt two.

"If they follow the Red, the Red leads," Marrow said. "And the Red does not count."

The still place held the words. Marrow—the runt whose body was his whole voice, whose curled hand read pulses through iron bars, whose cage was the boy's anchor against the current that ate the after-things—had named the thing he had been circling since the tithe-stones.

The Red did not count. The Red filed. The Red shelved. The Red built its appetite-map and learned *keep* and learned *mine* and leaned against the door with the patience the boy had taught it. But the Red did not count. Counting was the boy's—the human thing, the thing the Red could not eat because the Red did not understand what counting was for. The Red understood *more* and *less* and *enough* and *not-enough.* The Red did not understand eleven cages, ten bodies, one empty. The Red did not understand that the empty cage was the boy's because the empty cage was the thing not yet taken.

The count was the boy's law. The Red's was appetite.

If the bled followed the Red, the law would be appetite.

---

He went to Asha-kel on the twelfth low.

Not the sump's passive waiting—the active choice. The crawl-space stairs, the working tier, the passages that connected the brokers' counting-galleries to the bloodline-houses' alcoves cut into the shaft's upper wall. He walked with the count's authority—the clerk's record, the courts' shelving, the tithe-stones' witnessed name. The city had seen him hold the Red on the count's ground. The city's reading of him was a thing he could spend.

He spent it now.

Asha-kel's alcove was not a room. It was a cut in the stone—narrower than the sump, higher than the throat's galleries. A body-width of dark stone with a sleeping-roll and a clay lamp and the genealogist's tools: bone-beads strung on sinew, each bead scratched with the counting-marks of blood-lines the houses had tracked since before the city was a city. The beads were Asha-kel's ledger—the houses' memory, carried on the body because the body was the one thing the city could not take from the houses.

Asha-kel sat cross-legged beneath the beads. The dark eyes—flat, measuring, the eyes of a body that priced blood-lines the way Maren priced yield and Vessa priced meaning—found him in the passage. The finding was a weighing.

"The carrier comes to the houses," Asha-kel said.

Not *the boy.* Not *Rook.* *The carrier.* The houses' reading of him was the blood-line's reading: the boy was what the blood carried, and the blood-line was what the houses kept, and the keeping was the houses' ground.

He sat at the alcove's edge. Not entering—the boundary. The reading distance, as Vessa had stood at the sump's rim.

"The boy comes," he said.

Asha-kel's mouth did not change. The dark cloth at the throat—the houses' mark, a thinner line than the temple's eye-and-thorn—settled against the collarbone as the genealogist's head tilted a fraction.

"The boy who holds the Red," Asha-kel said. "The boy who sat on the count's ground. The boy who said his name three times and the Red did not speak."

He read the weight of each phrase. Asha-kel had not said *mastered the Red.* Asha-kel had said *held the Red* and *the Red did not speak.* The houses' reading was not Kade's reading. The houses did not see mastery. The houses saw holding—and the holding was a word the houses had a shelf for, because the houses had held blood-lines for longer than the temple had been shelving meaning.

"The bled want a leader," he said.

"The bled want a large body between them and the needle," Asha-kel said. "The large body is you. The large body carries the Red. The bled will follow the Red because the Red is the thing the city fears and the thing the city fears is the thing the bled need."

He heard the precision. Asha-kel did not say *the bled need a leader.* Asha-kel said *the bled need the thing the city fears.* The distinction was the houses' reading: the bled's need was not faith or loyalty. The bled's need was protection, and the protection was the Red, and the Red was the boy's, and the boy was the blood-line's—or could be.

"I will lead," he said.

Asha-kel's eyes did not change.

"But the Red does not lead," he said. "The boy leads. The Red answers to the boy's law. The boy's law is the count."

---

The terms took the rest of the low.

He set them as stones on the alcove's ground—each one placed, each one weighed, each one the product of the still place's architecture and the count's cold logic and the education that had taught him the same lesson in every ground: power without law eats the thing it protects.

The terms:

"The Red is not king."

He spoke the term and Asha-kel's bone-beads shifted—the genealogist's hand finding a bead without looking, the fingers reading the scratched marks. The bead was the houses' record of the old carriers. The old carriers who had led. The old carriers who had been led by the Red. The distinction was the houses' oldest scar—the line between the blood that serves and the blood that rules.

"The boy answers to law before appetite."

The law of the count. The law that said eleven cages, ten bodies, one empty and could not be reinterpreted by doctrine or hunger. The law that was the boy's because the Red could not count.

"The boy cannot call rite, raid, or sacrifice alone."

He set this stone and the stone was Marrow's—the runt's warning made structural. Followers ask permission after spending your name. He had read the green's law: the large body that acts alone is the large body that draws the wrong things. The tribe's king had answered the challenge alone and the alone had cracked the law. He would not make the king's error. The boy's law required the bled's count—the bled's voice, heard and weighed—before his body moved the city.

"If the boy goes Red and will not answer to his name, an outside hand may call him back."

Asha-kel's fingers stopped on the beads.

The term was the crown's hinge. He felt the hinge in his own chest—the membrane thinning, the Red leaning forward behind the door. The Red heard the term. The Red read the term as a cage-bar: a thing that would hold the Red inside the boy's law. The Red did not like the cage-bar. The Red pushed—not the breaking push, the testing push, the push of a body finding the new iron's shape.

He held the Red behind the door. The still place locked.

Asha-kel read the shaking—the tremor in the hands, the iron taste swallowed. The genealogist had read carriers before. The reading was professional.

"Who holds the hand?" Asha-kel said.

"The houses," he said.

Not the bled. Not Kade. Not the devotion. The houses—the rival faction, the blood-line keepers, the genealogists whose ground was the oldest claim on the blood he carried. The houses, whose reading of him was useful, or ours, or the thing to end.

The caller was deliberately the rival. Not his base. Not the bled. The faction whose interest in him was the blood-line's interest, not his welfare. The leash could not be captured by his power because the leash was held by the thing his power could not reach.

Asha-kel's hand left the beads.

"The houses accept the holding," Asha-kel said. The voice was flat—not the blade's edge, not the killing-floor. The houses' flatness: the voice of a body that had weighed a thing and found the weight acceptable, and the finding was the houses' reading, professional and cold.

"But the hand requires a relic," Asha-kel said. "Blood or hair. A thing the houses keep. A thing the houses hold against the tithe-stone when the name is spoken."

He felt the Red's push become a strain. The relic—a piece of the body, kept by the rival faction. A piece of him in the houses' hands. The boy who had been property, whose body had been branded, whose teeth had been pulled—the boy was being asked to give a piece of himself to another's keeping.

The cage had taken pieces. The green had taken pieces. The city took pieces. He had learned the lesson of pieces: a thing taken from you is a chain someone else holds.

But the relic was not a taking. The relic was a giving. He gave the relic. He chose the giving. The distinction was the crown's first law—the law that separated the cage from the crown. In the cage, pieces were taken. In the crown, pieces were given. The giving was the law.

"Blood," he said. "Not hair. The houses keep blood."

Asha-kel nodded. The nod was small, precise—the genealogist's acceptance of the terms' architecture. Then the genealogist set a term of the houses' own, and the setting was the first thing Asha-kel had done that was not flat.

"Blood, and the line the blood names," Asha-kel said. "The relic enters the beads. The carrier's count becomes the houses' count. What the houses keep, the houses carry."

He heard the letter of it, and under the letter, the claw. The leash was given. The line was taken. His blood would live in the houses' ledger beside the lines the houses had tracked since before the city was a city, and the ledger outlived bodies. The boy who would not be property was entering his blood into another's keeping by his own choosing—and the choosing made it law, and law was the thing that lasted.

The cage took pieces and called it holding. The crown gave pieces and called it law. The houses took the given piece and called it *carrying.* The three were not the same, and the not-same was the crown's ground, and he stood on it with the taste of iron in his throat.

He gave the blood. He did not give the line's meaning. The distinction was the only wall the claw had not named, and he built it in silence, behind the still place, where the Red could not read it and the houses could not shelve it.

---

The crown was not gold.

He had seen gold once—a ring on the slaver-captain's hand, the thick band catching the hold's lantern-light. Gold was the masters' metal. Gold was the thing the city's blood-courts hoarded. Gold was not his and gold was not the bled's and the crown he would wear was not the courts' crown.

Marrow made it.

The runt's hands—the curled hand and the straight hand, the cage's gift and the green's gift—worked in the sump's tallow-light with the patience he recognized from the green. The patience of the body that had survived. Marrow had saved three things from the bleeding-house floor: a strip of dried sinew from a wrong-blood's wrapping, a shard of chipped bled-stone from the central floor's edge, and a tooth.

The tooth was not human. The Skell had given it—a back molar, yellow, ridged, pulled from its own jaw with a silent offering and no explanation. The Skell's gift was the scavenger's law: a thing shared at the kill is a thing that binds the sharing.

Marrow worked the shard and the tooth onto the sinew. The strip became a band—a brow-band, dark with old blood, the bled-stone shard and the Skell's tooth and three knots tied in the sinew between them. Each knot was a term. Each term was a notch. The crown was a law written in stone and tooth and sinew, and the law was the boy's, and the crown bit the wearer first.

Marrow set the crown on the sump's rim. The runt's hands shook—not the Red's shaking. The shaking of a body that had built a thing and found the building harder than the surviving.

Marrow had built a king's crown with a runt's hands. The building was the bravest thing he had seen since the green's rot-hollow, when Marrow held the rope at the Hesh hunt. The rope-holding was the body's bravery. The crown was the runt's.

---

He took the crown on the thirteenth low.

Not in the throat. Not on the tithe-stones. In the sump—the ground he had claimed, the ground the doctrine could not reach, the ground where the count was the only meaning.

The bled were present. The six who could walk, and Kade—released conditionally for the rite, the masters' petition and the courts' agreement and the brokers' tally. And Marrow. And the Skell, whose amber eyes held the reading that was not worship but recognition. And the Mure in the eighth cage, whose breathing rattled and whose web-fingered hands pressed the iron and whose presence was the warning: this is what the Red's carrier becomes without the count.

The fifth body was there too, the one whose eyes counted stairs. It knelt with the rest when the bled knelt, and the kneeling was honest in the knees and dishonest in the eyes, and the eyes went to the crawl-space entrance once, twice, marking the exits for a report the masters would hear before the low ended. He let it kneel. The crown's law would have to hold for the watchers as well as the watched, or it was not law. He added the body to the count the way the green added the wind: a thing that moved, and would move again, and could be read when it moved.

He knelt.

Not to the bled. Not to the houses. Not to the temple or the masters or the courts. He knelt to the sump's ground—the wet stone, the hum, the floor's frequency under his knees. The kneeling was the cage's oldest posture inverted: the body that had been forced to kneel choosing to kneel. The choosing was the crown's first beat.

Asha-kel stood at the sump's edge. The genealogist held the relic—a clay dish, sealed with wax, his blood dried on the inner surface. The blood he had given on the twelfth low. The blood the houses kept. The relic was the leash made physical—the outside hand's hold on his name.

He set the crown on his own brow.

The stone-shard sat between the eyes. The tooth sat above the right ear. The three knots pressed the temples—the three terms made physical, the sinew cutting the skin with a thin, precise pain. The pain was the crown's first law: the wearer bleeds first.

The Red felt the crown. The push came—not the breaking push, not the testing push. The reading push. The Red reading the crown the way it read all things: what does this do, who does it do it to, what is the cost. The Red found the terms in the knots and the terms were bars and the bars held. The Red pushed against the bars and the bars were not the boy's alone. The bars were the houses', and the boy's, and the bled's, and the count's. The bars were the law.

The Red went behind the door. Not the boy's driving—the Red's own withdrawal. The Red read the crown and the crown was a cage and the Red knew cages and the knowing was the Red's oldest education.

The Red did not like the crown. The Red accepted the crown.

The acceptance was the most dangerous thing the Red had done.

---

He stood.

The crown sat on the brow. The stone-shard between the eyes carried the old blood's dark color—the color of the throat's floor, the color of the sump's central stone. The tooth above the right ear was the Skell's gift. The three knots held the sinew tight and the sinew cut and the cutting was the law.

The bled saw the standing. Not the standing of the tithe-stones—the boy holding the Red behind the door, the boy shaking, the boy spending. The standing of the crown. The standing that said: this body carries the Red and the count and the law, and the three are not the same, and the body decides which one speaks.

Kade's brightness flared. The devotion read the crown as the vessel's coronation—the mastery made visible, the Red mastered, the boy ascended. The devotion was the danger. The devotion would not see the cutting. The devotion would not see the terms. The devotion would see the crown and read king and the reading would be the wrong reading and the wrong reading would spread the way the pit-singer's song spread—faster than the clerk's clay, faster than the count.

Marrow saw the cutting. The runt's eyes—the body's whole weight in the gaze—found the thin lines of blood where the sinew cut the temples. The crown's first law. The runt read the blood and the reading was the reading he needed: not mastery. Cost.

Asha-kel held the relic. The clay dish, sealed, his blood inside. The houses' hand on the leash. The genealogist's dark eyes found his across the sump's circle and the finding was not warmth. The finding was terms.

"The houses hold the relic," Asha-kel said. The sump's acoustics carried each word clean—the stone giving back the sound without echo, the words placed as the killing-floor placed them. "The houses hold the call. If the carrier goes Red and will not answer to the name, the houses strike the relic against the tithe-stone and speak the name three times. The carrier answers. Or the carrier is bound."

The bound was the second term's shadow. Bind or kill. He had set the term. The houses had accepted the term. The bound was the word the sump heard and the word the bled carried and the word that would travel the city the way the pit-singer's song traveled.

He felt the Red behind the door. The Red had heard bound. The Red had heard kill. And the Red did not push.

That was the wrongness. The terms had drawn a testing push, and the relic a strain—but the rite, spoken whole in the sump's clean acoustics, drew nothing. The Red held still and took the shape in: the relic, the tithe-stone, the striking, the name three times, the interval between the speakings. The stillness was not acceptance. The stillness was the sump's own crouch—the body going quiet so the prey would not know it was read. The Red had placed bound and kill beside keep and mine and the door that thins, and beside them it was placing the rite itself, whole, waiting for the low when the shape of the cage was a thing it could put its hands on.

The Red was learning the law the way it learned all things: by the shape of the cage, by the spaces between the bars. And the not-pushing was worse than the pushing, because the push was hunger and the stillness was study.

---

Asha-kel spoke the recall.

Not the recall itself—the recall's shape. The genealogist's voice carried the houses' oldest reading, and the reading was not the temple's doctrine. The reading was the blood-line's memory: carriers before him, carriers who had been called back, carriers who had not been called back, carriers who had been bound.

"Three carriers," Asha-kel said. "Three that the houses kept the relic for. One answered the call. One did not answer. One answered and went Red again."

The sump heard it. The bled heard it. He heard it—the count running beneath the words. Three carriers. One return. One silence. One failure.

"The one who answered was bound for nine lows," Asha-kel said. "The binding held. The carrier lived. The Red went behind the door."

"The one who did not answer was found in the sump," Asha-kel said. "The body was cold. The blood was dark. The Red had left the body the way water leaves a cracked jar."

"The one who answered and went Red again—" Asha-kel paused. The pause was not Vessa's stone-placement pause. The pause was the houses' weight—the weight of a body that had carried the memory and found the carrying heavy. "The one who answered and went Red again was the houses' failure. The binding was used. The binding did not hold. The carrier went Red and the Red did not go behind the door and the carrier was not the carrier after."

The hand was on the beads again. Not the reading-touch of the first term—the fingers had found one bead without looking and stayed on it, the pad of the thumb pressed to the scratched marks the way a tongue goes to the gap where teeth were. He read the bead's count the way he read all counts: by what the body would not do with it. The genealogist would not let it go. The genealogist would not speak the line it named.

The third carrier was of Asha-kel's own line.

He did not say it. The saying was not his ground. But the flatness re-read itself in the reading: the voice that priced blood-lines like Maren priced yield was scar, not cold, and the scar had a shape—the shape of a body that had held the relic of its own blood and struck it against the tithe-stone and spoken the name three times, and the name had not been enough. The hand that would hold his leash was the hand that had already failed once, at the worst cost the houses knew. The leash was not malice. The leash was the houses' oldest grief, given the only work the houses had left for it.

That was worse than malice. Malice could be counted.

He read the pause's weight. *Was not the carrier after* was the houses' language for the thing the green had taught him to call the root. The carrier who went Red again became the root. The after-things burned away. The warm—the human thing, the Kala-thing, the name-thing—the warm was gone. What remained was the survival-core without the still place, the appetite without the count, the Red without the door.

The husk.

The word sat in the sump. He did not speak it. Asha-kel did not speak it. The word was the thing the houses' memory held and the holding was the weight the relic carried—not just his blood, but the blood-line's memory of what his blood could become.

---

He stood in the sump with the crown on his brow and the terms in the sinew and the Red behind the door.

The bled were his. Not Kade's devotion—the bled. The Skell's honest reading, the five who sat at the sump's edge, the Mure's warning, Marrow's anchor. The bled were the count's, and the count was the boy's, and the boy was the law's, and the law was the crown's, and the crown bit the wearer first.

The relic was the houses'. The call was the houses'. The leash was held by the rival faction because the leash could not be held by his own.

The Red was behind the door. The Red had read the crown and the terms and the relic and the recall, and the reading was not defeat. The reading was the Red's newest education. The Red learned the law the way the Red learned all things: by living inside it, by feeling its shape, by finding the spaces between the bars.

The Red was patient. The crown was law. The two were in the same ribs.

He knelt once more. Not the cage's kneeling. The count's kneeling—the body touching the ground that held it, the sump's wet stone, the hum. The Red's frequency in the stone and the count's frequency in the boy and the law's frequency in the crown.

The three frequencies did not match.

He stood. The bled stood with him. The Skell's amber eyes. The five. Kade's brightness. Marrow's hand on the iron, the curled fingers reading the pulse the runt could not reach.

Asha-kel held the relic. The clay dish, sealed. His blood. The houses' hand.

The crown cut the temples. The sinew held. The terms held.

He was king of the bled, leashed by a rival, useful to all four powers, owned by none.

And the Red placed the law beside keep and mine and patience and the door that thinned, and the placing was the thing he feared most. Because the Red that learned the law was the Red that learned where the law's cage had spaces.

And the law's cage always had spaces.

The count held. Eleven cages, ten bodies, one empty.

The empty cage was the boy's. The boy wore the crown. The crown was made of the bled's stone and the bled's tooth and the bled's sinew, and the wearing was the law, and the law bit first.

The sump held them all—the crowned boy, the anchored runt, the devotee's brightness, the scavenger's read, the houses' relic, the Mure's warning. The hum carried. The chanting above did not stop.

And the Red was patient behind the door, and the door was thinner than the sinew, and the sinew was already cutting, and the blood ran down the temples into the gap where the three teeth were gone.

The blood tasted of iron. The iron tasted of the Red. The Red tasted of the law.

And the law tasted of the cage.
