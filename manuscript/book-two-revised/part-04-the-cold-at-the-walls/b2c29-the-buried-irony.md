# Book Two, Chapter 29: The Buried Irony

The new doctrine climbed down the city faster than any count he had ever run.

Three darks after the seventh gallery, the story had already gone down eleven landings, and it went down changed, the way all stories went down changed: at the rim it had been *the carrier bled and the wall lived*; by the ninth gallery it had become *the carrier bled once and the walls will hold while he lives*; by the fifth, where the tallow got thin and the freed-but-owing pressed their backs to the stone to let the count pass, it had become *the carrier is the wall*, and the boy read the change the way he read water — not in the words, which were never where the truth sat, but in what the bodies did around the words. A woman touched his sleeve as he passed, and did not beg. Begging was for people. You did not beg a wall. You leaned on it.

He counted the leaning. That was the finding he entered on the first dark, alone, in the flat voice: *They lean. A wall does not lean. I am still counting. Mark the difference.*

The offers came the way Maren had priced them, to the dark and to the turning.

The master of the ninth sent his offer up in writing, in a hand so clean it had never held a stylus near smoke: the short-corded freed released to the count's service, all of them, and a schedule — a *schedule* — of walls to be held, tier by tier, beginning at the master's own ninth, written first, which the boy noted and did not answer. The temple sent no writing. The temple sent the under-priest, who stood now at the count's floor with the iron ladle hanging at his belt like a man who had hung a rope there, and whose gentleness had not left him, and whose certainty lay on his neck with its whole weight aimed at the boy's forearm, and who asked for nothing yet — only stood where the floor could watch him stand, patient, an answer that had learned to wait. The houses sent Asha-kel with the relic, sealed in its clay dish, and she set it on the alcove shelf and said, in the caller's flat way, that the terms held, that the relic was not a request, and that the houses' offer — a rite of the carrier's blood, witnessed, lawful, *contained* — was written and waiting and she would not speak it aloud until he asked, because the leash was not hers to pull.

He counted the offers and did not answer any of them, and that too was an entry: three hands held out, and all three of them wanted the same cut.

The border-holds he could not refuse. That was the difference between an offer and a law, and the count had made the law himself, in front of witnesses, with his own blood, on the seventh gallery: the blood ends the Cold. What ends the Cold will be asked for. That was not doctrine. That was arithmetic.

So he bled.

Gallery six on the second dark, a hand's-width of white gone quiet at the eighth turning, two drops from the small knife, the drops falling gray-tallow red, the white ending under them with a sound that came to the teeth and not the ears — a line being ruled off, somewhere, in a ledger he could not see. Gallery five on the third dark, three drops, the border drawing back floor-stone by floor-stone, and the drawing back had the same direction it had had on the seventh: not away. *Toward.* Every line of white leaning to face him as it died, the rim-side of the wall turning like grass before a wind, reading him to the last inch it ever read.

And the cuts closed the Red's way — fast, whole, seamless — and around every closed cut the skin stayed cold.

He had entered that finding on the seventh gallery and he entered it again on the sixth and again on the fifth, because the count's law was the count's law: what is, spoken plain, aloud. But by the third dark the finding had grown a second half, and the second half was the kind the head is slow to hold, and the body held it first.

On the seventh gallery there had been one cold patch, one hand's width, on the forearm.

On the sixth, two — and the two were joined.

On the fifth, three, and the map of them made a shape, and the shape ran up the forearm toward the elbow the way frost-sweat ran up a wall: in a border, and the border moved. He sat at the gray light with his sleeve back and his palm flat on his own skin, the way Maren had put her palm flat on the warm stone, and he read himself the way he read walls. Warm, under the palm, where the blood still ran his. Cold at the closed cuts, cold that did not warm under the pressed hand, cold that had a *set* to it, the way the white had had a set on the gallery stone. He held the palm there a full turning of the count — sixty, spoken, silent, the old number the runt's breathing had taught him — and the cold under his hand was at the end exactly what it had been at the start.

*Findings, spoken plain,* he entered, in the count's own hand, because any other hand in Vgrael would write it softer. *The cuts close. The cold holds. The cold joins. The border moves.*

Behind the door, the Red read the entry over his shoulder. He could feel it reading, the warmth laid along the inside of the door like a man laying an ear to a wall.

*You price it like a wound,* it said, and the tone was the new tone, the one it had learned in the counting-rooms — level, exact, almost kind. *Price it like a column, boy. Two drops a dark holds a gallery. The gallery holds the tier. The tier holds the city. And the cost-line —* the warmth moved along the cold border of his forearm, tracing it from the inside, an inch of attention at a time, *— runs at exactly the rate the hold runs. Not ahead. Not behind. That is not a wound. That is a schedule. I have kept the entries since the seventh. Would you like to hear the total?*

"No," the boy said, aloud, in the empty room, and entered the refusal too.

---

Kade came to him on the fourth dark, at the gallery mouth, where the stairs went down.

The man had stood his watch at the rim since the turning was entered — the boy had seen to that, had put him in the row alive and meant the row to hold him. But the row was not holding him the way rows held. Kade stood in the frost-cloak with the wrapped hands at his sides, and the coal of him, which had burned down to its ash on the seventh gallery, had been relit from somewhere, and the relighting was worse than the ash.

"Carrier," Kade said. "The fifth gallery holds. I watched it hold. I watched the white go out under your blood like a lamp under a hand." The wrapped hands came up, not offered this time — shown. "It kept me. On the seventh. It stood off from me a floor-stone's width and it kept me, and I have had four darks to count what keeping is for." The eyes were the problem. They were bright again, and the brightness was aimed. "The provision kneels and the stone will not drink. The willing blood runs gray. But it ends under yours. Feed me to the wall, carrier. Measured. Witnessed. Enter it lawful, the way you enter everything. Eleven of theirs were closed on the seventh and not one of them was *given.* I would be given. That is a different coin. You taught me that yourself — a given thing counts different from a taken thing. Let me count."

The boy read him whole shape first, the way he read all bodies: feet planted to be moved, hands turned up, the neck set for the blade. Wanting, all of it, wanting gone clean to the bone — and the wanting was not fear and was not devotion. Or it was devotion, turned all the way through, the way the under-priest's certainty had turned: Kade had stood inside the answer to his own wanting and found it standing off from him, patient, with nothing owed, and the not-owing had gone into him like a splinter, and four darks of counting had taught him the only arithmetic that quieted the splinter — *if it would not take me, let me make it take me.*

"No," the boy said.

Kade's jaw set. "Price it, carrier. You price everything."

"I priced it." The boy took the wrapped hands and turned them down, closed, the same press he had given them on the gallery, and held them down until the giving-up came into the wrists. "The wall kept you. The count keeps you. Those are two keepings and they are not the same animal, and I will not feed the second one to the first to learn the difference. You stand the watch. You stand it alive. That is the row, and the row holds." He released the hands. "And Kade. The next one who asks me for that coin, you turn back yourself. You know the shape of the wanting now. It is the only thing you know that I cannot watch for. That is your watch."

Kade went down the stairs. The boy counted his going, and then stood a long time at the mouth, because the finding under the finding had already entered itself, and there was no column for it: *the wall keeps men by not taking them. So does the Cold. I told him they were different animals. I have not yet entered on what grounds.*

---

He lost the smell of her hands on the fifth dark.

He had been holding it the way he held all the priced things — deliberately, at the gray light, one turning of the count for each: the sister's counting-voice, the sound of her going, the green's first dark, the mother's face. The count's law applied to the inside as much as the floor: what is, spoken plain. The Red had spent the smell of her hands on the seventh gallery and had said so while spending it, warm, exact, itemized — *this next is small, only the smell of her hands* — and he had known the spending the way you know a door closing in another room, and he had told himself that knowing was a kind of keeping.

It was not. On the fifth dark he reached for it and found the place where it had been, and the place was smooth.

Not gone the way a name is gone — worn, worked at, a thing the body could call back with effort. Gone the way a closed account is gone. Settled. Nothing owed, nothing owing. He knew the shape of that from the kneeling line on the seventh gallery, eleven of them neat and dry and *finished*, and his own memory now had the set of those necks, and the knowing came up through the marrow before the head would hold it, and the head held it anyway, because that was the count, that was the whole of the count: *speak what is.*

What was: he could not remember what his sister's hands had smelled like. He could remember that he had remembered. The shape of the keeping, kept; the thing kept, taken. On the shelf at the back of himself — the old shelf, the first one, from before the cages had names — the gap sat warm and dark, like the gap behind the teeth.

He entered it. *Findings. One item, priced out and spent on the seventh gallery, entered here five darks late: the smell of her hands. The remembering of the remembering remains. Enter the difference.* His hand did not shake on the stylus. That was its own finding, and he entered that too, and it was the worst entry of the dark: *The hand does not shake. Ask why the hand does not shake.*

Behind the door, the Red was quiet. Not the old quiet, the hiding quiet from the den. An entered quiet. The quiet of a thing that had already made its entry and was waiting, without hurry, without need, for the boy's side of the books to catch up.

---

Brannoc came up on the sixth dark.

The rim-watch brought him, two sworn, slow, the eleven landings taking most of a dark under the old one's feet — and the stair changed for him the way it changed for the count, but differently: the freed-but-owing did not press back from Brannoc, they came *out*, hands to sleeves, and more than one of them, the boy saw, had an antler on a cord at the wrist, old-cut, the marks worn to shadows. The carver of the buried endings walked up through the city the buried endings had been buried from, and the city came to the walls of the stair to touch him. The count read it whole: Brannoc was not visiting the count. Brannoc was being returned. The hands had kept him all this time the way hands keep things, without telling.

The blind eyes found nothing. The carver's feet found the seventh gallery, the border of it, the warm lines, and stopped a body-length off, and the clouded face tilted, and the boy watched the old man read the room the only way he had ever read it — through what the room did to the air.

"Colder than it was," Brannoc said. "Warm where you walked. Cold the way cold is inside stone. And under the cold —" the face tilted further, an ear's worth of listening aimed at the floor itself, "— quiet. It is not gone, carrier. You ended its standing, not its waiting. It has learned what your floor sounds like. It is doing what you do."

"What do I do," the boy said.

"It listens," Brannoc said, "before it leans."

The old hands came up, palm out, the asking-gesture of a man who had spent his life asking bodies for what the eyes were refused. The boy looked at the hands. Then he set his own forearm into them, sleeve back, the cold border bare, and the count's law held him still while the fingers walked.

He had watched those fingers once move over antler-marks and read a buried century in a turning. Now they moved over skin, and the reading was different — slower, and the slowness had a weight in it, and twice the fingers stopped entirely and stood, the way a carver stands at a grain he has cut before. At the border's edge, where the cold ran newest, the old one's whole hand lay flat, and the boy felt him take the cold's temperature the way Maren had taken the wall's, palm flat, patient, the check of a fever that does not break.

"I have cut this mark before," Brannoc said.

The boy waited. The gallery waited. The old man's mouth opened and shut on nothing, twice, and the boy understood he was watching a carving — the mouth working at a saying the way the hands worked at an antler, feeling for the grain, finding where the line had to stop.

"The third one," Brannoc said at last. "Not the ending — the middle. This exact mark. Here." One finger traced the cold border, elbow-ward, where it ran newest. "The stopping place. The mark that stops in the middle of the line. I cut it into the record because the record had to have it, and I cut it *shallow*, because I was young, and the temple paid for deep, and the difference was the only lie I ever told with my hands." The finger stopped where the cold stopped. Stood there. "He held the rim too. Longer than you have been alive, boy. The rim held and the rim held and the mark stopped in the middle of the line because the stopping is not a thing that happens at the end. The stopping is a thing that *happens the whole way*, at the rate of the holding, and the line only records where you notice."

"Say the ending," the boy said. "The whole one. Plain. You are the only hands that have it."

"I am not," Brannoc said. "That is the thing I climbed to say, and it is not the ending. There is another keeper of it, and he is climbing your stair right now behind me, carrying it in temple clay, and he is the one who *filed* what I cut. Between his shelves and my hands, the whole ending is in this city, boy, and it has been in this city for nine hundred years, and no one has ever said it aloud, and it is not going to be said aloud tonight either." The clouded eyes aimed at him, and the blindness in them was older than the cataracts. "Not because it is forbidden. Because it is a mark that stops in the middle of the line, and saying it is a way of cutting it deeper, and I have cut it shallow all my life, and I mean to die having cut it shallow. You will put it together from his clay and my hands. That is not my instruction to you. That is just what the count does. It is the only thing the Cold has never learned to price."

---

Lorren came on the same dark. Brannoc had known it, and the boy entered that too: the two keepers arriving together was not the stair's accident. One of them had sent for the other.

The priestling was thinner than the sump had left him — the stripped collar whiter where the washing had worked at it, the ink-stained fingers steady the way fingers are steady when the steadiness is all that is left to spend. He carried a clay tablet against his chest, both hands, the carrying-grip of the archives, a tablet held the way the bone-frame grief-keeper held her cord: the shape of the holding become the shape of the body. He came onto the gallery, and he saw Brannoc, and the two of them stood in the tallow light looking at each other, and the boy counted the look. Nine hundred years of files and carvings, and the two ends of it meeting on his rim, over his arm, in the cold.

"Carver," Lorren said.

"Copyist," Brannoc said. "You wrote small. I asked the temple for forty years to know why the endings were shelved in the hand that writes smallest, and I never asked the man who wrote them."

"Because the small hand is the hand that fits the column no one reads," Lorren said. "They knew what they were doing when they gave it to me. A buried thing wants a small shelf." He turned to the boy and knelt — not the kneeling of fealty; the kneeling of a man setting down weight he has carried up eleven landings — and laid the tablet on the gallery stone between them, and did not touch it again, as if the setting-down were the whole errand and his hands were finished with it.

"Information, not loyalty," Lorren said. "You know my terms. I will not kneel to the blood, and I will not call it sacred, and I will not call it damned either, because I have watched two doctrines do both to it for a lifetime and I have the endings shelved in my hand. I bring you the oldest ledger in the temple that no one reads. I copied it when I was the smallest hand in the archives, and I have carried the copy since, and I carried it before I knew what it was, the way a man carries a thing he has not read yet. I have read it now. Read yours."

The boy took the tablet the count's way, and read the whole of its shape first, and then line by line, and the reading took a long time because the count read everything twice: once for what was written, and once for what the writing stood off from.

It was a tithe ledger. The oldest form of it — rim-stones, schedules, troughs, names. And down the left margin, in the small hand, six entries spaced across what the dating-marks said was a span of years the boy made himself re-count twice, because the first count said *three hundred* and the count held.

Six names. Each name had held the rim. *Held the rim, the turns of the low entered beside each, seven turns, four turns, eleven turns.* And after each holding, in the ledger's own dry arithmetic, the same closing line, entered in the same hand that had entered the feedings and the schedules, without a space between the holding and the closing, without any mark of difference at all:

*Retired to the rim.*

"Retired," the boy said aloud. Flat. The count's voice.

"It is the temple's oldest word," Lorren said. "Older than the doctrine. I looked for what it *meant*, boy, for years, in the shelves, in the rites, in the mouth of every old one I could buy bread for. There is no other meaning shelved anywhere. It is a word that has only ever closed one kind of account. I will tell you what I know and it is one sentence, and the sentence is why I climbed: a body that is retired to the rim is not buried. The ledger says of the fed, *given to the stone*. Of these six it does not say given. It says retired. The stone was not where they were sent." The ink-stained hands pressed flat to the thighs, the sump's old gesture, the holding of a body that had reached its saying. "They were sent to the wall. Holding. That is the whole of what is written, and I have watched your gallery for six darks, and I am not going to say the thing that is not written. I am a copyist. I copied. The count is the one who reads standing."

The boy stood with the tablet in his hands, and the gallery was very quiet, and the quiet was the wrong quiet, the air in front of the white had been like this — air with nothing owed to it. Six names. Six holdings. Six lines, dry, even, spaced like fence-posts down a road that went in one direction, and the road was the margin of the tithe ledger itself, and the ledger was three hundred years long, and the last entry was not the oldest one. The last entry was the newest of the six. The column continued to the bottom of the clay and stopped, and the stopping was not a finishing.

The stopping was a place left open.

He looked up and found Brannoc's clouded eyes on him, aimed, unerring, the way the blind aim. Lorren knelt with his hands flat and his head bent over the tablet he had carried for a lifetime. Between the carver and the copyist, the boy stood in the tallow light on the rim of the city he had climbed, holding the ledger of the six who had held this rim before him, and every piece of it — the cold that joined and moved, the mark that stopped in the middle of the line, the retired, the *leaning*, the city leaning, the leaning, the *leaning* — came together in him the way the heaviest columns closed — not read but totaled, all at once, in the body before the head.

He did not say it. He entered the not-saying.

*Findings, spoken plain,* he wrote, and the words were the flattest he had ever entered, and the entering was the heaviest. *The tablet holds six. The column is open. The word is retired. The count has read standing. The count will not speak the reading until the count can price it, and the count cannot price it, and so the reading stands unspoken, entered here as unspoken, and this entry is the count holding a thing with the mouth closed on purpose, and the count knows what a closed mouth is for, and it is not always for keeping the name safe.*

---

Behind the door, the Red had heard every word of it.

It had heard the ledger. It had heard *retired.* It had heard the not-saying. And the boy stood at the gallery rail with his forearm bare to the frost and asked it the count's way, aloud, in the flat voice, because law that was not spoken was not law and there was no law that exempted the inside of the door:

"Enter your column. All of it. The way you keep it. Speak what is."

And the Red, who had waited three darks in that same entered quiet for exactly this asking, unfolded its books.

*Six held the rim,* it said, warm and level, the counting-room tone, and beneath the tone the older tone, the collector's, and beneath that something with no name the boy had ever entered — not triumph and not hunger. *Accrual.* *The rim held them back, boy, the same way it holds you: it leaned, and the leaning fed, and the account ran at the rate of the holding — the same rate I ran your warm lines and your cold border, balanced to the drop, and you called it a schedule because a schedule is what it is. Six names, six schedules, six retirements, and the city above them warm and singing, and the ledger open at the bottom because the column is not finished, boy. Columns like that are never finished. They are* continued. *And you have been asking for six darks what you are becoming, in that flat voice, entering around the word, and I will not say the word either, and mark this, because it is the first time in the whole life of this door we have wanted the same silence —* the warmth came forward against the door until the wood creaked, laid along it like a cheek, like a hand, *— not because the word is terrible. Because the word is* premature. *Two drops a dark holds a gallery. At that rate, boy, you have turns yet. Turns. And every drop holds a wall, and every drop closes a line, and the city lives, warm, leaning, singing your arithmetic down eleven landings while its own runs underneath — and I have run the total, and the total is this: the wall holds exactly as long as you do, and you hold exactly as long as the wall does, and nothing in that column is waste. Nothing. Do you hear me? Nothing is wasted. Every coin goes where it is spent. Even the smell of her hands, boy.* Even that. *That is my entry. It is the only one I have ever made where the columns balance and the ledger is still hungry.*

The boy stood at the rail and the frost came off the wall behind him and his own breath stood in the air in front of his face, and he counted it, one turning, sixty, the runt's number, and entered the Red's entry in the count's hand, whole, undressed, because a ledger that hid its worst line was a temple.

*The Red finds it efficient,* he wrote. *The Red's books balance. The Red's ledger is hungry. Entered.*

Marrow found him there at the gray light, and the runt read him standing, the way the runt read everything, from the ground up — feet, hands, the set of the neck — and the counted breaths ran their one-spent-one-owed, and the curled hand came out and lay — of its own deciding, unasked, the first time since the throat — flat on the cold border of the boy's forearm, and stayed.

"One finding," Marrow said.

"You keep a two-finding mouth."

"One finding," the runt said. "Six names in that clay, and six schedules, and the seventh schedule running, and every one of those six thought the wall was the thing they were holding up." The curled hand pressed, once, over the cold. "You're the first one who got to read the column standing. That's all. That's the whole finding. Price it how you want."

And then Maren was on the stair, two at a time, frost-cloaked, no slates, the readings already in her walk, and her face did the thing broker's faces never did in the count's whole acquaintance of them.

"Carrier," she said. "The third gallery. The watch-post at the fourth turning has not come down at the gray light." And the boy was already moving, because he knew the law of that before she finished it, the count's own law, the rim's oldest law, learned on the seventh gallery and priced to the drop: the post gone quiet, the air gone finished, the floor going up to become the news.

The Cold had come back. It had come back *down* — three tiers below the lines that held, three tiers below the warm signature it had walked away with, patient, learning, doing what the count did, doing what Brannoc had named for him on this stone not one dark gone:

It listened before it leaned.

The boy took up the small knife and went down the stairs of his city toward the quiet post, and the knife was cold in his hand before he reached the first turning.

Cold from the inside.
