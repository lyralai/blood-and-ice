# Book Two, Chapter 27: More Tithe

The sixth husk came down the shaft on the eighteenth dark, and by then the city above the fourth gallery had stopped sending them.

That was the finding the count entered first, before the body was even off the poles: *they have stopped sharing the dead.* The first three had come down wrapped and carried, a mystery passed to the only floor that read things. The sixth came down because a stair-boy had smuggled it — rolled on a barrow-frame under sacking, past two watchmen he owed, down eleven landings in the dark — and when the sacking came off, the boy stood there shaking and would not take his eyes off it, and the count understood at the platform, reading the shaking before the husk, that the husks had stopped being news above and started being *currency.* The factions were counting them now. Counting them, and pricing them, and the first rule of a thing that has become currency is that you stop giving it away.

The husk was a tallow-wick cutter from the seventh gallery. Neat. Dry. Settled like the route-runner: nothing owed, nothing owing. The sworn laid it in the cold-rows beside the other five, and the rows were becoming a thing the sump had a word for, which was worse than the rows themselves — the sump had needed a word, and the sump had made one, and the word was *the counted.*

Kade's people stood at the cold-rows with their wrapped hands at their sides and did not sing. That was new too. Two turnings ago they had sung. Now they stood in the attitude of men at a shrine they were building, and the not-singing was the building.

And Maren came down at the gray light with two slates and the face she wore when the arithmetic was the good news.

"Two findings," she said. "One you'll want. One you need more." She opened the first slate. "The rim-watch reports frost on the *inside* of the top galleries. Not rim-side — inside. The wall-stones sweat cold at the fourth turning of the dark and dry by morning, and the sweat has moved down a gallery in five darks. It is coming down the walls like a level finding its floor. That's the one you'll want, because it's a number, and numbers are the news I can hold." She closed it and did not open the second slate, and the not-opening was the second finding arriving ahead of the slate. "The arena floors have started."

"Started."

"Provision, they're calling it." The clerk's voice was level the way a floor is level after a shake — the true gone out from under it, the surface kept. "The master of the ninth posted a schedule on his own clay two darks ago. The freed-but-owing on his floors — the short-corded, the ones whose debt sits inside his walls — are being run to the stones between feedings. Off the books. No count clay, no witness, no yield spoken. The going rate above the fourth gallery is that it buys warmth, and the going rate is set by men who have never once in their lives watched a wall." She opened the second slate then, because she had made herself do it. "I priced it before I came down. I told you I'd read the doctrine as a shopping list. Here is the list, carrier: the temple is down to its last unbroken sentence, and the arenas are the temple's arms now, and the arms have already started moving. What comes to this floor next is not a clay. It's a *seal.* They're coming to make it law, and they are coming to you, because law that isn't written on the count's floor is just practice, and they know it, and I know it, and God help us both, so do you."

---

They came down on the twentieth dark, and the count took them at the platform in plain air, because a thing read in a worse room reads worse.

The master of the ninth came in person, which he had never done. The boy read him as the tide-line had taught, feet and hands and the set of the neck, and got the reading before the man's mouth opened, and the reading was not the one the tiers would have priced. The tiers had the master of the ninth as appetite on two legs, the smoke-smell of his pits in the word *provision,* a man who bled for coin and had never once bled at a loss. The man who came down the stair was thin under his iron-colored cloak, and his hands were wrong — not still, the way a strong man's hands are still, but *held,* the way hands are held when the body has forbidden them a motion they want. He stood at the six paces and his neck was set like a man facing a doctorand the boy, who had spent two seasons reading floors, read the whole ledger of him in one breath: the yield had come to *his* stones too. His wrong-bloods were thinning. His stock was draining in the bodies, the way the rim-cages drained. The arenas had believed the shortfall was the city's problem, and the city's problem had walked into his own counting-room wearing his own columns, and this man was not here out of greed. He was here the way a drowning man reaches, and he had dressed the reaching in the only grammar his house had ever kept.

The temple sent the under-priest. Not a high voice, not a clay — the man himself, the one who had read the tithe-words to a stone that would not drink, on the count's floor, in front of everyone. The boy had entered him that dark as a man performing, and had watched the third reading crack in his mouth, and had kept the reading. Now the under-priest stood at the six paces with the iron ladle hanging at his belt like a confession, and his eyes were the worst eyes in the tallow light, because they were *certain.* The crack had gone through the rite and found bedrock under it, and the bedrock was this: a man who has spent his life as the arm of a thing that failed in public has exactly two roads — the thing was false, or the thing was *starving* — and the second road is the only one a life can walk down. The rite had not failed. The rite was hungry. The stones had drunk centuries of the spill and the walls had stood, and if the stones would not drink now, then the spill was too small, and the answer to too small was *more,* and more than more, and the under-priest had walked eleven landings down into the pit he had been taught was unclean in order to say so to the only floor that mattered, and the walking itself was the measure of what he believed.

Two men. One afraid his walls were dying. One afraid his life was. The count entered them both, openly, and the entering took the time it took, and the floor stood in the tallow and watched the count read, because that was the count's law and the floor's schooling.

The master of the ninth spoke first, because he had paid for the stair.

"Carrier." The word was flat, correct, unwarmed — the man had learned the count's forms the way merchants learn a harbor's customs. "You've seen the yields. You've seen the rows." A breath. "The houses above have a schedule, and the schedule wants a floor, and there is one floor in Vgrael where a thing can be done in front of everyone and stay done. I'll say the whole of it, because your law likes the whole of things. A tenth. Voluntary, walked, entered in your tablet as freely given — the freed, the owing, the wrong-bloods between feedings, a tenth of the sump's blood, on a schedule, to the stones at the rim, temple ladles, count witness. The seal wants your mark under it, and the seal is worth having: done on this floor, it's a *tithe,* and done anywhere else it's a raid, and there are men above me who will do it as a raid and sleep fine, and I am not one of them, and that is the whole of why I came down the stair myself." The held hands moved once, a half-inch, and were caught. "I am asking you to make it clean."

"And the temple's word," the boy said, in the flat voice.

The under-priest's certainty came forward like a man stepping into cold water. "That the walls are cold, and the blood of the willing is the oldest warmth the walls have ever heard, and that the count —" the ladle brushed his thigh, iron on cloth, a small dead sound — "that the count of all floors knows a willing giving is not a taking. We ask the floor, carrier. Not the masters. The floor. You have taught them to carry things open; let them carry *this* open. A tenth, given by the counted, for the count's own walls, witnessed under the count's own law. There is no theft in it. There is no raid. There is only the city, feeding its own walls with its own blood, the way it has since before the tiers had numbers." He believed it. That was the thing the boy stood with, under the tallow, in the flat hands: the man believed it, the way a man believes the only bridge over the water — not because he has tested it, but because the water is behind him and rising.

The boy let the asking sit like a trough — unlidded, unguarded, until the whole of it showed — and the floor waited, and the silence was the silence of hundreds of bodies who had been taught by two turnings of the count's law that a thing read in silence is read whole.

And the floor answered before the count could.

---

Kade came out of the crowd at the cold-rows with the wrapped hands held up — not raised, *offered*, turned palm-out so the wrappings showed — and behind him, in a line that had formed without any clay calling it, stood thirty of the sump's own. Freed-but-owing. Bled. A woman with a bleeder's flat scars down both forearms, old schedule, healed white. A stair-man with the ninth's smoke-rasp in his breathing. Two of the wrong-bloods from the third-landing cages, gray and docile in the way that was not docility but the shape of a body that has stopped keeping the outside of itself. The line stood behind Kade as water stands behind a broken bank, and it had come on its own, and the boy looked at it and felt the floor of his understanding shift the way a shelf shifts under a weight it always expected.

"You read the rows," Kade said, and the brightness was all the way banked now, banked to a coal that did not need air. "You read them plain. Taken neat, taken settled, nothing owed. That is not the throat's work, carrier, and it is not the stone's work, and you know whose work it is, because your own marked back knows it." The wrapped hands came down and pressed together, a sound like a step in dry leaves. "The eye is moving. The watched are being taken up — *whole accounts, closed.* And the counted have read the two roads the way you taught us to read: taken standing, or given kneeling. A tenth, given. Given *open,* on this floor, under your tablet, with my name entered first." He turned his head and the coal of him lit the line behind him, one face at a time, and the faces were not beaten faces. That was the horror, and it sat in the boy's chest with its full weight while the tallow burned: they were *relieved.* Every face in the line had the look of a body that has found a door out of a room it did not know it could leave. The under-priest's eyes had that look, up at the six paces. So did the master's held hands. The whole city was one reaching, and the count's floor had been taught to read so well that it had read itself into the reaching first.

Signs, in a city built on a tithe, get fed — the count had read that one dark ago on the cold-rows, and here was the feeding, come due with interest: his own floor, first in the city, standing in a clean line to be the shopping list. He had spent two turnings building the one floor where a sign could be read *orderly* — and this was the price of the schooling

Behind the door, the Red had gone very still — ledger-still, mid-column — and the boy did not look at it, because looking would have shown on his face, and his face was the count's face, and the count was about to need it entire.

---

He came down off the platform stone — the count's distance, kept, one step, no more — and he took the reading to the floor in the flat voice, and he did it the only way this floor could be held, which was with the count's own law turned all the way open.

"Maren," he said. "The yields. The rim houses are short a third, and the third is growing. Speak the arithmetic of the tenth. Not the feeling of it. The number."

The broker opened the slate she had been holding like a shield. "A tenth of this floor's blood, run to the stones on the temple's schedule, buys —" and she gave the number in plain darks, and made herself say it twice, because the second saying was the count's way and she had learned it — "buys four darks of warmth at the rim. Four. The shortfall has been running nine. The gap does not close. The gap *narrows by a third,* for four darks, and then the tenth is spent, and the schedule runs again, and the next tenth buys three and a half, because the bodies are the stock, and the stock is thinning with everything else. It is not a wall, carrier. It is a first payment on a debt that grows by feeding it." She closed the slate. "I priced the doctrine as a shopping list. The list is a man eating his own hand to keep the fire lit and calling it fuel."

The tallow hissed. The line behind Kade did not move, but the relief in the faces had gone to work on the number, because the floor could read now, every one of them, two turnings of the count's schooling sitting in them like the cage's old schooling had sat in the boy — and numbers, once spoken plainly, cannot be unspoken. The boy watched the arithmetic land in thirty faces, one at a time, as he had watched frost claims land, and he turned to the under-priest and the master and gave them the same number without mercy and without heat, because mercy and heat were both lids, and the count carried things open.

"You came for a seal," he said. "Here is the count's finding, and it will be entered, and it will be spoken above by every tongue this floor owns. The Provision does not hold the walls. The Provision feeds the shortfall, and the shortfall eats first payments and asks again, and a city that begins bleeding itself into a stone that has stopped drinking will not stop at a tenth, because *the number does not work,* and numbers that do not work do not persuade men to stop — they persuade men to *try harder.* That is the road you are asking me to write my mark under. I will not write it. The count's floor bleeds no one on a schedule whose arithmetic is a lie." The flat voice did not rise. It had never once risen. "But you came to the only floor that reads, so I will give you what reading is *for.* The wanting is real." And he turned to the line, to Kade, to the coal and the wrapped hands, and the hardest sentence of the dark came out of him the way the hardest sentences did, plain as a trough-yield. "The wanting is real, and it is not for blood. It is to stand between the city and the thing at the rim. You are not wrong to want it. You are wrong about the coin."

He gave them the rim-watch.

Not the ladle — the wall. Every volunteer of the line, entered open, first names in the ledger of the new watch: the counted, walking the rim galleries in pairs at the fourth turning of the dark, reading the frost-sweat where it moved, speaking its coming-down to the tablet, dark by dark, gallery by gallery. The only blood the count would take from the floor was the blood of *standing there,* and standing there would cost, and the cost was the point — because a body that wants to be spent will find a place to be spent, and the count's work, the whole two turnings of it, had been to give the wanting a column that closed instead of a stone that did not drink. The line took it. Not all of it — three of the wrong-bloods were led back to the cages gray-faced by men who owed them, and Kade stood a long breath with the wrapped hands pressed together before he bowed his head to the entering — but the line took it, and the tallow light stood on thirty names going into the tablet as *watch,* and the floor breathed, and the holding held.

For that dark.

The master of the ninth read the refusal with his held hands and his set neck, and the boy read him reading it, and what came back off the man was not anger. It was accounting. *You have chosen, and the choice will be owned.* He bowed the arenas' shallow bow, the one that closes nothing, and went up the stair, and every step of him going was a step of the doctrine leaving the count's law and going home to the mobs. The under-priest stayed one breath longer, and looked — of all things — at the tablet, at the thirty names, and said, in the voice of a man on a bridge he now knew was the only one: "They will go to the stones anyway, carrier. Above the gallery. Badly. With ladles." And he touched the iron at his belt once, like a man touching the wound he was about to go and make larger, and followed the master up.

---

Marrow stood the dark-watch at the platform's edge, breathing through the cut, the curled hand flat on the stone.

"Say it," the boy said.

"Two findings." The rasp had its smoke in it, and under the smoke the clear-water eyes. "One: you held the floor, and holding the floor was the right reading, and I watched you do it, and I've watched you since the sixth dark, so I know what it costs you to stand up there and hand thirty wanting bodies a *roster.* That's the one you'll want." The runt shifted his weight off the bad side, the old arena economy of meat and pain. "Two: the broker's number is true and it's the small true. Here's the large one. The rim-sweat moved down a gallery in five darks. The sump is eleven landings deep. You don't need to be the Red to run *that* column, and the city's going to run it too, in its own way, in the streets above, and when the arithmetic of a tenth stops persuading even the men who *want* it to persuade —" the curled hand tapped the stone, once — "they won't stop. They'll reach for the bigger number. And the bigger number isn't a tenth of the sump, boy. It's the sump."

The boy stood with it. The far hunger stood with him, facing the rim, the whole dark long — the belonging engine turned toward the walls like a held lamp, and the city it had found to belong to going cold from the top down, gallery by gallery, level finding its floor, and the boy let the far hunger stand where it stood, because it was the truest thing in him and he had stopped pretending it was not.

The rim-watch came down at the gray light with the first watch-finding, brought by the stair-man with the smoke-rasp, who stood at the platform's foot with frost on the outer side of his cloak and the flat eyes of a man who has stood where he said he wanted to stand and found it answering.

"Top gallery," he said. "Fourth turning. The sweat didn't dry."

He held up a chip of wall-stone, broken off at the watch-line, and the stone in his palm was white to the center, white from the *inside* — and the wrong-cold reached off it across the platform's air, and the boy knew it before the head did, marrow-knowing, older than the tiers — and behind the door the Red looked at the stone as it had looked at the husk on the poles, and what came back through the door was not fear, and it was not the ledger-tone either.

It was the tone of a collector who has finally seen the signature at the bottom of the other hand's work.

*Eleven landings, boy,* the Red said, warm, unhurried, reasonable, out of the gray light with the frost-stone sitting on the platform like a coin come to rest. *Run the column you are so fond of. The sweep reaches this floor in — I could give you the dark. You know I can give it to the dark.* The sinew cut, thin, both temples, the cheapest coin, spent without being asked, the way a clerk lays his knuckle on the desk before the master has finished the sentence. *You held your floor tonight, and it was well held — enter that, it is yours, no one will take it from you. And it bought what buying buys: time. Less time than the city thinks. Less than the broker's number says, because the broker's number counts blood, and blood is not the coin this market is asking for anymore.* The warmth sat back behind the ribs and let the debt accrue in a book no one audits, and spent the whole of the cut on the last of it. *You asked my price once and held the door. I am not asking you to open it, boy. I am only telling you it is the one door left in this city, and the thing at the rim is already climbing your stair. Ask me what the walls cost. You know now that I know it to the drop. And you know — you have known since the goat-child came down on the poles, because you are the best counter this city has ever raised and you have never once been able to stop yourself from finishing a column — you already know whose blood the first line of it is priced in.*

The gray light came down the shaft, gallery by gallery, the one light in Vgrael no ledger had ever priced, and lay on the frost-stone and the tablet and the thirty names, and the boy stood the platform with the flat hands and the door held, both hands of the head, the full old weight — and the door held the knowing back, and the knowing pressed, and the far hunger faced the rim, and the city above the fourth gallery reached in the dark for the bigger number.

And the boy, who had never once in his life been able to stop himself from finishing a column, finished it.

The best blood in Vgrael was behind his ribs.

*End of Chapter 27*
