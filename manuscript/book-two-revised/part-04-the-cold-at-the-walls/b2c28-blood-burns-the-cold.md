# Book Two, Chapter 28: Blood Burns the Cold

The seventh-gallery post did not come down at the gray light, and the rim-watch had a law about that, and the law was the count's own: two bodies, one tablet, one turning, and if the tablet did not come down, the floor did not wait for news. The floor went up and became the news.

The stair-man brought it, smoke-rasp and frost-cloak, standing at the platform's foot with the words coming out of him in the wrong order because the order had stopped mattering to him somewhere on the stairs. The post had gone quiet at the fourth turning. Not fallen quiet — gone quiet, the way a room goes quiet. The pair at that post was Kade's pair. Kade, who had put his name in the watch ledger first of thirty, standing at the rim with the wrapped hands, dark after dark, reading the frost-sweat where it moved. And the stair-man had gone up with two sworn at the mid-dark because that was the count's law too, and he had come back down alone, and what he had seen on the seventh gallery he could not say, only that the wall was wrong and the air was wrong and Kade was standing at the far end of the wrongness still on his feet, and that he, the stair-man, had run.

The count entered it all, openly, at the platform, in front of the floor. Then the count broke its oldest habit.

It went up.

---

He had walked landings before — the tithe-stones, the arena floors, the arenas' shallow courts — but he had never climbed toward the rim with his own sworn behind him, and the city felt it. The stair changed under his feet the way floors changed when a price reset: the freed-but-owing pressed back against the walls to let the count pass, and their eyes did the thing eyes had done since the throat — they looked at him like an answer now, and the answer was not his, and he had stopped expecting it to be. A woman on the fifth landing held a child up to see him go by. Someone behind her was already crying. Above the fourth gallery the tallow got thin on the walls and the air carried the arenas' smoke down and the cold came up to meet them, and it was not the smoke-rasp cold of a working stair. The boy knew it in the marrow before the head could hold it, old past the tiers of any counting: a cold that was not weather. Not the absence of a fire. The absence of a *thing*, walking.

Marrow climbed behind him the whole way, breathing through the cut in the short, counted breaths the boy could read like a slate — one spent, one owed — and the boy did not send him back down, because sending him would have cost more breath than the climb, and because the truth the count's floor never entered was plainer than any tablet: the runt went where the boy went. That was the whole of that column.

The sworn of the watch made the line at the seventh-gallery mouth, six of them with bars and frost on their cloaks, and none of them would stand on the gallery itself, and their not-standing was a reading, and the count took it standing where they had stopped, and looked down the long floor of the seventh gallery, and the column finished itself in his head all at once, the way the worst columns did.

The rim wall was white.

Not frosted. Frost sat on stone the way debt sits on a slate — on the surface, in figures, readable. This was white *in* the stone, white to the center, and the white had a border, and the border was moving. It came down the gallery wall slow as a spill finding its floor, and where it had passed, the tallow-flames on the wall-hooks stood up straight and thin and gave no heat, and the air in front of it was not cold so much as *finished* — air with nothing owed to it, air that had stopped being party to any warmth at all.

The provision had run there. He could read that too, the way he had been taught to read floors by the tide-line and the tiers and two turnings of his own law. Rim-stones at the wall, spaced to a schedule. Troughs. Sacking. The master of the ninth's short-corded freed, run up from the floors between feedings, and the stone laid open to take them. The blood was there in the troughs and on the stone's lip, and the blood was gray.

All of it. Gray, and dry, and settled — blood with the blood gone out of it, the way the husks were bodies with the body gone out of them. The stone had not drunk. The stone had been *drunk from.*

The kneeling line of the provision was still kneeling. Eleven of them, neat, dry, nothing owed, nothing owing, arranged along the wall exactly as the schedule had arranged them, and the boy read each one in the old order — feet, then hands, then the set of the neck — and the necks were settled, and the reading came back the same for all eleven: taken whole. Account closed. Above them, at the watch-post, Kade's second lay the same at his tablet, and the tablet's last line was cut off mid-word.

And at the far end of the gallery, where the white had not yet reached, Kade stood against the wall on his feet, and the wrapped hands were held out in front of him, palms out, offered, the way he had offered them once at the cold-rows — and the white border stood off from him the width of a floor-stone, and did not come on, and did not leave. It stood. It stood the way a thing stands that has time and no need, and the boy's whole skin understood before any column ran: the Cold did not take Kade because Kade was not what it had come for. It had left him the way a hand leaves a hold that is not the one it wants. It was keeping the floor.

Behind the door, the Red stood all the way up, and this time the boy did not make it wait, and it did not pretend to be still.

*There,* it said, and there was nothing of the ledger in it, nothing of the patient accruing tone; this was the tone from the frost-stone dark, the collector's tone, and under the collector's something older, something that had been waiting longer than the city had existed to wait. *There it is, boy. Do you see it now? Do you see what it is holding back from your spent man, and why?* The warmth came forward against the door until the door creaked in him like a timber taking weight. *It knows me. Look at the border. Look where it stopped. Eleven of theirs it closed without slowing. Yours it will not touch at any price, because yours is the one coin in this whole gray market it cannot counterfeit, cannot drink, cannot allow. Ask me the word for a thing like that, boy. You have been carrying the word since the throat. Fire and poison, boy. To that —* and the warmth turned in him like a man turning to face a door he had waited a life at, *— to that, you are fire and poison both.*

The boy stood at the gallery mouth, and the count's law ran itself in him the way it ran on the floor below: *Speak what is. Name what is not. Aloud.* And what was, was this: the wall coming down a gallery's width a dark, faster than the sweep arithmetic had priced; eleven closed and one held; the rim-stones drunk dry backward; and the one floor in Vgrael that read things standing at the top of its own stair with the door creaking and the answer to the whole book of the tithe one cut away from being spoken in blood.

The under-priest was there.

He was kneeling at the dry stone with the iron ladle in both hands, and he had been kneeling, by the look of the stiffness in him, since before the stair-man ran. He had come up to run the provision — the boy read that off him the way he read floors, and the man did not hide it, because the man was past hiding anything — the arenas had needed a temple hand to make the spill lawful, and the under-priest had walked up the eleven landings he had once walked down, and had given the blood of the willing to the stone, dark after dark, because the rite was hungry and the answer to too small was more. And the stone had not drunk. And the wall had. The man's certainty had not broken. That was the horror the boy stood with, in the thin tallow, at the rim of everything: the certainty had *turned.* It lay on the man's neck like a hand, and it was aimed now down the gallery, at the boy, at the door, at the cut not yet made, and the under-priest knelt there holding the ladle the way a man holds the rope of a bell he has already decided to ring.

"Carrier," the under-priest said, and his voice was gentle, and the gentleness was the worst sound on the gallery. "The walls are cold, and the stone will not drink, and I have bled it lawful to the last drop of my learning. And the Cold stands off from *you.*" The ladle shifted in the held hands, a half-inch. "The doctrine was true. All this time, all of it — the tithe, the warmth, the walls. True, and starved, and priced wrong from the first century, because the blood the walls were built to hear was never the blood of the willing. It was *this.*" His eyes did not waver. He was not asking. He was on the bridge, and the bridge was the boy's veins, and he had already started across. "Give it to the wall. Once. Witnessed. I will enter it myself."

Behind the door the Red said nothing at all, which was the most fluent thing it had ever said, because the boy and the Red, for once in the whole life of the door, wanted the same cut — and that frightened him more than the gallery, and he entered the fright, openly, in his head, in the count's own voice, before he made the choice: *Both of us want it. Mark that. That is a finding.*

---

He set the terms before he set the knife, aloud, because law that was not spoken was not law.

"Marrow." The flat voice carried down the gallery without rising. "The count's terms, this once, spoken here so the walls hold them. If I stop answering to my name, you call me back the caller's way — the relic, the stone, the three speakings — and if I do not come back, the terms Asha holds are the terms. Sworn?"

"Sworn," Marrow said, and then, because he was Marrow, in the rasp with the smoke and the clear water under it: "And the third term, boy. Say the third one."

"Bind or kill," the boy said. "Before following the monster. Say it whole."

"Say it whole," the runt said. "Bind or kill before following the monster." The curled hand was flat on the gallery stone. The counted breaths had stopped being counted. "I'm here. Cut."

The boy drew the count's knife — the small one, the one that had opened every clay and every reckoning since the first floor — and he opened his own forearm, low and long, the way a man opens a vein he means to price, and he walked forward past the sworn, past the stair-man, onto the gallery floor that no living thing had stood on since the fourth turning, and he held the arm out over the white, and he let it fall.

The drop came down through the gray tallow light, red as red, his.

And the white went out.

Not melted. Not driven. *Ended* — a hand's circle of it, stone-colored again under the drop, warm to the sight the way a wound is warm, and the border drew back off it all along the line, quick, the whole width of the gallery flinching back a floor-stone's width with a sound the boy felt in his teeth rather than heard: a sound like a ledger closing in another room. The tallow-flames on the hooks stood wide for the first time and gave heat. The air let go of its finished stillness and became cold air, common cold, weather cold, nothing but cold.

Behind the door, the Red laughed — low, and short, and wordless — the old laugh from the throat, and under the laugh the boy felt it spending, and it let him feel the spending, it *itemized* the spending for him as it went, and that was new, that was a thing it had never done, each drop priced as it left:

*That one is a dark of the cage,* it said, warm, exact, almost kind. *That one is the sound the sister made, counting. Hold still. This next is small — only the smell of her hands.*

"Kade," the boy said, and walked the line.

He walked the border the way he had once walked tide-lines, reading where the white ran deepest, and everywhere his blood fell the white went out before it, and the gallery came back floor by floor behind him, ordinary stone, ordinary cold. It was not fighting. That was the finding he had not let himself expect: the Cold did not fight him anywhere along the line. It ended, and ended, and drew back, and the drawing back had a direction — not away from the blood. *Toward* the blood. Every border in the wall leaned to face him as it died, the whole rim-side of the gallery turning like a field of iron filings finding the lodestone, and the boy understood, walking, bleeding, that he was not driving it out. He was being *read.* The Cold had come down eleven landings through the city's blood and stopped at the seventh, and held a floor, and waited — not for Kade, not for the watch, not for the provision. It had come to see the signature. Now it had the signature, whole, priced to the drop, and it was withdrawing with the information it had spent eleven galleries to buy, and the Red behind the door stood at his shoulder and watched it go with the stillness of a collector watching the other house's man leave the auction, and neither of them, boy or Red, could have said which of them the thought belonged to, and that was the finding under all the findings, and he carried it open.

He reached Kade at the far wall. The wrapped hands were still out. Up close the man was gray with more than frost, and the coal of him had burned down to its ash, and his eyes on the boy's face were the eyes of a man who had stood inside the answer to his own wanting and found it standing off from him, keeping him, patient, with nothing owed.

"It wouldn't take me," Kade said. The voice was the size of a coin. "Carrier. It wouldn't take me."

"It kept you," the boy said. "There is a difference, and I will enter it, and you will stand in the row while I enter it, alive. That is the count's finding." He laid his uncut hand over the wrapped ones and pressed them down, closed, finished. "You stood your watch. The turning is entered."

Behind him, along the dead border, the gallery lay in its own ordinary dark, warm where he had bled — warm in lines, low-shaped, the only warm lines on the rim-side of the fourth gallery — and at the mouth of it the sworn were coming forward now with the tallow, and the stair-man was on his knees, and the under-priest stood at the dry stone with the ladle hanging forgotten from one hand and both eyes full of the boy's blood running back down the white in thin, ending, red lines, and the certainty on his neck had finished turning. The boy read the new doctrine off him before the man's mouth opened, whole, the way you read weather: *the tithe was true; the coin was wrong; the carrier bleeds; the walls will hear him.* A religion being born ten paces away, out of his forearm, in front of him, and no count in the world that could enter it faster than the man's face would broadcast it.

---

Maren came up two darks later with the wall-readings and did not waste a breath on the climbing. She stood at the border where the boy's lines ran warm and put her palm flat on the stone, and took it off, and looked at her palm.

"It holds," she said. "Gallery eight, gallery seven, your lines, still warm, while everything either side of them sweats. I priced it, because pricing is what I'm for." She did not open the slates; the numbers were already in her. "Two readings, carrier, and they are the same reading, and that is the whole of the trouble. One: a ward that *walks.* A living wall that can be carried from tier to tier, that ends the Bloodless on contact, that the rim-watch can be re-formed around — you have just become the only military asset in this city that the Cold cannot price. Two: a beacon that burns. Every white border in Vgrael leaned toward you last night — the rim-watch saw it in the top galleries, the whole wall turning like grass before a wind. A thing that draws what it ends. Fire, and every moth in the world." She put her palm back on the warm stone, briefly, the way you check a fever. "The same blood. The same reading. The master of the ninth will make an offer by mid-dark. The temple will make a rite. The houses will make a cage. And the Cold —" and for the first time since the stair-man's frost-cloak, the broker's voice found something it could not level, and she let it be unlevel, because the count's law was the count's law — "the Cold will make a plan. You are the prize and the target, carrier. You always were. It's just written plain now."

The boy stood at the border with his forearm bound, and the far hunger faced the rim the way it had faced it for darks uncounted — the lamp held out toward the walls of the city it had chosen — and the boy let it stand where it stood.

Marrow came to him at the gray light, at the gallery mouth, where the stairs went down to the count's floor and the floor would be waiting for the telling. The runt stood in the thin light with the counted breaths running again, one spent, one owed, and looked at the bound arm a long time.

"One finding," Marrow said.

"You keep a two-finding mouth."

"One finding," the runt said again, "and the second one I'm not saying, because you already know it, and because it's not mine to say up here in the cold with the tallow listening." He looked — of all things — at the boy's hands. "Say the first one with me, boy, so it's said right. The blood ends the Cold. Say it plain. It's true. I watched it. It's true, and it's yours, and no one gave it to you, and no one can take it from you." The curled hand came up and tapped the boy's chest, once, over the door. "Now hear the part I'll say. Everything that thing in there has ever wanted, it has wanted in coin you could count. Blood. Memory. Names. Standing. Tonight it wanted a thing and got it, and the coin it spent was the smell of her hands — I heard it price them, boy, I was standing right there — and it paid that coin *gladly*, and it itemized the paying so you'd hear every digit, and that is not a spender. That's a buyer showing a customer how easy the next purchase is." The hand came down. "That's my finding."

The boy said the finding after him, flat, aloud, the count's way: the blood ends the Cold; the Red paid gladly, and priced the paying so the count would hear.

Then he took out the tablet, because the last thing had to be entered before the floor told it differently, and he wrote it there in the gray light at the top of his own city, and he did not dress it, because dressing was a lid.

The cuts had closed. The Red's way — fast, whole, seamless, the skin closed over each low before the next low opened. And around every closed cut, a hand's width of skin, the skin was cold. Not cool. Cold the way the wall-stone had been cold, marrow-cold, an hour after the bleeding, two hours, still cold, and the boy put his own palm on the place and held it there and read it the way he read everything, feet first, hands, the set of the thing, and the reading came back, and he entered it in the flat voice, aloud, alone, at the gallery mouth, so that it would be in the count's own hand before any other hand in Vgrael could hold it:

*Findings, spoken plain. The blood of the carrier ends the Cold on contact. The doctrine of the tithe was true, and was priced wrong from the first century. The Cold has read the carrier's blood, whole, and withdrew with the reading. The count's own cuts run cold, and hold their cold, and do not warm.*

He stood there while the light came down the shaft, gray and unpriced, and below him the city was already climbing toward its new arithmetic — the arenas, the temple, the houses, all of them reaching now for the one number that worked, the one warm line on the rim of the world — and above him the walls sweated and the Cold walked away with his signature in its keeping, and the cold sat in his own skin, small, patient, local, his.

*End of Chapter 28*
