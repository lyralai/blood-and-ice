# Book Two, Chapter 30: The Choice at the Walls

The knife did not warm in his hand all the way down.

That was the first finding, and he made it in the body first, the head only coming to hold it later, stair by stair, landing by landing: the cold in the hilt had a *set* to it, the same set as the border on his forearm, and it did not come from the air. The air of the stair was warm — tallow-smoke, packed bodies, the freed-but-owing pressed back to the stone to let the count pass down — and the knife stayed cold against his palm like a thing held apart from all that warmth, listening to something below.

The border on his forearm ran toward the elbow, and the knife ran with it. He entered the finding on the fourth landing, standing, in the flat voice, and did not slow: *The cold in the blade is mine. It goes where the border goes. Ask later what that makes the arm.*

Maren kept his pace two steps behind, and Marrow kept hers behind Maren, and neither spoke, and the stair emptied ahead of them the way water empties off a tilted stone.

The watch-post at the fourth turning of the third gallery was standing. That was the wrong of it. The post had not come down — the man had not come down — and he stood in the arch of it with his back to them and his spear upright and his stillness was the finished kind, the kind the boy had first read on the seventh gallery in eleven kneeling necks, and the air in the archway had nothing owed to it.

"Dead," Maren said, flat, a reading.

"No," the boy said, because the count had already read the whole shape. "Kept."

He crossed the gallery floor to the man slowly, the way he had learned to cross the dens of sleeping things, and the man did not turn, and the boy came around him by degrees, and the eyes were open. That was the whole of the good news. The eyes were open and they were aimed at nothing and the breath went in and out of the man in long, even strokes, unhurried, unasked, and the skin of the face had gone gray-tallow pale, cold to look at, the way the trough-blood had been cold to look at on the seventh.

The man's chest was warm. The boy laid his palm flat there, the wall-reading, and the heart under the palm ran slow and even and *held*, and the wrongness of it came up through his own arm before any word arrived: the man was not dying. The man was being kept the way the wall kept water — held, without being used.

And past the man, past the arch, the gallery floor went white.

Not a border. The boy stood in the arch and let the count run it, edge to edge, wall to wall, and the count came back and he made himself enter it twice because the first entry shook: the white at the third gallery was not the rim-white, not a hand's-width leaned in from the stones. It was deep. Joined from below, up through the floor itself, a column of it, standing in the stone the way the warm lines stood in the seventh gallery — a body of white with its own bones in it, and along the whole face of it, where it met the gallery air, the surface was still. Not leaning. Not coming.

Standing off. The way the Cold had stood off from Kade.

*Findings,* he entered, and the hand was steady and the steadiness still cost. *The post kept, not taken. The white deep, not leaned. It is not feeding here. It is holding what it holds, and it is waiting, and I know what it waits for, because I taught this city to wait for me.*

It had heard him coming. That was in the floor, in the set of the stillness. It had made a floor of quiet and kept one man like a lamp in a window, and everything in the arch was arranged toward the stair, toward the descending feet, toward him — the warm signature it had walked away with on the seventh gallery, invited down.

"Carrier," Maren said quietly, at his shoulder. The slates were in her hands and she had not opened them. "Price it."

He priced it. Two drops ended a hand's-width of border-white. He stood in the arch and made the count run the depth of what stood in the floor below him, gallery-length and stone-deep, and the arithmetic closed in him the way a ledger closes when the last line is ruled — all at once, not read, totaled.

Two drops would not end this. Twenty would not end this. The schedule that had held six galleries and a city's leaning had met its first column that did not run at the rate of his holding, and the meeting place was here, three tiers under his rim, with one kept man standing in the arch like a coin laid out on a counted stone.

"Up," he said. "All of us. Now. It does not move while it waits, and I will not teach it what I do when I am made to hurry."

---

They came to him at the rim, in the tallow light, and the boy looked at what the dark had brought to his floor and understood that he was holding council, and that he had never once held a thing so heavy by such a thin name.

Maren with her slates shut. Marrow with the counted breaths running one-spent, one-owed. The under-priest, who had not left, whose ladle hung at his belt like a rope, whose patience had been standing on this floor for six darks. Asha-kel at the stair-mouth, sent up by the houses the way a cord is sent up a wall, the leash-terms in her flat hands. The master of the ninth's clean writing, which had been sitting sealed on the alcove shelf for four darks. Brannoc on the settled stone with his clouded eyes aimed at the floor, feeling three galleries down through what the air did. Lorren standing behind the blind man's shoulder like a margin.

And behind the door, the Red, with its books already open.

*Let me say it first,* the Red said, before the boy had finished the count of the room, warm, level, and beneath the level something leaning. *It saves time, and time is one of the things you are about to be short of. The white below you is not a wall, boy. It is a floor. It has stopped pretending to climb, because it does not need the tiers. It needs what the tiers stand on, and it has set its price where you cannot help but pay it: come down, or watch what it keeps. You have one column that ends it and the column is your arm, and the depth below the third gallery will run your arm dry to the shoulder and past it. You have seen the ledger. You know the rate.* The warmth moved along the inside of the door, unhurried. *So do not pay. Take the runt, take the broker, take the bled who can walk, and go out the tide-flats in the dark. You know the route. You priced it on the second landing — I felt you price it. The city leans, boy. Let it learn what it leaned on. What falls when you are gone is not yours. Nothing here was ever yours. It is theirs, and their arithmetic is not your column.*

The boy stood still and let it finish, because law that was not spoken was not law, and because the count's law cut both ways: what is, spoken plain, and the Red's wanting was a finding too.

And then he entered the worst finding of the dark, the one he had carried up eleven landings without letting it into his hand:

The Red's wanting and his own wanting were not the same wanting. But they wanted the same door.

He had priced the route down. That was true. On the second landing his feet had done it without asking him — the tide-flats, the third turning past the sump, Marrow's pace against the dark's length, how many of the bled could walk and how many could not. The body had run it the way the body ran everything, fast, first, and underneath price. And under the price, in the place where the shelf had always sat, was the shape of a den far away and a fire no one leaned on, and the shape was warm, and he had stood in the arch of the third gallery with a kept man's gray face in front of him and wanted — with the whole animal wanting, the old wanting, the wanting from before the count had a name — *out.*

*Findings,* he entered, and made the hand write it. *The boy wants the door. The Red wants the same door for its own reasons. Entered apart, because the count is not the Red and the Red is not the boy, and any ledger that closes those two columns into one is a temple.*

Then he said it aloud, to the floor, because the floor was the council and the council had a right to the count's worst line: "I want to go. That is entered. Now price the other side."

"Carrier," the under-priest said, and the gentleness in it had stopped being a softness days ago; it was a hand on a rope. "The third gallery—"

"The third gallery is not a wall," the boy said. "It is a mouth held open. It will keep what it keeps and it will not spend it, and down the darks it will take the fourth the same way, and the fifth, and the tiers between will lean on nothing, and the leaning is what I built. If the count runs, the walls hold the people and the people hold the walls. If the count runs, the rim runs. That was always the arithmetic. The Cold has finally read it back to me: it did not come back for the floors." He looked at the floor of his own gallery, at the warm lines, at the rows. "It came back for the keeping."

Brannoc's blind face had been aimed down since the council began. Now the old hands came up off the knees, and the boy watched them make the asking-gesture, and gave the forearm again, and the fingers walked the cold border in the tallow light, and every face at the council watched the reading the way the city watched the count come down a stair.

"Deeper than yesterday," Brannoc said. "Past the elbow's shadow. It runs at the rate of the holding — you have priced that." The fingers stopped. Stood. "Now hear the other half, boy, the half I cut shallow: the rate of the holding is not the Cold's rate. It is yours. Six held the rim, and the six set their own rates, and the ledger took what the rates gave. The mark that stops in the middle of the line does not stop at a depth the Cold chose." The old hand lay flat over the border, light as a laid stylus. "It stops where the carrier stops holding."

The council was quiet. Lorren spoke into the quiet, copyist's voice, small and dry: "The third entry. Held the rim eleven turns, and the turns are entered — a third fewer feedings than the second, in the same span. The column thins as it goes down. Six entries, and every one thinner than the one before it. I have read that column a hundred times and called it a decay." The ink-stained hands pressed flat to the thighs. "It is not a decay. It is a schedule, drawn by the ones who held. They learned to hold *less.* Turn by turn. And it was not enough, and it was the only thing they ever tried, because no one ever read the column standing, and no one ever had a city to read it with."

The boy looked at the two of them, carver and copyist, nine hundred years of buried endings standing on his floor, and the count closed the total before the head could dress it: every carrier before him had held alone, had priced alone, had thinned their own giving alone in the dark, one arm against one wall, and the wall had always outlasted the arm. The column was open at the bottom of the clay because the column had only ever had one kind of entry.

One kind of arm.

"No," the boy said.

Maren's head came around.

"Not alone," the boy said. "That is the choice, and I am making it here, with witnesses, because a choice made in the dark is only a wanting, and I am done entering wantings as law. Here is the count." And he gave it to them flat, the whole of it, the way he had opened the bleeding-books on the rim what felt like a lifetime ago: the hold at the third gallery, this dark, this once. Not drops. An arm's price, spent by the count, willingly, witnessed, lawful — down the middle of what stood in the floor below, one burning line through the deep white, to break its standing and teach it the rim has an arm and the arm has a city. And around the burn, before and while and after: the rows. Not leaning. *Holding.* The freed of the ninth in the watch-rows at the fourth turning, taken into the wall-law as the price of the ninth's schedule — the master's walls held first, the master's men spent first. The temple's ladle for the contained rite — measured, witnessed, distributed to the rows that held, blood to the bled that stood the stair, that the leaning city might learn what leaning is *for*. The houses' rite — he looked at Asha-kel, at the sealed clay, at the flat caller's patience — deferred. "Tell the houses the leash is declined at this price. If they want the carrier's blood lawful, they can stand in a row for it like everyone else in Vgrael, and be entered in the count, and owe what a row owes. Their dish stays sealed or it goes back. Those are terms. They may not be terms the houses recognize. They are terms the count enters."

Asha-kel looked at him a long moment, and the flat face did a small thing, the smallest thing, and she said: "The houses will call that a war sentence."

"The houses can read the third gallery," the boy said. "The sentence was not mine."

And the under-priest — the boy watched it happen, watched the certainty that had turned once on the seventh gallery turn again, and the turning was quieter than the first one, and deeper — the under-priest unhooked the ladle from his belt with both hands, and set it on the gallery stone, and stood back from it, and his eyes were wet, and his voice did not shake.

"Contained," he said. "Witnessed. I will pour it myself, carrier. I have spent my life standing where the floor could watch me stand. Let me stand where the floor can watch me pour."

The Red had been silent through all of it, and the silence was the old silence, the listening one, and when it spoke at last it did not argue. That was the finding under the finding, and the boy entered it even as the warmth came forward against the door:

*An arm's price,* the Red said. *Spent down the standing column. Witnessed, lawful, in front of the whole leaning city.* The tone was level, and exact, and something in it had come off its hinge somewhere and was hanging open. *Boy. Do you understand what you have built here? Six of them thinned their arms alone in the dark because no one ever told them the price could be shared. You have just taught the ledger a new line of entry. I have run it three times since the priestling sat the ladle down.* The warmth laid itself along the inside of the door, the full length of it, like a man laying his whole arm along a wall he means to hold. *The columns balance. Boy — the columns balance, and there is nothing spent on nothing, and I have never once, in the whole life of this door, held a thing I did not price first.* A pause. The door creaked once under the leaning warmth. *I will hold your arm in the burn. All of it. Nothing wasted, and none of it more than you set. That is my entry. It is the first one I have ever made with the spending written by another hand. Price that how you want.*

*I will,* the boy entered. *After. Now hold the door and keep the count off my arm while the count runs. That is the row you stand in tonight.*

Behind the door, for the first time in the whole life of the door, the Red stood in a row, and held, and was quiet.

---

The third gallery was worse in the light of forty tallow lamps than it had been in the dark.

The rows stood at the fourth turning: freed of the ninth, two deep, the short-corded and the long, and their fear came off them in the cold air like the warmth off the white lines, and they stood anyway, because the count had spoken the wall-law down their lengths and the wall-law was the only law in Vgrael that had ever entered them as anything but a price. Behind the rows, the under-priest with the ladle. At the stair-mouths, Marrow, one-spent, one-owed, and Kade at the deep stair with his wrapped hands loose and his bright eyes aimed downward at nothing, watching for the shape only he knew.

The kept man still stood in the arch. They had not been able to move him. His breath still ran, long and even and unhurried, and the boy had entered it — *the kept keep* — and set two of the freed to stand one to each side of the man, hands on his arms, holding him upright in the world while the world settled what he was kept for.

The boy stood at the edge of the deep white with his sleeve off and the small knife in his cold hand, and he did not begin with the knife.

He began with the count. Aloud. Flat. His own voice carrying down the gallery the way it had carried down the stair, the way the old line of sound had carried across the fires of a green so far away it might have been another ledger's column: what was about to be spent, and on what, and at what price entered, and what the price would buy — this dark, this once, this holding. And the rows breathed with the numbers, and the numbers steadied them, and the boy understood, entering it even as he spoke, that this was the wall the Cold had truly come down to break.

He opened the forearm along the old border-line, and gave the Red the arm.

The burn went into the white like a held line goes into a tide-flat: not fast, not slow, held. He stood in the middle of the gallery floor and the blood ran gray-tallow red off the arm and fell where the count had aimed it, and where it fell the white ended, and the ending ran out ahead of him floor-stone by floor-stone, and the pain was the old pain, the clean kind, the kind with a set to it — and the Red held the arm under the burn the whole way, and the boy felt the holding, and it was perfect, and the perfection was the horror of it: the care was flawless, and the thing giving the care had spent his life's warmest room to buy the right to give it.

Deeper. The white ran deeper than the seventh gallery's ever had, standing bodies of it under the floor-stones, and the count paid and paid, and somewhere in the middle of the paying the Red's voice came, warm, exact, itemized, and did not stop the burn:

*This next is small. Only the green's first dark — the wet dark, the sound of the rain on the leaves that first night, the way it came down on you alone. Only that. Entered.*

And the rain-sound went out of him. He felt it go the way he had felt the door close on the smell of her hands — a room in him going dark and settled, the account closed clean, nothing left in it to draw — and he did not stop the count, and his voice did not break, and the not-breaking was a finding he would enter at the gray light and did not enter now, because now was the wall, and the wall took everything and gave it a place to stand.

At the deep of the burn, at the fourth turning's stones, the white made its stand. It stopped ending. The blood fell and the white held, and the boy heard the rows behind him take the fear into their breathing — and he did not turn, and he did not spend more. He held the line he had, one arm's priced length, and lifted his voice into the count again, flat, carrying:

"Rows. The wall-law. Hold."

And the rows held. The freed of the ninth set their shoulders at the fourth turning and did not come off it, and the under-priest poured the contained rite down the line of them, measured, witnessed, and the ladle's small warmth went into the bled like the count's numbers into the stair, and Marrow's one-spent-one-owed ran underneath everything like a heartbeat laid along the floor. And the white — the boy watched it, and made himself enter what he watched — the white ended. Not under his blood only. Under the standing. The rim had an arm, and the arm had a city, and the standing white at the fourth turning met the both of them together and closed like a column closes, all at once, totaled.

The Cold went out of the third gallery the way it had come in: whole. Unhurried. Not spent, not beaten — withdrawn, down through the floor it had come up by, taking its deep white with it stone by stone, and the kept man in the arch sat down all at once like a cut cord, alive, breathing his own breath, wet-faced, held up by the two freed who had stood him in the world.

And on the floor of the gallery, where the standing white had been deepest, and where it had gone down last, there remained one line of white.

Thin. Deliberate. A hand's-length, no more — a single stroke lying in the stone at the middle of where the body of it had stood, facing up, facing the rim, facing him.

The boy stood over it a long time, bleeding his priced blood into the dressing the Red held closed along the arm, cold past the elbow now, cold into new country, and he read the stroke the way he read all marks, whole shape first.

It was not a border. It was not a keep.

It was an entry.

*Findings,* he entered at the gray light, at the rim, with Marrow's curled hand flat on the new cold and Maren writing the figures and the tallow burning down, and the words came the flattest and the slowest of anything the count had ever set down: *The third gallery held. This dark. This once. The price is entered: the border past the elbow; the green's first dark, spent. The holding was not the Red alone. Entered plain: the holding was never the Red alone. — And below the fourth turning the Cold left a mark, and the mark was made, and the mark was kept, and it did not end under the blood. It stops in the middle of the line. I have seen that mark before. I have seen it in clay nine hundred years old.* The stylus stood, and the hand did not shake, and the not-shaking was entered too. *The count is no longer the only thing in this city that keeps books. Something down there has learned to write. And its first entry in the stone is my mark, cut shallow.*

"One finding," Marrow said.

"Say it."

The runt's counted breaths ran their line, one spent, one owed, and the curled hand pressed flat over the new country of the cold, over the elbow, into the sleeve's shadow.

"You held the walls tonight," Marrow said. "Every wall but the one it was holding for you. Six read the column lying down." The bright eyes came up. "It left your mark in the floor. Price what it is asking."

Behind the door, the Red was silent — the entered quiet, the ledger-quiet of a thing whose columns balanced — and out past the rim, past the cold horizon, the gray light came up on the water, and under the light, far off, where no lanterns had ever stood, lanterns stood.

*End of Chapter 30*
