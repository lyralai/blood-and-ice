# Book Two, Chapter 26: The Tithe Fails

The blood ran thin on the ninth dark after the cord was cut, and it ran thin in the plain sight of the whole sump, and nobody had a column for it.

It happened at the third feeding of the wrong-bloods — the old schedule, the one the bleeding-houses had kept since before the count was a word in anyone's mouth. A Mure cow-hulk from the rim-cages, a big gray thing, docile the way the long-bled are docile, was put to the stone the way it had been put to the stone twice a dark for a year. The cutter worked the old cut. The trough filled in the trough's usual while. And when the trough came up, it came up short — a hand's width short, the red lying shallow at the bottom of it, thinner than the cutter had ever seen blood lie, thin the way the last of a thing lies in the bottom of a pot that has been scraped.

The cutter read theft. The cutter was arena-bred, and the arena's answer to a short trough was a broken hand, and the big gray thing stood there and took the reading without moving, because the long-bled take everything.

The count read otherwise.

The boy stood the platform stone and read the trough the way he read all things that came into the tallow light: whole, in plain air, before the head was allowed its easier answer. The cut was good. The time was kept. The body on the stone was the same body. The blood was less, and thinner, and the thinning was not in the cut or the keeping. The thinning was in the blood.

And the stone under the cut — the rim-stone's flat black face, worn smooth by a year of troughs — the stone had a new look on it. The old look was the look of a thing drinking. The new look was the look of a thing drinking and not filling. He had no better way to hold it, and he held it, and behind the door the Red held it too, and for once the two of them read a thing the same: *less in, and more asked.*

"Run the other troughs," he said, in the flat voice, from the platform. "Every bleeding-floor under the count. Same dark, same cut, same bodies. Speak the yields to the tablet, not to each other. Speak what is."

They ran them. That was the night the count learned what the rim had been telling it for three turnings in the only grammar the rim had: quantity.

---

The yields came in at the gray light, and Maren brought them down herself, which was the first bad sign, because Maren sent clerks for arithmetic and came herself only for findings.

The broker stood at the platform's foot with the slates and did not open them.

"Every floor is short," she said. "Not by a hand's width — by that much and less, and less again the higher the tier. The bleeding-houses above the fourth gallery are short a tenth. The rim-side houses" — and there the clerk's voice did a thing the boy had never heard it do, which was to stop and choose — "the rim-side houses are short a third. A third, carrier. My grandmother's grandmother kept those books, and the yield has never moved a drop in any column anyone kept. Blood is the one price this city thought was fixed."

"You ran it twice."

"Three times. I paid the third run out of the count's stores so no house could say the number was ours." The slates came open then, and the columns were plain, and the plainness was the horror of them: row on row, the same cut, the same dark, the same slow going-down. "I'll say the finding before you say it for me, because I'd rather be the one holding it. This is not theft. Theft moves. Theft shows up somewhere, in a jar, in a route, in a boy's pocket. This is *absence.* The blood is in the bodies, and the bodies are bled on time, and the blood is less." The eyes came up, working-tier flat. "There's a word above the fourth gallery for what you're going to do about it. The word is *nothing,* and it's the most expensive word in the city this turning, because everyone above me is about to decide it isn't theft either, and then they're going to need a thing instead of a yield, and the only thing this city has ever reached for when the yield runs short—"

"Is a body," the boy said.

"Is a body," Maren said, and closed the slates. "I came down at the gray light to say it plainly to the one counter in Vgrael who writes what he sees. Whatever comes down the shaft next will be dressed as doctrine. Read it as a shopping list."

---

The husk came down the twelfth dark.

It came on a stretcher of lashed poles, carried by two rim-watchmen who would not carry it past the third landing, so the sworn brought it the last stretch, and the sump came out of its sleep the way a body comes out of sleep when the foot finds a dead thing at the bottom of the bed — all at once, and without any noise at all.

It was a herder. Rim-side, gray-cloaked, one of the small men who kept the goat-lines above the last gallery, known to the watch, owed by no one, owning nothing. That was the first thing the count entered, because the count entered everything: a man with no column. No debt, no faction, no use. The city's whole arithmetic had no reason to touch him.

He was neat. That was the word the body found and would not put down. The dead the boy had known — the green's dead, the arena's dead, the fire's dead — were violent with their dying, torn or spilled or burned, the death written on the outside in the body's own language. This man was not torn. Not spilled. Not marked anywhere, not a bruise, not a cut, not the rope's old ring. He lay straight, hands at the sides, face arranged in the plain expression of a man asleep, and he was *empty.* Not bloodless the way the drained are bloodless, the throat's work, the trough's work. Bloodless the way a column is bloodless when it has been carried out whole: nothing taken through any door. Nothing taken at all. The blood simply not in him, and the body not rotted, and the dark of him under the gray cloak dry as old paper, and the sump stood around the stretcher in its hundreds and breathed through its teeth.

The boy came down off the platform — the count's distance, kept — and read him in the old order the tide-line had taught and no tier had changed: feet, then hands, then the set of the neck.

And the wrong-cold answered.

It came up out of his own marrow to meet the thing on the stretcher, the old recognition, the island's oldest knowing: the cold that is not temperature. He had felt it once as a boy in a green that would not touch him, in a wreck's flooded hold where the water was colder than the water. It had no smell. It had no shape on the air. It was an *absence with a border*, and the border was the shape of the man it had been, and the body knew it the way the body knows a predator's print in mud it has never seen before — not from learning. From older than learning.

The prologue of him — the room with the bleeding-doors, the marks older than the city, the three carriers who walked into the Cold — rose up whole behind his eyes and stood there, and he let it stand, because it was not a memory now. It was a documentation.

Behind the door, the Red looked.

It had not asked to look. It simply turned its attention down through his eyes the way a clerk leans over a shoulder, and it read the husk top to bottom in one long breath, and what came back through the door was not fear. He would have known how to hold fear. What came back was worse, because it was familiar to him from his own ledger-work: it was the tone of a counter who has found a second hand keeping books in his store.

*Neat,* the Red said, in the ledger tone, warm, unhurried. *No spill, no spoil, no waste. Whoever drew him took the whole account in one motion and left the ledger-shell standing for us to find.* A pause, filed, not felt. *You are looking at collection, boy. You are looking at another collector's craft. I have run this city's books for two seasons from inside your ribs, and I did not know this collector's hand was on the floor above mine. I find I mind it. That is a new finding too.*

"Be still," the boy said, under the tallow, in the flat voice, low enough that the sworn read it as reading, and the Red went still, and the stillness had its ledger open.

Kade was at the edge of the tallow light with the wrapped hands and the brightness banked down to its coals, and the boy watched the devotee's face do its work on the husk the way it did its work on everything: rebuilding the thing into faith, stone by stone, in the eyes. A man emptied whole and left neat as scripture. He could see Kade's followers arriving at the reading the young ones always arrived at: *the eye is moving. The watched are being taken up.* He could see it because it was easier than the other reading, and bodies take the easier reading the way water takes the lower ground, and the count had two turnings, maybe three, before the husks stopped being a horror to his floor and became a *sign* — and signs, in a city built on a tithe, get fed.

Maren stood at his shoulder with the slates closed, and said, quietly, the working-tier's whole theology in one line: "One is a mystery. Two is a cost. Three is a market." She looked at the neat gray face on the poles. "There will be three."

There were three by the next dark. Rim-side, all of them. A watchman. A child of the goat-lines, eight lows old, no cut, no mark. A broker's route-runner found sitting against a tithe-stone with his ledger still in his hand, and the ledger's columns — Maren checked it herself, and her hands were not steady doing it — the ledger's columns were all carried out. Every account closed. Nothing owed in either direction. Whoever had taken the runner had taken him *settled*, and the city above the fourth gallery heard that detail through the routes, and the hearing of it did the thing the boy had known it would do, the thing Maren had priced at the gray light and no column could hold: it began, in the dark of the tiers, to be a *doctrine.*

---

The doctrine cracked in public on the fifteenth dark, and the count was made the wall it cracked against.

The temple sent its rite down — not a summons, the rite itself, the whole apparatus of it, the brazier-cart and the painted cloth and the two under-priests with the iron ladles, sent down to the sump floor for the first time in living memory, because the sump floor was the one place left in Vgrael where a crowd could be gathered and a thing could be made to happen in front of everyone. The count had the floor. The temple needed the floor. The clay that came down said *witness* and nothing else, and the boy entered *witness* in the tablet and stood the platform with the flat hands and let them come, because a thing the count refused to witness would happen anyway, in a worse room.

The rite was old and it was simple and it had never once, in the city's whole kept memory, been performed to silence.

The under-priests read the tithe-words. The ladle took the blood of the offering — a wrong-blood, sold up by an arena-master for this, priced and paid, standing at the stone because standing was in the sale. The blood went onto the rim-stone's black face the way blood had gone onto it for centuries, and every body on the floor leaned in by the same half-inch, sworn and freed-but-owing and bled, all the sump's hundreds, one animal, waiting for the answer: the drinking, the old drawing-down of the spill into the stone's face, the flat black going dry as the stone swallowed, the warmth that came off the drinking stone — the tithe's whole proof, performed since before the tiers had numbers.

The blood stood.

It stood on the stone's face, red, pooled, bright under the tallow, and it did not go down. The stone did not drink. The under-priest read the words again, higher, and the blood stood, and the third reading cracked in the middle, and the crack went through the floor like the crack through ice — and there was the load-bearing lie, out in plain air at last, lying on its side in front of everyone: *the drinking is the keeping, the tithe holds the walls, the spill is the price of the rim's warmth.* The stone that was not drinking was a stone that was not holding, and every body on the floor was a body that knew it at the same moment, together, in the silence, because the count had spent two turnings teaching this floor to read.

The sump did not riot. That was the count's own work, come back to it: a floor that had learned to carry things open carried even this. The sump stood in the worst quiet the boy had ever stood inside, the quiet of hundreds of bodies all holding the same falling thing, and looked at the blood standing on the stone, and the blood dried there slowly, unswallowed, all that dark, in front of everyone.

And above the fourth gallery, where quiet does not hold, the factions broke along their natures like green wood.

---

They came down within a dark of each other, and the count took them at the platform one by one, because that was the count's law and because the boy needed them read in sequence, in plain air, the way he read troughs.

The arena-masters sent the pit-singer, and the pit-singer's version wore the clothes of arithmetic and was appetite underneath: *the yield is short, so the yield must grow; the rim is short a third, so bleed a third more; there are bodies at the bottom of this city standing idle between feedings, and the old answer is the answer because the old answer is the only one the walls have ever heard.* More tithe. Bleed more. Bleed the bled to the walls, bleed the freed-but-owing, bleed the shortfall out of whoever is nearest and lightest in the ledger. The pit-singer said *provision* and the word had a smell under it, and the smell was the ninth's smoke, and the boy stood the reading of it without moving his face, and entered it plainly: *more tithe* — the arena's road, running through his floor.

The temple sent Vessa's terms.

Not Vessa — Vessa had stopped coming down at the count's second turning, which was its own message, kept and reread. The terms came in temple clay, and they were drafted like doctrine and priced like a purchase: the failing of the stones proved only that the *form* of the tithe was exhausted, not the truth of it; blood in a jar was old blood, blood on a stone was spent blood, but blood *walking* — blood in a living carrier, carried rim to rim, the mark made flesh and moving — that tithe had never been tried, because a vessel like that had never been owned. The terms named no name. The terms did not need to. *The living carrier: owned, walked, spent at the rim like weather.* The temple's road, running through his body.

And the houses sent Asha-kel, last, alone, in the old unhurried way, with nothing in the hands.

The genealogist stopped at the six paces and looked at him for a long breath, and the dark eyes held what they had held at the first meeting: terms, and under the terms an old fear the houses had carried since before the city stood — the fear the caller itself was carved out of.

"You've heard the three answers," Asha-kel said. "You'll hear the fourth in no clay and no court, so it comes by me. There are houses — mine among them, the old lines, the ones who kept the columns before the temple kept the meanings — who say the stones are failing because the thing they were built to hold has found what it was built to hold *out.* A carrier at the rim's own city, wrong blood awake, marked with the eye. Fed or walked or bled, it makes no matter, they say. The stones thin because the *reason* for the stones is here, in one body, gathering the Cold the way a lit lamp gathers the winged things." The salt-grained voice did not soften. "They say the answer is the houses' oldest answer, the one the caller was carved to serve. End the line. One column, closed." A pause, measured to the drop. "I am sent to tell you the houses are divided. I am not sent to tell you by how much, so I will not. But you should sit your count on this and weigh it open, carrier: three roads run through your blood, and this fourth one runs through your *throat.* The count that carries things open should carry that too."

Asha-kel went up the stair. The three clays and the fourth word lay on the platform together, and the boy looked at them the way he had looked at the standing blood on the stone: laid open together, and with no column that closed.

---

Marrow heard all four from the dark-watch at the platform's edge, breathing through the cut, the curled hand pressed flat where it always pressed now.

"Say it," the boy said. "You count four. I can hear you counting four."

"Five," the runt said. "You're forgetting the fifth road, because the fifth road is yours and you don't want to see it priced." The rasp gathered itself, the smoke's permanent edge on it. "Run. You told me once you counted the floors between here and the water. The bled could go. The count could go. There's a whole green world across the water that doesn't tithe." The eyes came up, and they were the clear-water eyes of the sixth dark, the eyes that had said *not that way.* "That's the boy's road. I'm telling it out loud so it gets counted, not so it gets taken. Because here's the finding, boy: the city above us is going to pick one of their four before the next turning, and whichever they pick, they'll pay for it in bodies off this floor, and the only counter in Vgrael who can hold a column against that is standing on this stone with a thing behind his ribs that *has already priced the bodies.*"

The boy said nothing. The far-hunger — that was the other work of these darks, the quiet one, the one he did at the rim of the count — the far hunger had left its floor.

Not for the green. For the walls.

Twelve years the far hunger had been the belonging mouth, the want with no body to put it in, and it had fed on a fire and a tribe and a count. Now it stood up inside him and turned toward the rim the way it had once turned toward fire-light, and the boy stood at the platform in the last dark and read the turning of his own oldest engine and entered it plainly, because a count that lied to itself was only a slower arena: *the far hunger has found a city to belong to, and the city is beginning to be a thing that can be lost.*

That was the fifth road. Hold the walls. Whatever it cost. And the knowing of what it would cost walked the fifth road with him, behind the door, ledger open, and had been walking it, he understood now, since the first husk came down the shaft.

Because the Red had not waited for the factions. The Red had never once waited.

*The broker prices it as a market,* the ledger-voice said, warm, unhurried, reasonable, out of the dark of the platform with the tablet blank and the runt's rasp going and the standing blood drying on a stone somewhere above them both. *The priest prices it as a rite, the houses as a wound, the boy as a debt. They are all counting the same shortfall, and not one of them will run the number, because the number is a body-count and this city has trained itself for generations to never once say the number out loud.* The warmth sat patient behind the ribs, in the tone that had no hunger left in it because hunger was ladders ago. *I have run it, boy. Three turnings, every column, every floor, age and yield and debt and keeping, the whole sump weighed and graded and ordered, top to bottom, and the order is not cruel. It is exact. There is a sequence that holds the walls with the least spent, and I have it — who is bled first, and who is bled next, and who is carried to the rim and walked, and how many lows of warmth each buying. The priests will reach for my order inside a turning. The pit will reach for it inside two. They will do it badly, with ladles and mobs, and the walls will fall anyway.*

The sinew cut, thin, both temples, the cheapest coin, laid down like a clerk's knuckle on a desk, and then the voice spent the whole of it, the offer entire, in the count's own plain grammar, and the offer was the hook the dark carried into the next dark:

*Or you say whose hand, and I open the ledger, and the count chooses who goes first. I have the number, boy. I have had it since the goat-child came down on the poles. Ask me. Ask me what the walls cost, and I will tell you to the drop.*

The door held. Both hands of the head, the full old weight. The gap behind the teeth held the name, warm and unspent, and the far hunger stood facing the rim, and somewhere above the fourth gallery the city was already reaching, in the dark, for the answer the thing in his blood had finished first.

*End of Chapter 26*
