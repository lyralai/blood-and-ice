# Book Two, Chapter 18: What the Red Learned

The ultimatum sat on the stone like a third climbing that would not end.

The hook-chief had left it there at the second dark of the twenty-sixth low: two lows to make the bowl heavier than blood. After that, not a watch. A herd.

The frost had not been kind.

The fourth gate's sweat had gone to rime before mid-dark. The fifth's frost was chipped away at first light and stood on the bars again, thicker, by the second — the wall writing its own side of the ultimatum, a stroke a dark. The rim-stones drank what they were given and asked for more. The watchman who had opened his palm to the stone between climbings still stood his post. There was no law yet that said him wrong, and no one had asked for one twice.

The line at the far wall had kept the word the Skell gave it.

Later.

One hundred and twelve fed. One hundred and forty-one sworn. Twenty-nine in the difference now, and the difference had stopped being a number. It stood at the far wall with knots in its cords and ages in its faces: the winter-thin woman with the three-knot cord, the mother with the child at the hip, the old ones the Skell had given the wall to lean on. They did not press. They did not curse. They waited, and the waiting was a law too, one the count had taught without meaning to — the feeding came, only later, and later was a mouth that opened without teeth.

The Skell's split had held. The old sort, then the new one, and no tier had asked the Skell to undo either. The bodies born knowing how to stand in a short had learned what the count did with a short it could not close. They learned it off the Skell's silence, and off the half-cakes the fed broke for the waiting, and off the far wall's not roaring. And the half-cakes had begun moving inside the waiting too — the winter-thin woman breaking hers again, the waiting feeding the waiting, the count's shortage teaching the count's own lesson back down the line to the people who had the least of it. No was a word the count did not own. The word it owned was later, and a body can live on later for exactly as long as later holds.

At the stair-foot, Kade kept the line on the cadence. *Count what is.* The second half had not come back, and this low had settled that it was not coming: the carrying had grown long, the mouths had set the heavier half down, and no stair in the city had bent to pick it up again. He heard his own law come back to him thinned by half and knew the thinning was the city's doing, not Kade's. A law that travels by mouth arrives at half.

The bowl climbed a third time, because the wall had asked and the count had no answer that was not more climbing. One drop from each sworn body, measured by the needle's glass, a name spoken as each drop fell. At the glass, the old wrong-blood who had held his fist closed the low before opened it. No one had come to him. No one had pressed him. He nodded once, and the drop fell, and the name was spoken, and the glass moved on, and the boy understood what he was watching: the rite had done its work on the one body in the line that had known enough to refuse it. A refusal, worn smooth, is a giving.

The wall did not care whether the drop was taken or given. The wall only drank.

---

The old hauler's wrapped shape still lay on the platform stone, waiting on the lifting. His two-knot cord had gone into the count's keeping before the breathing was all the way out of him; his owing sat in the owed-column now, workable by no hand he had, the count's to carry. Above the tiers, the temple held what it had not spent. The grief the two thin voices had sung for had not come down the stairs. Nothing the temple stored came down until the temple wanted it down.

On the third gallery, under the owing-cloth, Nai slept, and the wet edge on the crack in her breathing had ridden the whole of the low without draining. The pressed cake was long gone, and nothing had followed it down the gallery. Lorren sat the vigil, the fasting sign worn into the wrist now, and had stopped answering the child's cadence with words. The child murmured in her sleep on the stair-foot's rhythm, keeping the law's time while the law's feeding did not come.

*Count what is. Count what is.*

The law traveled lighter than bread.

---

He sat in the circle after the mourning thinned, the clerk's tablet open on the stone, the owed-column running longer than the stores could close. The twenty-sixth had been fed out of the twenty-seventh's measure, and the number was cut, and under the number, four cuts more. Rook. The clay went up the stairs at second dark in its oiled cloth, onto the courts' shelf, every low now, working above the ceiling without him. The bowl carried the name of the owed in its base. The clay carried his under the number. The city was teaching itself his name from both ends, and both halves of the lesson lived above his head.

The clerk stayed when the others went, the stylus still in the thin fingers. The staying was the asking.

"There are two counts in this city again," the clerk said, to the clay, which was the clerk's way of saying it to everyone. "Yours is open clay. Any tier may read it. The other is sealed. The courts' shelving has been buying seal-stones for a low and a half, and I shelve, crowned one. I know what a room is for by what it buys. A city that keeps one count is counting. A city that keeps two is preparing."

He let it sit. Maren had priced the same shape at the first dark, in the flat voice, and had left the naming off the board. Now a clerk had named it, and the naming was worse.

"The sealed count will want a debtor," the clerk said. "Not the count. The count has four factions under it, and none of them fit on a tablet. A name fits. A name is already climbing to them every low, cut under the number, bound in cloth. When the sealing breaks, they will not reach for the count. They will reach for Rook."

He had known that law from underneath, from the cage years and the hold: a named thing can be reached for. The naming had not closed the door above the ceiling. It had given the door a handle with his name on it.

He ran the arithmetic in the still place, the last room the numbers fit in. One measure in five for carrying. The trough at one hundred and twelve — and next low, lower. The borrowed measure in the clay under his name, compounding. The frost back on the bars before second dark. Two counts in the city, and one of them sealed. The promises were mouths — he had known it a low and a half — and the mouths were still winning.

The Red attended.

It worked beside him now. That was the change, and it had come without a dark it could be dated to: not the old push, not the heat at the back of the neck — attendance, a second clerk at the same column. He went to check a subtraction and the checking was already done, the sum turned and true before he reached it. He set the tablet down, and inside him the Red set the same tablet down in the same moment, and for the length of a breath he could not tell which of them had finished first.

*First.*

The word had been almost, at the last dark. It was almost still. But the almost had changed trades. It had stopped pushing at the door and started waiting by it, and he knew what waiting was, down here. The line waited. The wall was fed by waiting. Patience in the sump was never rest. It was appetite standing still.

---

The mother from the waiting wall came down the stairs at the last of the dark.

She was not the winter-thin one with the three-knot cord. This one carried the child at the hip — the child the crush had set out at the trough, low upon low — and the child was quiet. Hunger had taught it quiet. She crossed the sump to the platform stone the way the first woman with the cord had crossed it, without music and without permission, the deciding finished before the first step. She set nothing on the stone. She had nothing left to set.

"The count asked the sworn to give," she said. The voice was not loud. It did not need to be. "The sworn gave. The count said the giving would be counted. The giving is not counted. The waiting is counted. My child waits."

He read her whole. Winter-thin past the cord's three knots, the feeding run onto the body itself. The eyes were not angry. Anger was a thing a body spent while it still believed the sum could be moved. Her eyes had done the arithmetic and found the sum already standing.

"The count holds what it can hold," he said. The words were true as far as they went. They went to the troughs and stopped. "The child is held. The feeding will come."

"Later," the mother said.

The word sat between them on the stone. The Skell had given it to the line. Now the line gave it back.

"My child is not a later," she said. "My child is a name the count took when the cords came down. The count took the name. The count has not fed the name. A name the count takes and does not feed is a name the count has chosen to let go."

He reached for the clean words. The stores had chosen. The routes had chosen, and the fee that had doubled, and the courts buying against the rim. The count was the one thing in the city that refused the choosing — that had been its whole reason. The words came, and they came clean, and the cleanness should have warned him: the Red had already been through them, and the Red left shapes cleaner than it found them.

The mother held him with the sum a moment longer. Then she turned for the stairs, and the going up cost her more than the coming down had. The child at the hip did not cry. Hunger had taught it that too.

The line at the far wall watched her go. Twenty-nine faces. The arithmetic solved itself in their eyes, the same arithmetic solving itself behind the door.

---

He came back to the circle with the tablet and let the still place come up. The arithmetic ran and ran, and at the end of every column it arrived at the same stones: the twenty-nine at their wall, the mouths no feeding would reach. And there, where the numbers pointed, the Red set its weight against the door.

No heat. No hunger. None of the old pushes. Not a word this time.

A sentence.

It gathered in the gap behind his teeth, where the three were gone — the old house of the name. The Red was not visiting the house tonight. It was living in it, and it had learned the house's trade, and the sentence came in the count's own grammar:

*Let the weak die usefully, by the ledger. The mouths that cannot be fed are the ones the wall will drink anyway. Choose them now, before the frost drinks more than the bowl can carry. The count can hold what it must if it lets what it cannot hold go first. No pit. No blood on the stones. Only the choosing, spoken aloud, witnessed, recorded. The same glass that measured the taking can measure the going. The same mouths that learned to count what is can learn the rest.*

The sentence was clean, and the cleanness was the horror of it. No rage in it. No hunger. Nothing the cage years could have taught him to fear. Every word of it was arithmetic.

The word at the bottom was the word he had carried up the stairs himself, the low the old price asked for the Mure: *useful.* He had set it against the spilling and made the killing unnecessary, and the bowl had taught the thing behind the door what no corpse ever had — that taking can be scheduled. Scheduled taking can be aimed. It had sat through the whole of the count's building, learning the building, and now it offered the building back with one room added, and the room was the choosing.

The administrative solution.

He sat very still. His hand came up and lay over the gap in his teeth — a man putting his palm over a mouth that was not his.

The logic had the shape of the count he had built, and that was the deepest cut. The count that would not drop what it carried. The count that asked the sworn to give what the city had taken. The count opened so the short could be seen and carried. He had built it so the city would never again have to choose which body was meat. A count in a shortfall chooses anyway — slowly, in the dark, by omission — and a choice made slowly can be made faster, and a choice made by nobody can be picked up by anybody. The anybody was already standing behind a door in his ribs, holding the sentence, patient, waiting to be asked.

He could not tell whether the sentence had been his first.

That was the part the body went cold with. Not the sentence — the not-telling. He turned it over hunting the seam, his thought, its thought, and there was no seam. The arithmetic had finished itself somewhere he could not point to, and it had finished clean, and it was merciful, and it fed the wall, and it quieted the hook-chief, and it emptied the far wall, and it fed Nai.

If the twenty-nine were let go first, the count held the rest. The bowl would climb less. The frost would stay off the bars past second dark. The watch would stay a watch. The child on the third gallery would stop waiting for a feeding that came later and later, because the later would have an end in it.

The arithmetic finished itself without him asking it to. That was the whole of the trouble. It finished without hating anyone.

---

He came to the cages at the last of the dark, and the hand was already out through the bars, as it was every dark. The curled fingers found his wrist and closed, and the hold was the same hold — no numbers in it, no asking in it, the runt's oldest answer, older than the count, older than the city: *still here.* Everything else in the dark had changed shape. The hold had not. The boy stayed in the grip and felt the sameness of it like ground that does not move under a body that has been moving too long.

The arithmetic ran again with the runt's hand on his wrist, and the Red attended beside the running — patient, the sentence laid flat beside the numbers, a knife by a plate.

Marrow's fingers tightened once. Not the counting press. A different pressure — the grip of a body that has found something in the thing it holds.

"You are counting like it now," Marrow said.

The voice was low. The runt did not speak often, and when he spoke, the words came up from the body first, the body having learned to speak when the large one took everything else.

The boy did not answer. The sentence sat behind his teeth where the Red had left it. He could feel the shape of it against the gap. It did not push. It waited for him to say it, or think it, or let the arithmetic finish it.

Marrow's fingers eased. Not letting go. Only the pressure changing — a body coming off a thing it has had to name.

"The count is learning to drop what it carries," the runt said. "It is learning it from you."

He wanted to say the count did not drop what it carried — that the holding was the whole of what the count was. He said nothing. The sentence behind his teeth made every wanting true at once, and clean, and the cleanness had a grip on him now, and the grip was gentle, and gentle was the last thing he had left to fear from it.

He looked past the cages to the waiting wall. Twenty-nine faces in the tallow's edge. The winter-thin woman. The mother with the child at the hip. The old ones at the lean. The arithmetic ran down the wall and the wall came out even, and the evenness was the abomination.

The Red had learned to count order. Which mouth. When.

He had built the count so the city would not have to choose. The Red had learned the choosing from the count.

Marrow's hand stayed on his wrist. The holding was not the same as it had been — not because the grip had changed, but because the thing under it had. The runt's oldest wisdom had always been the hand that does not count what it holds. The hand was holding something now that had begun to count even the holding.

He stood. The line at the far wall looked at him as it had looked when the cords came down the stairs — like the answer, like the largest thing that would let them come near. The city's Rook sat in their mouths. It had gone ahead of him into the tiers, and it worked there while he sat still.

He could not tell whether the work was still his.

Behind the door, the Red waited. It did not push. It had two words now, *later* off the city and *first* off its own counting, and it had made a hinge of them, and the door stood on the hinge. Later, said the city. First, said the thing behind the door. Between the two words lay the whole of the season, and the thing could wait, because waiting was how both words were fed.

The ultimatum sat on the stone. Two lows. Two lows to make the bowl heavier than blood.

He looked at the twenty-nine faces, and the arithmetic finished itself without him.

*End of Chapter 18*
