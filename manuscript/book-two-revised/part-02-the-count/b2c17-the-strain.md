# Book Two, Chapter 17: The Strain

The lines had closed.

He found it in the last of the dark, fingers to the temples, before the wearing. For three lows the crown's two cut-lines had stayed open between wearings — the bite outlasting the night, the blood going thin and dry at the hairline before the next cutting came. Now the skin was smooth. Whole. Sealed overnight, and sealed unasked.

Fast healing was the oldest wrong he owned. The green had never let him keep a wound; the keeping had been the body saving the body. This was not that. The crown had done the cutting. The wrongness had done the closing. The two of them had arranged it in the night without him, as a household settles a debt while the debtor sleeps.

A crown that costs nothing is a crown something else is paying for.

He sat in the circle with his fingertips on the smooth place until the first dark came down the shaft and the tallow was lit, and then the crown went on, and the crown cut the same two lines, and the reading of the twenty-fifth low began.

The reading ran long now. The count had grown past the dark that held it. The clerk read the columns — names, yields, feedings, differences — and the owed-column took the longest. One hundred and twenty-six sworn bodies. The thin voice was dry and exact and did not hurry. The tallow burned down a finger's width before the speaking was done. His own throat was raw at the end of it. The law was *aloud*, and aloud had become a labor, and the labor was his: the count's voice was a body, and bodies wear.

At the stair-foot, Kade held the line with the cadence while the reading ran — the count's own words in the devotee's carrying rhythm, so the waiting bodies learned while they waited. The boy heard the law come back changed. *Count what is.* The line chanted it, low, under the hum of the floor. The second half had fallen off somewhere in the traveling — *name what is not, aloud* — dropped between the stair-foot and the city's mouths. A heavy thing set down first when the carrier tires. The half that named the missing was the half the city let go.

He could not order it back. A law that lives in mouths lives by what the mouths keep.

Then the clerk read the difference, level, dry, exact, as the clerk read everything. This was the first time the number had been said aloud in the city's hearing: the feeding held for one hundred and ten. The sworn stood at one hundred and twenty-six. Sixteen faces stood inside the difference. Now the difference had a voice, and the voice was the count's own, and the count's own voice had named the short in the open dark, in the hearing of the short itself.

The line did not roar. The line went still — the stillness of bodies doing arithmetic. Mouths against food. Every body on the stairs had been born knowing that sum. Down the tiers the reading would travel, clay to mouth. The law that said *speak what is* could not stop at the waterline of the bad number. He had built the count honest, and honesty was a knife with no handle. He was holding the blade end tonight. So were they. They stayed in the line anyway, and the staying was the whole of what they had.

---

The feeding came down after the reading, as the feeding came down every low — the pressed cakes broken at the troughs, the line sorted to receive them.

The troughs ran dry at one hundred and ten.

The Skell did not close the line. The Skell split it.

The amber eyes went down the faces and did the old sort — the winter-thin forward, mothers set out of the crush, old ones given the wall — and the need outran the troughs. The amber eyes did the new thing without being asked: they chose who waited. One hundred and ten fed. The rest set at the far wall, in the tallow's edge, where the dark came in from the stairs. The waiting.

Not a no. A later.

He stood on the platform stone and watched his law learn the word. The count did not refuse the cords. The count did not drop what it carried. The count had begun to say *later*, and later was a mouth too. He had known it since the green, where later was the hungriest word a body owned. The law that would not say no had found the word that says no slowly. The word would be said again, and again, each low a little sooner, until the waiting wall was the count's second column, standing in the dark, wearing faces.

The winter-thin woman — the first cord, the three knots — broke her cake at the trough and gave half to the mother behind her, the one with the child at the hip. The giving was fast and practiced, a thing her hands had done before there was a count to see them do it. The line saw. The line's seeing was the other teaching, the one he had not built and could not stop: the bled had begun to feed the count back.

The bowl's second climbing took the rest of the dark. One drop from each sworn body, measured by the needle's glass, a name spoken as each drop fell — the rite the wall had asked for, twice a low now, the fill and the climb and the pouring at the rim-stones. At the glass, an old wrong-blood held his fist closed. The boy read him whole before the fist finished closing: not a body refusing the count — a body that had been measured all its life, by needles, in cages, and knew in the bone where measuring went. The glass waited. The asking stood at the stair-foot with the glass in its hand.

He nodded once. The drop was not taken. The bowl climbed without it, and the wall did not care. The wall's not caring was the rite's cost laid open on the stone: the count could ask, and the asking could be refused. The old answer could not be refused. It could only be outlived. Somewhere above them the city was learning that about both of them at once.

---

Maren came down at the twenty-sixth low's first dark, without guards, with the board and the beads, and the flat voice gave the price before anything else, because with Maren there had never been anything else.

"One measure in five." The beads clicked once. "That is the carrying-fee on grain, where it was one in ten. The routes run. The routes are whole. The fee has doubled. You are going to ask me why, so I will price the why first. The courts have begun buying against the rim."

"Buying what?"

"Grain. Oil. Dried fish. Whatever holds." The clean hands kept still at the broker's sides. "The fourth gate's sweating went up the tiers faster than the bowl went up the stairs. Fear buys, and buying against fear does not come down. The courts are laying in the season they believe is coming. That is not doctrine. That is a column, and I have run it twice, and it closes the same both times." The beads held. "The grain exists. The feeding can hold. It holds at one hundred and twelve, no higher, and next low it holds lower, and none of that is the brokers' doing. I will not have you reading a siege where there is only weather and fear. Read sieges later. They keep."

He heard the honesty and priced it. The broker could have let him read a siege. A count at war with the brokers would buy grain at any price, and the brokers would carry it, and the fee would run all season. Maren was standing on the sump's edge refusing the profit, and the refusing was Maren's whole self, and it was also a wall around the broker's own foundation — a count that trusts the routes is a count the routes can hold without owning. He set both readings on the stone and let them stand together.

"Lend the count the difference," he said. "Against the owed-column's labor."

"No." No weight on it. A sum, not a refusal. "I will not lend the count the difference. Not because the sum frightens me — it does, and that is why. A count in the brokers' books is a count the courts can claim at the sealing of a tablet, and I did not spend a season underwriting this city's floor to find the paper under it held above the ceiling." The shelving clerk's stylus stopped, a small way off, and listened, and went back to the clay. "Buy the small carrying. One low at a time. It is the only shape of this I will sign, and it is the only shape of it that leaves you owning your own count when the season turns."

"And if the season turns deeper?"

The beads did not move. The broker's eyes went up the stairs and came down again, summing, and the sum cost Maren something, and it was still not priced anywhere on the board.

"Then the difference stops being arithmetic," Maren said, "and becomes a choosing. There are two ways to feed one hundred and forty mouths out of one hundred and twelve measures. The first is that the measures grow. The second is that the mouths are chosen. Every city that has ever stood on a shortfall has arrived at the second, and the ones that arrived slowly arrived there anyway." The beads clicked once. The click was the only anger in the flat voice, and it was old. "I have watched the strong do the choosing since before you had a name down here. I priced silence over iron all those seasons. Clay is moving past both. The courts have opened their own tablets again. Sealed counts. Not the public kind — the old kind, the kind that lived above the sump's ceiling before your books came down." The flat voice went flatter. "A city carrying two counts is a city counting toward something. When you find out toward what, it will not be in my column. It will be in yours."

The broker went up the stairs at the reading's close, and the fee stood, and the small carrying stood, and the feeding held at one hundred and twelve, and the sworn stood at one hundred and forty-one, and the twenty-nine stood in the difference, and the difference had faces, and the faces were on the far wall in the tallow's edge, waiting.

---

The hook-chief came down at the second dark and gave the wall's report from the stair-foot, the stone-drag voice flat, and the report believed itself.

"The fourth gate sweats. Two lows. The fifth's frost has gone past the cloth — the watch chips it off the bars at first light, and it is back by the second." A pause the length of a breath held down. "The watch asks the bowl a third climbing."

The third climbing was granted. There was nothing else the asking could have been answered with. The dark did not widen to hold it. Something would give — the reading, the sleeping, the watches — and the giving would be counted, because everything was counted now.

"And there is this." The flat voice did not change, and the not-changing was itself the report. "One of mine took his palm to the rim-stones between climbings. His own blood. The old words over it. He is not a man of the old law. He is a man of thirty lows' watch who felt the stone drinking, and answered the drinking."

"Will you discipline him?"

The hook-chief was quiet the length of a body thinking honestly, and the honesty came out level, and it was worse than anger.

"Bring me the law that says he is wrong, and I will stand him under it, and he will stand, because he is mine and he obeys. Bring it fast. A wall-man serves the heavier answer. You have a low — two, if the frost is kind — to make the bowl heavier than blood. After that I am not commanding a watch. I am herding one."

The wide body went up. Behind it the bowl was already filling for the third climbing, the glass moving down the sworn line, drop by drop, name by name, the rite asking more dark than the low held, and every drop a taking's shape worn soft — the same glass, the same measuring, the old machine's gesture done gently and sworn. He watched it climb and could not find the seam anymore between the asking and the needle, and the not-finding sat in him like a stone in a boot.

The old hauler died while the third climbing was on the stairs. The frost-burned one. Rim-route, winter-old, the one who had come down two tiers because he heard the bowl sung and would not die where the singing could not reach him. The Skell had given him the wall to lean on, and he had leaned on it, and the wall held him up while the breathing stopped, and no one heard the breathing stop, and that was the whole of the mercy: a death without an audience is the only death this city does not charge for.

His cord went onto the platform stone beside the wrapped body. Two knots. The clerk took the cord into the count's keeping before the body cooled — the debt does not die with the debtor; the debt was the only thing in this city that had never once died — and the reading of it went into the owed-column: a dead man's owing, workable now by no hand he had, and the count's to carry.

The going-down was sung when the bowl came back down empty, and the smoke rose thin enough to see the stone through.

Mother Harl stood over the old man's wrapped shape with the bone frame against her chest, and the rough voice was low enough that only the circle held it.

"Nine are asked. Two came." She did not look up from the shape on the stone. "The temple is not thinning, crowned one. It is storing. Grain is not the only thing this city lays in against a bad season — grief keeps too, and grief keeps dry." The fingers moved on the frame's cord, reading the cuts they had made. "And when they spend what they are saving, I do not think we are the ones they will spend it on."

Lorren stood at the circle's edge, the stripped hood back, and the flinch came before the honesty, as it always did now, and the honesty after it was brief.

"It is not rationing," the priestling said. "It is aiming."

The boy held both readings and looked up the shaft, tier over tier, to where the chanting came down through the stone, unchanged, hour on hour. He knew how to hear the unchangedness now. A wall of the temple's own, built facing inward. And behind the wall, a granary. Grief, stored against them, kept dry, kept counted. The temple was counting too. Everyone in the city was counting now. The count had come down the stairs, and counting had gone up them, and the city was learning the numbers from both ends, and numbers learned from both ends meet in the middle, and the middle was the sump, and the sump was his.

---

Nai slept under the owing-cloth on the third gallery, and the breathing had a new sound in it.

He heard it from three steps out, and the hearing stopped him as a hand stops a body at a door. The crack in the small breathing had carried air and a whistle since he had first knelt here. There was water on the crack now. A wet edge, riding each breath in, each breath out, the sound of a thing trying to drain itself and falling behind.

The pressed cake was long gone. A child's hunger is the most honest ledger in the city — it holds exactly what has been paid in and nothing else — and nothing had come down the gallery to replace it, because the feeding the strained count could carry was on the stairs and the far wall now, and the third gallery was not the front of any line, and the child knew that better than the child knew words.

Lorren sat the vigil, back to the walkway wall, the fasting sign pressed into the wrist. The ink-stained fingers were folded, and the fold was the flinch already done, the honesty already standing in the room before it was spoken.

"The crack has water in it," the priestling said. "She asks for the dark to be longer. Dark is when the feeding comes. I have stopped telling her it is coming. She tells me. In the cadence."

In the cadence. The child murmured in sleep, small under the owing-cloth, and the murmur ran on the rhythm the line had carried all low, the rhythm the stair-foot gave it: *count what is, count what is.* The half-law, in the smallest mouth in the city, keeping time while the feeding it counted did not come. Kade's cadence had climbed three tiers without carrying food. The law traveled light. The law traveled lighter than bread.

He had nothing to leave. His own ration had gone down the child's throat two lows back, and the body had run on the body since — two lows of it, and the hands shook at the readings now, and the iron taste sat behind the missing teeth and stayed, and a body could count two lows of that and still count. He did not set down anywhere, not even in the still place, the number of them a body could not count past.

So he sat the whole of the watch and let the body be the thing given. The counting-house lamp burned low, and the cold of the gallery came through the stone into him, and the child's hand came out of the cloth in sleep and found his finger and closed on it — a cracked grip, small, exact, the keeping grip — and held. The wrongness in him did not offer to help. It never did, for this. It stood at its door and watched him sit, and the watching had no hunger in it and no patience either. It only marked the vigil down, as the clerk marked a debt, and the marking was for something. Everything the thing marked was for something. He held the child's grip and made himself not ask for whom.

---

The clerk stayed after the mourning thinned, when the stair-foot stood empty and the bowl was back on its stone and the sump was down to its night bodies. The thin body stood over the clay with the stylus and did not leave, and the not-leaving was the clerk's way of clearing a throat.

"The borrowed feeding is a debt," the clerk said, to no one, which meant to everyone. "The waiting wall was fed out of the twenty-sixth's first measure. The twenty-sixth was fed out of the twenty-seventh's. The column carries the borrowing now, and a debt in clay wants a debtor's mark. The number is cut. Under the number, the law wants a name." The shelving eyes came up. "*The count* is not a name. Clay does not hold *the count.* And an unnamed debt is an open debt. Whoever names it first owns it."

He had read that law from underneath, in the cage years, in the green, in the hold — a thing without a name is a thing any hand can close on. The courts would name it inside a season if he did not. The temple would name it inside a rite. An open debt on the public clay was a door standing wide above the sump's ceiling, and doors that stand wide get walked through, and the walking-through is always called something else by the walkers.

The bowl carried the name of the owed. The clay would carry the name of the owing. The city's vessels were learning his name from both ends, and the mouth-version of it fed the line, and he did not yet know what the clay-version would feed.

"Rook," the boy said.

The stylus made its strokes, quick and hard, four cuts under the number, and the sound the city had made of his name went into the clay, and the clay was bound into the oiled cloth with the rest of the low, and the cloth went up the stairs in the clerk's thin hands — up past the tiers, past the working galleries, onto the courts' shelf, into the keeping of the books that had never yet held a debtor they could not seize. His name went up those stairs at second dark every low now, traveling without him, working without him, owned in directions he had not signed.

The bowl climbed on the backs of the sworn. The law climbed on the backs of the named. He stood on the platform stone in the empty circle and could not tell, anymore, which of the city's carried things was his.

---

At the last of the dark he came to the cages, and Marrow's hand was already out through the bars — the curled fingers, the high shoulder, the runt's whole body in its one gesture.

The fingers closed on his wrist and did not count.

Just held. The holding said what the counting had always said, said it without the numbers, older than numbers: *still here.* The boy stood in the grip and let it hold, and the grip did not weaken and did not press and did not ask, and the not-asking was the runt's oldest wisdom — the hand that had learned in the cages that a held thing is not a counted thing, that counting is what the city does to what it means to spend.

Then he sat in the circle, and the still place came up in him, and he ran the arithmetic. Shares, stores, routes, fees. One hundred and forty-one sworn. One hundred and twelve held. Twenty-nine in the difference, and the difference compounding low over low, and the borrowed measure already in the clay under his name, and the bowl climbing three times now out of a dark that held two, and the frost back on the bars by second dark, and a wall-man's blood already on the rim-stones between climbings, and the grief coming down two voices thin. Every promise the count had made was a mouth now. The count had made many promises. The mouths were winning.

And behind the door, the Red attended.

It had attended since the bowl was fired. Tonight the attendance was quicker. He checked a subtraction against the clerk's tablet and found the sum already checked — the second attention arriving before his own, quietly, as a clerk arrives first at the desk and has the column balanced before the master sits down. It did not push. It did not want. It had owned one word since the rim-stones first drank, and the word sat beside the numbers while it worked, laid flat, like a knife laid by a plate.

Then, at the place where the numbers pointed — the twenty-nine, the waiting wall, the mouths the feeding would not reach — the Red came off its patience and leaned a shape against the door.

Not heat. Not hunger. Not the old pushes. A word, almost made. It gathered behind his teeth, in the gap where the three were gone, in the old house of the name, the thing borrowing the name's address to write from.

The word was almost *first.*

Almost. Not spoken. Not bargained, not offered, not said aloud, not anywhere, not yet. But almost — the weight already forward, the floor already chosen — and he sat very still with his hand over the gap in his teeth and understood what the thing had learned to count. Not mouths. Order. Which mouth. When. The wall had been built out of the Red's one lack — the Red does not count — and the wall stood, and the wall would keep standing, and the thing had stopped trying to climb it. It stood at the wall's gate now, dressed in the count's patience, asking the count's own question, one word from answering it.

Above, the chanting did not change.

*End of Chapter 17*
