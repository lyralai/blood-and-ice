# Book Two, Chapter 16: Unite the Bled

The line began with one cord.

The reading of the twenty-second low had closed. The tablets lay bound in their oiled cloths. The bowl had climbed at first dark — one drop from each sworn body, names spoken, the old words bent over the rim of it at the stair-foot — and the sump had settled into its after-quiet, the hum of the central floor carrying under everything as it always had. The boy sat in the circle with the crown's two lines thin-burning at his temples and counted what the low had cost him. That was when the feet came down the crawl-space stairs.

Not the labor-rhythm. Not the guards' beat. The stairs had taught him every body they carried — the clerk's dry shuffle, the hook-chief's stone-drag weight, the pit-singer's quick light pair. These feet were none of those. They came down without music and without permission. The body behind them had decided, and the deciding was finished before the first step.

A woman. She was not young; the route had seen to that. The rope-scars crossed both palms, the neck was winter-thin, and the cloth was the freed-but-owing's undyed weave with the hem cut at the owing-length. She carried her cord wound around the left fist. Three knots. The cord was the oldest thing on her.

She crossed the sump's floor to the platform stone. The bled watched her come — the cages going still one by one, as the green went still when a new body crossed a boundary every body in it had agreed on. She set the cord on the stone, beside the bowl's empty station, close to where the frost-stone had sat two lows before, and stepped back with open hands.

"Count me," she said.

The clerk had stayed for the bowl's accounting. The thin body went still, the shelving eyes moving from the cord to the boy to the cord again.

"There is no column for this," the clerk said.

"The books are open," the woman said. "Open a column."

The boy read her. Whole shape first, then the parts. The voice was steady. The hands were not — the hands shook, and the shaking was not fear, it was anger worn down to its last currency, the thin coin a body is left holding when everything heavier has been taken. She was not asking for mercy. Mercy lived on tiers that never came down. She was asking for arithmetic.

"The count does not take bodies," the boy said.

"I know what taking is." The open hands did not close. "I have been taken. I am asking to be counted. The brokers count what I owe them. No one counts what I am owed. Count that."

The sump held the words. Marrow's hand found the bars of the third cage. From the eighth, Ulla's thread of sound rose and sank. From the ninth, the Skell's amber eyes found the cord on the stone and did not move from it.

He looked at the three knots. Three debts, tied by brokers' hands on brokers' ground, and every knot a year of labor the owing-length did not cover. The freed-but-owing were the brokers' foundation. Their debt was the ground the houses of the routes stood on. To count this woman was to set the count's hand on that ground.

The count's hand was already on the courts' books. That hand had not been struck. It did not mean the striking was not coming. It meant the striking was being aimed.

"The count will hold the cord until the count rules," he said. "The ruling will be public. You will stand in it."

The woman looked at him a moment longer. Then she nodded once and went back up the stairs. The climbing was slower than the coming-down.

The bled were quiet. Then the sound came: feet on the stairs. More of them.

By second dark there were eleven cords on the platform stone.

Eleven. He set the number beside the others he owned — eleven cages, eleven cuts on the bone frame, eleven spillings at the rim-stones. The city's counting rhymed with itself, and tonight the rhyme had turned inside out: eleven cords laid where the frost-stone had sat. The number that had always meant the city's appetite now meant the city's asking.

He did not read the rhyme as accident.

---

The stairs filled on the twenty-third low.

The freed-but-owing came first, cords wound on fists, whole families of them drifting down in the dark between labor watches. Then the wrong-bloods between floor-times, the ones whose yield had fallen below the needle's price and whose bodies knew what came after a falling yield. Then a rim-route hauler with frost-burn on both cheeks who had heard the bowl sung two tiers below his gate. Then the mothers.

The Skell read it before any of them reached the stone.

The amber eyes went up the stairs and came back changed — the old change, the scavenger's change, the reading the boy had first heard across the island's squaring-ground: the leavings were moving, and the moving meant the large body had decided where the leavings went, and the leavings had decided to be there when it happened. The ninth cage stood open for the bleeding-house route. The Skell left it and took the stair-foot, and no one had asked the Skell to do what the Skell did there.

The Skell sorted the line.

Not by strength — the guards' sort. Not by yield — the needle's sort. By need. The winter-thin were moved to the front. The mothers with children at the hip were set out of the crush. The old ones were given the wall to lean on. The grist, the thing that had fallen between rungs, now stood at the ladder's foot and told the rungs where to stand. The amber eyes did it without hurry and without a single word, and the line let it be done, because the line had been sorted by strength and by yield its whole life and this was the first sort that ran the other direction.

And Kade.

Kade had been released to labor under the count's watch when the cages opened. The brightness did not come back. What came back was the voice. Kade stood at the stair-foot below the Skell's sorting and gave the line the reading's cadence — names, yields, feedings, differences — count what is, name what is not, aloud — the count's own words in the devotee's mouth, over and over, in the carrying rhythm a city without reading learned everything by. The devotion had lost its vessel and found a low place, and the low place filled, and the filling was fluent. Kade had been dangerous loving the Red. Kade was more dangerous loving the count, because the count was a thing the city could follow.

The boy heard his own law in another mouth and could not tell whether it was still his.

That was the line's first cost, and it landed before any faction's answer landed.

The line passed the cages on its way to the platform stone, and at the eighth cage the bodies did a thing no one had taught them. They touched the iron. A finger, a knuckle, the side of a closed hand — one touch each, laid on the bars and carried on. Ulla's thread of sound rose, thinner than thin, and this time it was answered. Not by one voice. By the line. A low answering hum passed down the stairs body to body, wrong for the city, right for something older than the city.

The city had spent years teaching the sump that every sound was a yield. The line was teaching the other lesson back.

Mother Harl stood in the circle with the bone frame against her chest, where she had stood since the names were first spoken. The mothers came to her in the line, one by one, and pressed the fasting sign, and moved on. The grief-keeper's weathered face read the whole of it — the cords, the sorting, the touched iron, the hum — and the reading was long.

"The frame carries the taken," she said. "The line carries the living. Keep the two apart, crowned one. The city learns faster than the count does. The city has already learned it can fill one from the other if the feeding runs short."

Forty-one cords by second dark. The line did not shrink at the third dark. It grew.

---

Maren came down at the reading of the twenty-fourth low, as Maren always came — without guards, with the clerk and the board, the clean hands hanging still at the broker's sides. The broker stood at the sump's edge and read the platform stone.

The cords lay on it in a stacked column now, squared by the clerk's hands, the column the clerk had nowhere to record.

"You are holding my foundation on your stone," Maren said.

"The count holds what is owed," the boy said.

"The owing is ours. The owing is the ground the routes stand on. You are taking the ground out from under four houses and stacking it on a sump platform."

"The cords were laid by the hands that owe. The count did not ask for them."

"The count did not have to ask." The flat voice did not rise. The bone beads clicked once, a column adjusting itself. "A city that is afraid walks toward the largest thing that will let it come near. You have made yourself the thing. The cords were coming whether you counted them or not. The only question was whose stone they landed on."

The beads went still. Maren looked at the line on the stairs — the broker's eyes running it line by line, summing as they ran — and the reading cost the broker something, and the something was not priced anywhere on the board.

"An owed body administered by the count works its watches calmer," Maren said. "Calm feeds routes. Routes earn. The brokers will carry the owed-column's labor on our routes under your count's watch, and the carrying-fee stands, and the owed-column's account sits beside the bowl's account and the books." A pause, level as laid stone. "The safest column I have ever run is the one I do not control. That is not a comfortable sum. I am keeping it, because the sum is correct. Calm was never in any column I ran. I am running it now. I would like it recorded that I do not enjoy being right this many times in one season."

"It is recorded," the clerk said, and cut the new column into the fresh tablet while the broker watched — the owed-column, beside the yields and the feedings and the differences and the bowl.

The clerk's hands did not hurry. When the cutting was done, the thin body looked at the stone, and the shelving eyes held a thing the boy had never seen in them: a column that had outgrown its tablet.

"The column does not close," the clerk said.

The words went into the sump and stayed there.

"Hear the rest of the sum," Maren said. "The courts have not answered your holding of the books. The temple has not answered your bowl. Now you hold the brokers' owing. Every silence you have not heard break is being saved, and silence compounds. I price silence higher than iron. Iron at least shows you the edge of it."

He let the pricing stand. It matched his own.

---

The hook-chief came down with the bowl's second climbing of the low, and the wide burned body stopped at the stair-foot and gave the wall's report to the circle in the stone-drag voice, flat, carried, believing itself.

"The fifth gate sweats now," the hook-chief said. "Three lows. The watch asks the bowl climb twice."

He counted the fear in it. One gate had been a case. Two gates was a season turning. The wall's fear went down the tiers as water went down — finding every low place, filling it — and the low places were filling with the line, because the city that was afraid had heard where the answer was kept, and the answer was a bowl that climbed, and the bowl climbed from a stone the cords lay on, and the cords lay on the count's ground. Fear fed the line. The line fed the count. He wrote the shape down in himself and did not like either half of what it taught.

The galleries had come down too.

Bodies from the masters' floor stood on the stair landings through the reading, rim-cloth and gallery-scarves, watching the count be spoken as they watched the throat. The pit-singer stood above them all, bright-eyed, shaping. Two lines went out from the stair-foot and traveled the city mouth to mouth, faster than clay, as the singer's lines always traveled:

*He counts what the strong waste. Come down and be counted.*

And in the lines, riding the rhythm where a name rides, the sound the city had made of his name. He heard it from mouths that had never come near the sump — a hauler humming it at the bowl's passing, a gallery-scarf saying it to another gallery-scarf like a coin being shown. Rook. The name traveled without him. The name was doing work in the city while he sat still, and the work it did was gathering, and the gathering had his shape on it and not his hand in it.

Heat came up behind his sternum that was not the Red. The hands went still in his lap.

He could not read whose stillness it was.

Harvek came down alone.

The old patron-predator came to the circle's edge, the big body carrying its decades of meat and debt, and watched the line with an attention the boy had last seen on the throat's rim: the reading of a man studying a floor that was beating him.

"The galleries used to come down for me," Harvek said. "Thirty seasons I fed that floor. Ten thousand feedings, carrier. Meat walks away from me. Meat has always walked away — that is the whole of the trade, the walking is what you stage. And now look at it." The big head tipped toward the stairs. "Meat walks toward you. I have never once made a thing move like that. I had a song once, when I was young on the floor. Two lines. Not as good as yours."

He read the old patron and found no weapon in the reading. That was the reading's whole danger. What stood at the stair-foot was the showman's grief, undisguised, an old feeder watching his crowd learn a hunger he did not stock.

"Bring the counting up," Harvek said. "The staging is mine, the galleries are mine, the torches are mine. Let me make your count a floor. The city will see it from every tier, and a thing the whole city sees at once is a thing the whole city keeps."

The offer was real, and the offer was a cage, and the cage had torches on it. The boy had already read this law in the bowl: what the galleries watch, the galleries want, and a wanted rite grows teeth — and the teeth answer to the floor that stages them.

"The count happens where the counted are," the boy said.

"Then it happens on the edge of my ground," Harvek said, "and the edge is where the mob stands, and the mob is standing under a name, and the name is not mine." The old predator's voice came down to its lowest register, the register the boy had never heard it use, the one that did not perform. "Hear an old feeder before you hear the city. A name that feeds the mob is the first thing the mob eats when the feeding thins. I have watched it eat better names than yours."

A beat.

"I have watched it eat mine."

The big body turned and went up the stairs, and the line parted for it too, and the parting followed him up, tier by tier, like a wake.

---

The acolyte did not come.

Since the books had come down, a lower-order grey-cloth had stood at the stair-foot through every reading — the eye-and-thorn thread at the wrist, silent, attending the count from first dark to the closing of the tablets, carrying the temple's witnessing back up. The boy had counted the grey-cloth among the fixtures of the ground, no more to be read than the tallow-niches.

On the twenty-fourth low, the fixture was gone. The chanting above had not changed. The chant came down through the stone at the same hours, in the same order, with the same simplification in it, and the stair-foot stood empty of the temple for the first time since the reading began.

Lorren was there instead, at the edge, in the stripped cloth with the ink-stained fingers and the fasting sign pressed into the wrist.

"The temple does not argue with what it means to end," the priestling said. "Argument is attendance. Attendance is witnessing, and witnessing is a thread, and a thread can be followed in both directions. They have cut the thread. That is the answer. It is the only answer they give before the end of a thing."

"Say the reading whole," the boy said.

Lorren's face did what it always did now — the flinch that came before honesty, worn down to a wince the body no longer bothered to finish. "They will not send iron. Iron answers iron, and they have read what your blood did on the floor. They will send the thing your count cannot speak." The ink-stained fingers closed over the fasting sign. "Grief. When the temple answers you, it will answer grieving. Watch the rites that stop, crowned one. The first rite to stop will be the mourning. When the mothers cannot mourn where you stand, the ground you stand on will be named — and the naming will not come dressed as heresy. Heresy is the second word. The first word is *unclean*, and the word will come dressed as care."

The boy held it. The chanting went on above, unchanged, hour on hour, and now he knew what the unchanged chanting was: a wall of the temple's own, built facing inward, holding something back until it was aimed.

---

The line parted for the houses' dark cloth without being told.

The city knew the cloth. The city knew what the houses held and what the holding was for, and the owed bodies on the stairs made a lane, and the lane-making was its own reading — the deepest one the city had: *this body may end the crowned one, and we stand aside for it.*

Asha-kel came down the lane with a second body behind — a young one, houses' cloth, arms the boy did not know, carrying a wrapped thing the length of a forearm.

The genealogist stopped at the platform stone and read the cords. The dark eyes went up the line and came back down changed — not the clerk's change, a column outgrowing its tablet. The houses' change.

"Half these bodies carry lines the houses' beads still hold," Asha-kel said. "The city has been feeding its old blood to the wall for a hundred lows of lean and never once looked up from the needle to see what it was bleeding. You have gathered what we were told was gone. Some houses will read this as resurrection. Some will read it as the reason the relic was made. Do not build on the difference. The difference is not yours to spend."

The second body unwrapped the thing.

The clay dish. The seal. His blood, dried dark inside the wax, held at the lane's mouth where the line could see it — turned, slowly, so that the whole stair could read what the houses held. The lane of owed bodies went still. The city read the dish as it had read it at the coronation, in the sump, in the rite's own words: the call, the terms, the outside hand. The leash, shown at the crest.

"You did not need to unwrap it," the boy said.

"The line needed to see it," Asha-kel said. "A king the mob cannot leash is a king the houses must end. A king the mob watches being leashed is a king the houses can keep. You set the call in our hands because our hand could not be reached by your power. Your power reaches further every low. The relic does not move. The terms do not move. You are becoming the thing the relic is for — and the line is becoming the reason."

The flat dark eyes gave him nothing else. Terms, not obedience. The boy read the whole of the display and set it on the shelf with the rest of the season's sums: at his highest water, the houses had shown the city the leash. The crest did not loosen the hand. The hand was the hand because it did not loosen.

"The houses' hand does not loosen," he said.

"It was never a hand for loosening," Asha-kel said. "It is a hand for holding. Pray the holding holds."

The houses went up the lane, and the lane closed behind them, and the line went back to being a line.

---

The clerk's tally at the twenty-fourth low's second dark was exact, as the clerk's tallies were: one hundred and nine, and the stairs still full.

He sat in the circle with the owed-column against the stores, and the arithmetic did not reconcile. The margin the books had carried — the finger's width, shrinking low by low — had gone past narrow and turned. The feeding owed ran longer than the feeding held. The count now owed more than the count owned. The difference had been the city's profit when the books lived above the sump's ceiling. The difference was the count's debt now, and the debt had faces.

The shaking in his hands lasted longer that low than any low before it. The iron taste sat in the gap behind the missing teeth without swallowing. The crown cut the same two lines and the lines did not close between wearings anymore.

Marrow's hand came out through the bars in the dark and found his wrist. The curled fingers pressed the count — one, two, unhurried, exact, the still-here count, the runt's oldest saying — and at eleven the pressing did not stop.

Twelve. Thirteen. On.

The boy held still and let the runt count. The body was counting at the line, or at the cords, or at the size of the thing that had come to live on the stairs — the body did not have the number and counted anyway, and the count did not close, and a count that does not close is a body's oldest alarm. He put his other hand over the curled fingers and pressed back, once, and the runt stopped, and the stopping said what the runt's whole life said: *still here. And the here is bigger than the both of us now, and I have no shoulder high enough for it, and I am not letting go.*

He ran the arithmetic again in the still place — shares, stores, routes, lows until the grain ran to the bone — and behind the door, the Red attended.

It did not push. It did not want blood. It attended the numbers. He felt it as a second attention running beside his own, checking the subtraction: this many mouths, this much feeding, this many lows. The count had been his wall, built out of the one thing the thing inside him could not do. He had said it to himself in the dark of the green and the dark of the sump: the Red does not count.

The thing was learning to count.

And the line's warmth was still in his chest from the morning — the heat that was not the Red, the stillness of hands whose owner he could not read — and he held the two side by side, the fed boy and the counting thing, and could not find the seam between them, and the not-finding was quieter than fear and heavier.

He climbed.

The crown burned at the temples and the stairs were cold past the second landing, torch-smoke and wet stone, and the line's tail lived up there now — the owed sleeping against the walkway walls of the third gallery, bodies the count had sworn and could not yet feed, folded into the dark as the sump's bodies folded into the cages. He walked the length of them and counted them with his feet. That was the law. Know what is. He did not wake them.

At the gallery's far end, where the counting-house's lamp burned low, a small breathing came out of the dark.

Nai. Winter-late. Alive, and waiting for the feeding the strained count had shorted — the name on the bone frame that was still only a name, not yet a cut. Lorren sat beside the child on the stone, the stripped hood back, awake. The priestling looked up and said nothing, because there was nothing, and the two of them held the silence as the sump held the hum.

The boy knelt. The child slept curled against the wall under an owing-cloth, and the breathing had a sound in it he knew and could not place — air moving through something small and cracked — a sound out of the houses' telling, a sound the words had not needed to describe when Asha-kel gave them the cracked jar and the carrier who was not the carrier after.

He unhooked his own low's ration from his belt and set the pressed cake by the child's head, small against the dark. One cake against a cracked chest. That was the crest's whole truth, laid down on the stone: the count could hold eleven cages, hold a bowl, hold a line of one hundred and nine, and the holding was large, and the holding was real — and the first promise the count had ever made was made in this gallery, before the bowl, before the line, to a child the city had already shorted once. The count did not drop what it carried.

The keeping was the cost. The cost had learned to breathe.

He went down. Behind him the child breathed, and above him the chanting did not change, and below him, tier under tier, the city slept in a line on the stairs with his name in its mouth — and the count held, and the line held, and the store-rooms did not.

The crown cut on the twenty-fifth low's first dark, the same two lines, and the blood ran, and the reading began, and the clerk's owed-column stood open on the stone.

The column did not close.

*End of Chapter 16*
