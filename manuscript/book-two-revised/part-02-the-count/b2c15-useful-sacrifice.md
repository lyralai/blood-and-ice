# Book Two, Chapter 15: Useful Sacrifice

The city's answer came on the twentieth low.

The books were down. The reading was public. The clay tablets lay on the platform stone in their oiled cloths, and he read them aloud in the sump's circle as he had read them on the nineteenth low — names, yields, feedings, differences — and the bled heard, and the working tier heard, and what the city heard after that was the city's business.

The city heard a short.

He felt it before the head named it. The body knew lean seasons from the green — the tide-pools thinner, the fruit-fall sparse, the wrong quiet in the branches when the large animals ate less and moved more and every living thing counted without knowing it counted. The short was like that. Not one line gone dark. Every line, a little. Each yield a measure lighter than the low before. Each feeding a measure thinner on its way down. The difference between them — the city's profit, the margin the books had carried upward every low since the books began — had narrowed to a finger's width, and the finger's width was shrinking.

The books had never lied. The books had lived above. The hiding had been the lying, and the hiding was done, and now the open count said in clay and aloud what the tiers had only felt in the feed-troughs: the city was taking in less than the city had taught itself to spend.

Maren's board said it too. The broker-realist stood at the sump's edge with the clerk and the beads, and the sixth line — the line that tracked what went up — had shifted three increments left since the reading began. The bone beads did not lie either. The beads were the books' body, and the body was telling the tier what the tablets told the sump.

And at the top of the crawl-space stairs, reading the same short from above, the masters' people had begun to gather.

---

They came down at second dark.

Not the enforcer column. Not the hooks-in-fists dragging grip. A line of bodies in the masters' rim-cloth, iron carried low and slung, and at their head a wide man with a burned neck and a voice like stone dragged over stone. The hook-chief. The boy had seen him before — in the throat's galleries, standing the rim-posts behind the masters' seats, the one who ran the walls' watch and the walls' feeding both.

Behind the hook-chief came the courts' clerk. The thin body, the shelving eyes, the tablet of baked clay carried against the chest. The clerk's presence said what it always said: *this is recorded. This is law.*

The hook-chief carried nothing. Except the thing he carried wrapped in oiled cloth at arm's length, held out from his body as if it were a dead thing already going wrong.

He set it on the platform stone beside the tablets.

The cloth had gone stiff. The stiffness was frost. In the sump's wet warmth, where the tallow sweated and the stone wept, frost had no business being, and the frost was there anyway, feathering the cloth's folds white, and the cold came through the cloth as need comes through a prayer — untouched by the wrapping, uninterested in permission.

The bled felt it. The cages rattled once, all together, the small sound of bodies pulling back from iron. The Mure in the eighth cage pressed into the far corner of its cage and made no sound at all, and the making of no sound was louder.

The Red went to the door.

He felt it move — the first movement since the reading began — and braced. Not for itself. For what it would reach for. The bled were his; the Red had learned *keep*; the cold thing on the platform was kin to whatever the Mure had become, kin to the wrong-cold the green had spat off his skin; and the hooks had come down into the kept.

The Red did not push. The Red did not lean. The Red stood at the door with the stillness of a body at a shutter, hearing a sound it knew pass in the street. Not fleeing it. Not opening to it. Knowing it.

There was no fear-shape in the knowing. There was no hunger-shape. There was only recognition, and the recognition was the coldest thing on the platform, colder than the stone.

"The reading is public," the hook-chief said. His voice was flat and carried. It was not a cruel voice. That was the first thing the boy read in him, and the reading mattered: cruelty could be survived. Cruelty was simple. This was a man who had stood rim-watch for thirty lows a season since before the boy was born, and had come down off the wall with a stone under his arm, and believed every word he was about to say. "You opened the count. Good. The count is read. Then read this."

He put his hand on the stiff cloth.

"Third rim-gate, north quarter. The stone sweats cold. It has sweated cold for six lows. The watch has felt it through the sandals. The priests above have been told. The chanting has not changed." "The wall eats. Your count says the wall goes hungry. The count is yours, and it does not lie. So the case is open, and the case is old, and I will speak the old answer the way it was given to me, at this stone, in the hearing of the clerk."

The courts' clerk shifted the tablet. The stylus found the clay.

"When the wall hungers, the failing body pays," the hook-chief said. "Carried up. Spilled at the rim-stones. The old words over it. The wall drinks, the rim holds, the city lives. Rim-price. It is written older than the temple's chanting. The failing line this low is known to every tier that can hear." He did not look at the eighth cage. He did not have to. "A useful sacrifice. The words are the law's, not mine. I have said them at the stones eleven times in my seasons, and the wall held, and the city lived."

Eleven. The boy held the number against the others he owned: eleven cuts on the bone frame. Eleven cages around the circle. The city's counting rhymed with itself, and the boy did not read the rhyme as accident.

*Useful.* The word sat on the platform stone beside the frost. It was not cruel. It was worse. It was tidy.

The boy looked at the Mure's cage. The Mure had not come back off the far bars. The wrong-cold body — person once, before the needles took what they took — was pressed into the corner with the stillness of a thing that had heard a low sound behind a wall many times and knew exactly which sound this was.

He braced again for the push. The mine. The rage coming up through the floor like heat through the green's mud.

The door stayed shut. The Red stood at it, attending, and the attending had a quality the boy had not felt before it tonight: patience that was not patience. Interest that had no blood in it.

The absence of the push was louder than the push would have been.

---

"The count is open," the boy said. "Read the failing line whole."

The hook-chief blinked once. The law he had brought was old enough that its own weight surprised it sometimes.

He nodded to the clerk. The clerk read: cage eight. Yield this low, below the needle's price. Yield ten lows back, below. Feeding, unchanged. The line failing. The old answer's word: *useful*.

"Read the line before the failing," the boy said.

The clerk looked at the hook-chief. The hook-chief looked at the boy. Then the clerk turned the tablet, and turned it again, and found the older clay — the year-clay, the heavy tablets the books had carried before the coming-down — and read that.

Cage eight. Yield high beyond the sump's every other line, low upon low, season upon season. The highest yield the sump held. Feed-back, minimum. The difference between them, going up, going up, going up.

"The number is eight," the boy said. "The name is Ulla."

The name went into the sump. The Mure in the eighth cage made the small broken sound — the one from the reading of the names, the one that came from the place the needles had not reached — and this time the sound did not stop when the echoes stopped. It went on, low, under the hum of the floor, a thread of a voice wearing out its own wind.

"The line did not fail," the boy said. "The line was spent. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole count. This body yielded the highest in the sump for more seasons than the hook-chief has stood the wall. The city took the yield. The city fed back the least the body could live on. Everything between — every measure, every low, every season — went up those stairs and did not come down. The books say it. The books are open now. Read it as it stands: the city owes the eighth cage a debt no spill can pay."

"You cannot pay a debt to a wall," the hook-chief said. Flat. Not mocking. A man laying stone. "The wall does not eat owed. The wall eats blood. This is not doctrine. I have stood on the stone in the cold season and felt it drink. Your count is a new thing and a fine thing and it does not reach the rim. Nothing reaches the rim but blood."

"Then blood reaches the rim," the boy said. "Measured. Sworn. Living."

The sump went quiet in a different key. The clerk's stylus stopped. On the stairs, the rim-cloth line shifted its weight.

"The old price takes the failing body once," the boy said. "One spill. One corpse. And the line that held the sump's highest yield ends as a smear on a stone, and the wall is fed for a low, and the next short opens the next case, and the old answer goes up the tiers shopping for the next failing line until the failing line is whoever the tiers decide it is. That is the law you are asking me to write under my count: that any line that thins is meat. Read the five at the edge, hook-chief. Read the wrong-bloods on the working tier. Read the freed-but-owing. The old price is not a price. It is a permission. And it does not stop at Mure."

The wide-shouldered one of the five spoke without rising. The voice was route-worn and even. "The old price is a ladder too."

The hook-chief's jaw moved. Not denial. Arithmetic. He was a wall-man; ladders were also his trade.

"A debt is not paid by killing the body it is owed to," the boy said. "A debt is paid by paying it. The eighth cage is owed seasons. The wall is owed a feeding. So let the wall be fed — and let the feeding be counted." He turned to the platform stone, to the frost and the clay, and spoke the shape that had been building in him since the frost came through the cloth — built from the count, from the green's oldest law, from the debt-cords of the island and the giving that was the crown's first difference from the cage. "A bowl. Fired dark, wide as two hands. Each low, drops measured into it — one drop from every sworn body, the measure set by the clerk, spoken aloud, witnessed by the tiers. Not taken. Given. Sworn. The needle's glass, washed, does the measuring — the same glass that measured every taking. The bowl climbs. The rim-stones get their pouring. Your old words over it if the words warm you, hook-chief. The wall drinks. The rim holds. And no line ends, and no tier learns that a thinned line is meat, and the eighth cage is kept — its feeding carried as a debt on the sworn shares until its yield returns or its seasons close in the keeping of the count, not on the stones."

"Who swears?" the hook-chief said. Not *no.* The boy read the shape under it: a wall-man bargaining for his wall.

"The count does not volunteer bodies," the boy said. "The count asks."

He turned to the sump's edge. The five sat in their half-circle, Vorth-bodied, route-worn — bodies that had learned the large body's reading fed them longer than the small body's hunger. He had stood for them and they had followed the reading and none of them had ever called it a debt out loud.

The wide-shouldered one stood first. Then the second. Then all five, route-scarred hands opening at their sides, palms up, the oldest gesture of the giving that no one had ever asked of them.

The Skell made a low sound from the ninth cage — the scavenger's sound, the one the boy had last heard across the squaring-ground on the island when a bigger thing took the leavings somewhere new — and pressed to the bars.

"One drop, each low," the clerk said. The thin body had not spoken before in the boy's hearing, and the voice was dry and exact, a voice built for carrying columns. "Measured, spoken, witnessed. Recorded against the short. The bowl's account kept with the books." The shelving eyes went to the hook-chief, then to the boy. "The courts will shelf it."

---

Maren had been at the sump's edge through all of it, the board still, the beads still. The broker-realist spoke now, and the flat voice had its old level back — warmth struck from the ledger, or folded into it somewhere the ear could not follow.

"The bowl travels," Maren said. "Bowls do not climb stairs on their own. Routes carry it. The brokers' routes. The carrying wants a carrying-fee — the routes are not a charity and the bowl will want watching at every tier, and watching is labor, and labor is priced." A beat, the beads clicking once. "And a corpse closes a column. A debt keeps it open. Open columns earn. The brokers will carry the bowl for the fee and keep the bowl's account beside the books, and the fee is one measure in ten of nothing, because the bowl pays in calm, and calm was never in any column I have run. It should have been. I am putting it in."

The boy heard what the broker was doing with the changed column from the nineteenth low — pricing it. The pricing was the broker's way of standing still while a thing too large to lift passed by. He let it stand.

Harvek had come down somewhere in the middle of it — the old patron-predator filling the stair-foot, the big body carrying its decades of meat and debt, the pit-singer's bright eyes a tier above and behind him, reading everything as material. Harvek had not spoken through the demand. Harvek did not speak now until the bowl was a shape, and then he spoke as he priced — slow, each word set down like a body on a scale.

"The mob sees the wall fed or the mob makes its own feeding," Harvek said. "That is the whole of my trade and you have just built a rite out of it. The rim-stones stand on the masters' floor. The bowl fills on my ground, on my staging, in the sight of the galleries. The old words are said. The drops are counted aloud so the galleries can carry the counting home. My floor. My staging. Accepted."

*My floor. My staging.* The masters would make the count into spectacle, because spectacle was the only bowl the masters had ever held. The boy took the price and counted it honestly: a rite that could be watched could be wanted, and a wanted rite grew teeth, and the teeth would show in later lows. The shelf held it with the rest.

"The bowl will need a name in it," the clerk said.

The clerk said it to no one and everyone, already reaching. The stylus found the bowl's base — the bowl the sump did not yet own, and would by morning, fired dark and wide as two hands, and before it climbed once the clerk cut into the clay of its base, small, the width of a thumbnail, the marks that carried both things the law needed: the number for the column, and under the number, the name.

*Ulla.*

No one had asked him. The keeping was the clerk's law, and the law was older than anyone's convenience.

Mother Harl sat in the sump's circle with the bone frame against her chest, where she had sat since the nineteenth low's second dark. The grief-keeper's weathered face read the bowl's shape, and the reading took a long time, and when she spoke the rough voice was low enough that the cages had to lean to hold it.

"The frame carries the taken," she said. "The bowl carries the owed. Now the city has two things it must not drop." The hands rested on the cuts. "A frame can burn. A bowl can spill. What carries names can always be dropped, crowned one. Count that too."

He counted it. The shelf took the weight as it had taken every weight since the green — the taking was the oldest thing the boy owned.

---

They filled the bowl at first dark of the twenty-first low.

The needle's glass had been washed and lay in the clerk's thin hands, and the clerk measured what the count had set — one drop from each sworn body — and the drops went into the dark clay, and the clerk spoke each name as the drop fell, aloud, into the record and the sump together. The five gave standing, palms open. A taking was quick. The giving took longer.

The Mure watched from the eighth cage. When the bowl passed the cage on its way to the stairs, carried in the wide-shouldered one's two hands, the Mure made the small sound again — the thread of voice, wearing thinner.

The boy did not put the sound in the bowl. The bowl carried what was owed, and the Mure owed nothing. The city had tried for years to teach the sump that every sound was a yield. The count was the other teaching, and the other teaching held its ground while the bowl climbed past the eighth cage and did not stop, because stopping would have made the sound a taking.

Kade watched from the seventh. The brightness did not come back. The watching stayed, and the staying was a thing the boy had no shelf for yet — a devotion with its vessel broken off, pouring itself toward the bowl as water pours toward a new low place, not knowing yet what the place would ask.

The hook-chief went up with it. At the stair-foot, before the climb, he said the old words over the bowl — quietly, in the rim's own cadence, the words for spilled things, bent over a bowl that held drops — and the bending of the old words over the new vessel was not hypocrisy. The boy read it as it stood: here was a man who had demanded a corpse and been handed what he had actually come for. Not the death. The answer. Thirty lows a season on a cold wall will teach a man to want the wall answered more than it will teach him to want anything else, and the wanting had been in him longer than the law he carried, and the bowl answered it, and the answer was why the law let go.

That was the reading the boy sent up the stairs with the bowl, and it was the reading that chilled him most: *the city's fear teaches the city's men to want an answer more than they want blood.* He had built the answer out of the count, and the answer had worked, and the working had eased a hard man's fear without one spill — and the boy stood at the sump's edge feeling the ease as he had once felt the green's heat through his sandals, from a thing too large to see the edges of.

The bowl went up. The crawl-space stairs took it — tier by tier, the five carrying, the hook-chief's line behind, the pit-singer above already shaping it for the galleries. It would pass the working tier. It would pass the third gallery.

On the third gallery, in the dark, the child Nai was still waiting for the feeding the strained count had shorted. The bowl was not for her. The bowl was for the wall. The count fed the wall tonight, and a child waited in the dark tonight, and both of these things were true, and the count's law was to hold both and not drop either — and the holding was the cost, and the cost was the count.

The boy stayed. The count went up without him, in clay and drops and sworn names. That was what he had built, he understood, watching the stairs take it: a thing that worked where the maker was not. The shape of it went onto the shelf beside Mother Harl's warning. Instruments that act at a distance. Instruments that act at a distance do not stop acting when the maker sleeps.

---

The sump, after, was the quiet of the lean season — every body counting what it had.

The Mure breathed in the eighth cage. Under the old law, the body would be stone-cold by second dark, spilled at the rim, its seasons closed on the masters' floor. Under the new law it breathed, shallow, wrong-cold, owed, kept — and the breathing was the whole of the win, and the win was real, and the boy sat with it in the circle and let it be as large as it was.

Marrow's hand found the boy's wrist in the dark. The curled fingers pressed — once, twice, to the eleventh press and past it, unhurried, exact. The runt was counting him. The counting asked nothing and offered nothing. The counting said *still here*, as the still-sound said it, body to body, under the hum of the floor, and the boy let his own pulse slow to the runt's count as a fire's count slows a body that sits beside it long enough.

He reached, then, for the Red's reading. He had not reached for it all low. The reaching was the old risk, and he took it, because the door had been too quiet and the quiet had to be read.

The reading came whole.

The Red stood behind the door with the bowl. Not the bowl itself — the bowl was three tiers up by then, climbing. The bowl as the Red held it was a shape: drops, lows, names, measures, sworn bodies, a carrying-fee, a staging, a rite. A device. A line that yielded forever and never had to be closed. The Red turned the shape as it turned the marks — the open hollow, the circling line, the stopped mark — and beside them it set the new shape, and the setting had the quality of a builder finding a tool better than the one the trade was founded on.

The Red had not wanted the Mure's blood.

The wanting had moved. Not thinned — moved, as hunger moves when the body learns there is a food that does not run out. A corpse paid once — eleven spillings the boy had never seen, the same word over each: *useful.* A bowl paid every low, forever, in drops no one would miss, from bodies no one would mourn, on a count no one could name a monster. The boy had made the killing unnecessary, and the unnecessary was what the Red had wanted — the clean answer, the answer with no wasted line in it, the machine that fed the wall and the fear and the tiers while the maker slept.

The boy sat very still in the sump's circle with the runt's count on his wrist.

The Red had learned a new word tonight. Not *keep.* Not *mine.* Not the blood-word or the floor-word or the ending-words. It had learned the word from the boy's own mouth, spoken flat in the old law's language at the platform stone, and the word had gone behind the door and been turned, and turned, and found good.

*Useful.*

The count held. The bowl climbed. The child waited. The Mure breathed.

And behind the door, in a dark that was not dark to it, quietly, without hunger and without hurry, something was counting too.

*End of Chapter 15*
