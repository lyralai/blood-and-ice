# Book Two, Chapter 19: The Shorted Share

The ultimatum sat on the stone where the hook-chief had left it at the second dark of the twenty-sixth low. Two lows to make the bowl heavier than blood. After that, not a watch. A herd.

The frost had not been kind. It had written its side of the bargain on the fourth gate's sweat before mid-dark and on the fifth's bars by first light, thicker each time the watch changed. The rim-stones drank what they were given and asked for more. The wall had waited with the patience the count had taught it. The line at the far wall had not pressed. They had not cursed. They had stood, and the standing was a law the count had not meant to write but had written anyway.

At the troughs the pressed cakes came down short at the twenty-eighth low.

One hundred and twelve fed. The sworn stood at one hundred and forty-one. Twenty-nine in the difference now, and the difference had stopped being a number. It stood at the far wall with knots in its cords and ages in its faces: the winter-thin woman with the three-knot cord, the mother with the child at the hip, the old ones the Skell had given the wall to lean on. They waited, and the waiting was a law too, one the count had taught without meaning to. The feeding came, only later, and later was a mouth that opened without teeth.

The Skell sorted. The old sort first, then the new one, and no tier had asked the Skell to undo either. The bodies born knowing how to stand in a short had learned what the count did with a short it could not close. They learned it off the Skell's silence, and off the half-cakes the fed broke for the waiting, and off the far wall's not roaring. The half-cakes had begun moving inside the waiting the low before, and they moved again now. The winter-thin woman broke hers and passed half back without looking at the face that took it. The mother with the child at the hip took what was given and did not eat. The child was quiet. Hunger had taught it quiet. The wet edge on the crack in her breathing had ridden the whole of the two lows without draining, and it had grown louder.

He sat in the circle after the reading thinned, the clerk's tablet open on the stone, the owed-column running longer than the stores could close. The twenty-eighth had been fed out of the twenty-ninth's measure, and the number was cut, and under the number, four cuts more. The clay had gone up the stairs at second dark in its oiled cloth, onto the courts' shelf, every low now, working above the ceiling without him. The bowl carried the name of the owed in its base. The clay carried his under the number. The city was teaching itself his name from both ends, and both halves of the lesson lived above his head.

The naming had not closed the door above the ceiling. It had given the door a handle with his name on it.

He came to the cages at the last of the dark before the twenty-eighth feeding. The hand was already out through the bars, as it was every dark. The curled fingers found his wrist and closed, and the hold was the same hold. No numbers in it. No asking in it. The runt's oldest answer, older than the count, older than the city: *still here.* Everything else in the dark had changed shape. The hold had not.

The arithmetic ran again with the runt's hand on his wrist, and the Red attended beside the running. Patient. The sentence laid flat beside the numbers, a knife by a plate.

Marrow's fingers tightened once. Not the counting press. A different pressure. The grip of a body that has found something in the thing it holds. The reading was in the hand alone.

He looked past the cages to the waiting wall. Twenty-nine faces in the tallow's edge. The winter-thin woman. The mother with the child at the hip. The old ones at the lean. The arithmetic ran down the wall and the wall came out even, and the evenness was the abomination. The Red had learned to count order. Which mouth. When.

He had built the count so the city would not have to choose. The Red had learned the choosing from the count. The line at the far wall looked at him as it had looked when the cords came down the stairs. Like the answer. Like the largest thing that would let them come near. The city's Rook sat in their mouths. It had gone ahead of him into the tiers, and it worked there while he sat still. He could not tell whether the work was still his.

Behind the door, the Red waited. It did not push. It had two words now. Later off the city. First off its own counting. It had made a hinge of them, and the door stood on the hinge. Later, said the city. First, said the thing behind the door. Between the two words lay the whole of the season, and the thing could wait, because waiting was how both words were fed.

The ultimatum sat on the stone. Two lows had gone. The bowl was not heavier than blood.

He stood on the platform stone and let the still place do the sum. The clerk's tablet had the numbers. The bowl had the drops measured by the needle's glass. The troughs had the pressed cakes. The numbers and the cakes did not meet. The difference had stopped being a number. It stood at the far wall with faces and cords and the child whose breathing carried the wet edge from the third gallery down into the sump.

The mother came forward without permission. She had brought the child. The child was quiet the way hunger had taught it to be quiet, but the wet edge had grown louder in the low since the pressed cake had gone. The mother set nothing on the stone. She held the child against her shoulder so the line could see the face and the sound that came with each breath.

"The share was counted for her," the mother said. The voice was not loud. It did not need to be. "The count took her name when the cords came down. The count said the name would eat. The name has not eaten. The share has been shorted."

He read her whole. The winter-thin past the three knots had moved onto the mother now. The feeding run onto the body itself. The eyes were not angry. Anger was a thing a body spent while it still believed the sum could be moved. Her eyes had done the arithmetic and found the sum already standing. The child at the hip had the weight of a body that had already begun to leave. The wet edge had a sound now that had not been there two lows ago. It was louder. It was wetter. It was the sound a body makes when the later has run out.

Lorren came down the stairs from the third gallery. The fasting sign was worn into the wrist deeper than it had been. She had stopped answering the child's cadence with words two lows ago. The child had murmured in her sleep on the stair-foot's rhythm through the lows. The rhythm had not fed the child. The law's time had kept the child waiting and the waiting had taken what it came for.

He came down from the platform stone. The mother did not step back. She held the child so the line could see. The twenty-nine faces did the arithmetic the way they had learned to do it from the count. The sum was the same in every pair of eyes. The order built to protect the bled had taken from the bled.

"The count holds what it can hold," he said. The words were true as far as they went. They went to the troughs and stopped. "The child is held. The feeding will come."

"Later," the mother said.

The word sat between them on the stone. The Skell had given it to the line. The line gave it back. The child made the sound again. It was not a cry. It was the sound the wet edge made when it had finished what it came to do and was not finished yet.

He put his hand to the child's back. The body was small under the cloth. The warmth that should have been there was thin and wrong. The Red attended beside the touch. It did not push. It laid the sentence beside the body the way it had laid it beside the tablet the low before. The sentence had not changed. It had only acquired a name.

*Let the weak die usefully, by the ledger. The mouths that cannot be fed are the ones the wall will drink anyway. Choose them now, before the frost drinks more than the bowl can carry. The count can hold what it must if it lets what it cannot hold go first. No pit. No blood on the stones. Only the choosing, spoken aloud, witnessed, recorded. The same glass that measured the taking can measure the going.*

The sentence was clean. The cleanness was the horror of it. No rage in it. No hunger. Nothing the cage years could have taught him to fear. Every word of it was arithmetic, and the arithmetic had finished itself without him asking it to. The stores had chosen. The routes had chosen. The fee that had doubled. The courts buying against the rim. The frost back on the bars before second dark. The two lows the hook-chief had given. The bowl that had not climbed high enough. The child whose name the count had taken and the troughs had not fed.

The mother turned the child so the twenty-nine could see the face. The child's eyes were open. The wet edge came with the breath and did not leave with it. The line at the far wall watched. The winter-thin woman with the three-knot cord broke what she had and passed it back. The giving was fast and practiced. The bled had begun to feed the count back. The count had not built that teaching. The count could not stop it.

He felt the body go cold in the place behind the ribs where the still place lived. Not the old cold. A different cold. The cold of a sum that had already been solved. The child on the mother's shoulder was the one the wall would drink anyway. The choosing could be spoken aloud and witnessed and recorded and the count would hold the rest. The words were clean. They were useful. They were the sentence the Red had given him.

He said none of them.

The child made the sound a third time. It was smaller. The wet edge had become a rattle. The mother's hand went to the child's back the way a body goes to a thing it has already lost. The line at the far wall watched. The twenty-nine faces did the sum. The sum was the same in every pair of eyes. The order built to protect the bled had taken from the bled.

The mother laid the child on the platform stone. Not on the ultimatum. Beside it. The stone took the shape of the small body the way it had taken the shape of the words. The line at the far wall watched. The Skell's amber eyes went down the faces and did not split the line. The waiting had a name now. The name was Nai.

He put his hand over the gap in his teeth. The palm did not stop the sentence. The sentence had learned the house. It lived there. It had learned the count's grammar and the count's mercy and which mouths the wall would drink anyway. The arithmetic had finished without him asking it to. That was the whole of the trouble. It had finished without hating anyone.

The ultimatum sat on the stone. Two lows had gone. The bowl was not heavier than blood. The herd was coming or the watch was not a watch or the count would have to choose which of the twenty-nine the glass would measure next. The choosing could be spoken aloud and witnessed and recorded. The same glass that measured the taking could measure the going.

He could not tell whether the choosing had already been made.

The body of the child on the stone was the proof. The Red attended. It did not push. It waited by the door the way patience in the sump was never rest. It was appetite standing still.

The line at the far wall did not roar. The line went still. The stillness of bodies doing arithmetic. Mouths against food. Every body on the stairs had been born knowing that sum. The law that said speak what is could not stop at the waterline of the bad number. He had built the count honest, and honesty was a knife with no handle. He was holding the blade end tonight. So were they.

Marrow's hand stayed on his wrist. The grip was the same grip it had always been. The reading was not. The fingers tightened once. Not the counting press. A different pressure. The grip of a body that has found something in the thing it holds and does not like the finding.

"You are counting like it now," Marrow said. The voice was low. "The count is learning to drop what it carries. It is learning it from you."

The hand was holding something now that had begun to count even the holding. The counting had a body now. Small. Quiet. Not breathing. The body on the stone was the proof the count had learned what to let go.

The arithmetic had finished without him.

*End of Chapter 19*
