# Chapter 28: The Hold

I know the dark before they lower me into it.

The hold has been waiting under the world.

Tar. Wet wood. Bodies kept too close. The floor lifting and falling with water under it. A hole made by men to turn breath into property.

They lower me by pole and rope because Drevan will not risk hands near my teeth. The hatch waits below, black and square. Marrow's cage is already there. I smell him before I see him: blood, Vorth musk, swamp mud dried into the fur, pain held tight enough to become another scent.

He makes no sound when they drag him across the planks.

Neither do I.

The hatch takes us.

Dark closes.

The scream rises.

I keep it behind my teeth.

The first hold took my voice because I had no place to put it. This time I have places.

My name.

Kala's eye.

Marrow breathing beyond the bars.

Yara above, still alive.

The brand-words I will one day hand back.

I lay them out inside me like the Vorth laid their dead on clean stone.

Faced.

Ordered.

Kept.

The body wants to return to the old shape: small, silent, owned. It remembers before I do. Shoulders folding. Breath thinning. Hands hiding. The old wisdom of captivity, where being noticed was danger and being forgotten was life.

I give it a different silence.

Not obedience.

Banked fire.

I count.

The roll of the floor. Long lift, short fall, shudder in the ribs of the boat. The scrape of rope against my wrists. The wet breath of the Mure in the cage. The Skell's claws ticking once every few moments, not random, not calm. The drip from a seam above my left ear. The coughs on deck. Three men sick. Then four. The drag in Drevan's gait when he crosses over the hatch.

Marrow's breath.

That count matters most.

In.

Hold.

Out.

Angry even sleeping.

There are other breaths too.

The Mure breathes like wet cloth pulled through teeth. Its body is folded badly inside the cage, long limbs tied with ropes that have rubbed pale grooves into scaled skin. In the dark its eyes make two dull moons. It was a bright water hunter once, I think. A creature of river bends and moonlit shallows. Here it has been made into a bundle that smells of old mud and fear.

The Skell breathes in clicks.

Not broken. Waiting. Even chained muzzle to tail, even ribs banded wrong under bruised hide, it keeps one claw against the cage floor and taps whenever the ship rolls. Tap, pause, tap. A measure. A map. A way to tell itself the world still has edges.

Below all that, under plank and cage and rope, human breath.

Not crew.

Cargo.

At first I mistake them for shadows. Then one coughs words, another laughs dryly, and Drevan's catch fills the dark. Men and women chained in pairs. Some collared. Some roped. A few branded: grain debt, border theft, debtor's kin, punishment soldier.

They are not mine.

They are not free.

That should be simple.

It is not.

The woman sees my eyes shining in the dark and whispers a prayer.

The boy tells her to save breath.

The old man laughs once. "Breath is all they haven't priced yet," he says.

I catch enough human now. Priced. Breath. Save. He hears me listening and goes quiet.

Then he asks what I am.

No one answers him.

Marrow does.

He rattles his bars with one hard kick.

The old man laughs again, softer. "That one knows."

The woman begins to cry without sound. Tears have smell in the dark. Salt, shame, anger swallowed until it turns the stomach sour.

The boy beside her tells her to stop.

She says a name.

Not hers, I think. A child's. Or a husband's. Or a place. Names all sound like wounds in holds.

The boy says the name back badly, mocking at first. Then not mocking. Then he stops speaking.

I add their breaths to the count because I cannot keep them and can count them.

They threw him in as fodder and do not know they carried my right hand across the water.

Sometimes he wakes and forgets where he is.

For a breath his body jerks against the bars, teeth finding iron before his eye opens. Then he remembers. The high shoulder rises. The curled hand folds tight. He finds my breathing in the dark.

I make the still-sound.

He answers with a click of tooth on bar.

Not gratitude.

An insult, probably.

It steadies me more than gentleness would.

After a while, his cage shifts. A low Skell hiss. Marrow's answering snarl, small and immediate. A thud. Bars rattle. The Mure gives a wet click of protest.

"Still," I whisper in Vorth.

The dark takes the word.

Marrow hears anyway.

The cage goes quiet except for his breathing, which has changed shape.

Listening now.

Yara's voice comes through the planks sometimes, thin and careful. She is made to translate above deck. Even bound, she carries meaning for men who own her. I catch pieces when the wind drops and the water is kind.

Island.

Fever.

Valuable.

Shipment.

Buyer.

Pit.

The last word comes often.

Pit.

Its full shape is still closed to me. I know enough. Yara drew the circle in mud. Men around. Blood inside. Ownership turned into spectacle.

Men do not only want to possess.

They want witnesses.

The thought sits cold in the dark.

The cargo knows the word better.

When Yara's voice thins through the planks and pit comes with it, the old man stops laughing. The woman presses her forehead to her knees. The hard-mouthed boy spits toward the bilge and misses because the ship rolls.

"Not me," he says.

No one answers.

"Not me," he says again, louder, as if volume can shape law.

The old man says a place name. I catch only the first sound. Then he says another word.

Debts.

The woman with the swollen eye says husband.

The boy says sold.

They begin to tell each other how they were taken, each story pushed into the dark as if the dark might judge fairly. A farm note bought twice. A border tax unpaid. A brother accused of stealing grain. A guard who wanted a daughter and took the mother when the daughter ran. None of it sounds like raiding. All of it sounds like paper.

A mark on paper. A hand with a seal. A life moved before rope ever touches skin.

The old man says the pit pays better for animals than debtors unless the debtor knows how to hold a blade. Then he asks if I know blades.

Marrow answers again with a snarl.

The old man goes quiet.

Smart.

Not law. Law belongs to those with keys. This is smaller and sadder: who gets the dry corner when the ship rolls, who takes the first cup if a guard lowers water, who is allowed to sleep against the bulkhead and who is kicked awake for leaning too close. Captivity makes ladders even in the dark.

The hard-mouthed boy thinks he should stand higher on that ladder because he still has anger enough to spend. He bares it at the woman when her crying shakes the chain. He bares it at the old man when the old man laughs. He bares it at me until Marrow's cage rattles and his courage remembers its size.

The old man has another kind of place. He knows words the others need. Port names. House marks. Which pit gates pay for blade-work and which pay for beasts. Which collars mean debt, which mean sentence, which mean nobody will ever ask where the body went. Knowledge makes a poor blanket, but the others still huddle near it.

The woman has nothing anyone calls power.

Then the ship pitches hard and the boy's head strikes wood. He curses, sharp and frightened. Blood opens above his brow. The woman reaches with bound hands and presses the torn hem of her sleeve against it before he can refuse.

"Hold still," she says.

He says he does not need her.

She says, "Bleed quieter, then."

The old man laughs. Not cruelly this time.

Even Marrow pauses at that.

The boy lets her press the cloth.

Small kindness has to disguise itself here. If it comes bare-faced, someone will sell it, punish it, or lean on it until it breaks. So it arrives as insult. Accident. Spilled broth. A foot nudging a cup. A woman telling a boy to bleed with better manners.

I count those too.

A slaver comes below with a lamp during the second night.

Light opens like a wound.

He is not one of the marked owners. Younger. Nervous. The lamp shakes in his hand no matter how hard he tries to stop it. He checks the ropes at my wrists, then my ankles, then the throat loop. His eyes linger on cuts that should remain open and do not. On the bruise fading at my jaw. On the blood dried around my mouth.

He says the brand-word.

Mine.

Not with Drevan's certainty. More like a charm against fear.

It falls away.

He does not like my quiet.

Good.

Let him think stillness means damage. Captors grow careless around captives they believe have folded inward.

His lamp passes to Marrow's cage.

There the fear leaves his face. He sees only a small broken Vorth with one high shoulder and a torn ear. Cargo of lesser price. Fodder for the pit. He does not see Marrow's eye following the key ring at his belt. Does not see the curled hand test one bent bar each time the boat rolls.

I see.

Marrow sees me seeing.

The young slaver sees none of it because fear has made him stupid in the way comfort makes men stupid. He has a lamp. A key. A deck above him. A name other men answer when he speaks.

So he believes the dark belongs to him.

The dark has never belonged to men.

The lamp goes back up.

Dark returns.

After him comes the smell of broth.

Not for us.

A collared cook lowers a pot by rope while a guard above holds the other end and complains about the stink. The cook is broad-shouldered, bald, with burns along both forearms and a collar rubbed shiny from old use. He ladles broth into wooden cups and passes them through the far bars to the human cargo first.

The guard above tells him to skip the woman.

The cook does not look up.

His hand slips. Broth spills across the floor near her knee. Not much. Enough. She bends, cups it, drinks from her palm before shame can stop hunger.

The guard curses.

The cook apologizes so flatly the apology has no life in it.

He comes near my ropes last.

His eyes avoid my face. They stop at my wrists instead. At cuts closing too quickly. His mouth tightens.

He does not offer broth. Orders, maybe. Fear, certainly. But when he steps past me, one heel nudges a broken cup within reach of Marrow's cage.

Marrow stares at it as if insulted by charity.

Then he hooks it with the curled hand and drags it in.

The cook sees and does not see.

The owned survive around owners by making blindness into a craft.

The old man thanks him.

The cook says nothing.

When he reaches the ladder, the guard hauls him up too quickly and the pot bangs against the hatch frame. Broth spills down in a hot stripe across the cook's hand. He makes no sound.

Above, the guard laughs once and then coughs until the laugh breaks apart.

Fever owns him too.

The hard-mouthed boy waits until the hatch closes before he whispers to the woman with the swollen eye.

"Eat next time."

She answers that there may not be a next time.

The boy says there is always a next time until there is not, and until then a person eats.

That sounds like Marrow would say it if he had human words and less pride.

In the cage, Marrow gnaws the broken cup clean and then shoves it toward the Mure with his foot, pretending he meant to kick refuse away.

The Mure drinks the last slick of broth from the crack.

Marrow sees me watching and bares his teeth.

Tell no one, his eye says.

In the dark, among chains and fever, I keep that too.

Above us, men keep dying. Fever rides in their lungs. Rot climbs through their cuts. One body goes overboard before dawn: feet gather, prayer starts and is swallowed, heave, splash. Later another splash. No prayer that time.

The green is gone from sight, but it has not released them.

It lets me go, and that feels less like mercy than being singled out. My cuts close while men cough blood into the bilge. I count each breath I keep.

Drevan comes to the hatch after the second splash.

He does not open it.

He stands above us for a long while, breathing through his mouth, the way men breathe when fever has filled the nose and left pride no clean path. A drop falls through the plank seam and lands on my cheek.

Sweat.

Or rain.

Or him.

I hope it is him. I hate myself for hoping, and then I keep hoping.

Why?

Why my blood clears what kills them. Why the mark makes old seers bow and slavers pay in bodies. Why the Red wakes when blood touches my mouth. Why warmth rose in the pit of me when Marrow looked through the net and told me not here.

No answer comes.

Only the count.

So I keep counting.

At some point Yara comes to the hatch.

Not down. Drevan will not allow that. Her shadow blocks the seam of light above, and her voice lowers through the crack between planks.

She says the true name she gave the dark.

Then another word.

Sorry.

I know that one now.

It is a poor word for a wound this large. Still, it is hers to give.

I cannot answer without bringing men to the hatch. I press my forehead to the wet plank below her shadow and breathe once, loud enough that maybe she hears.

Maybe not.

Marrow makes a low sound from the cage.

Not warning.

Witness.

The boat rolls on.

The hold does what holds do. It narrows the world until the mind starts mistaking narrowness for law. Plank. Rope. Breath. Dark. Pain. Count. Again.

I refuse the lesson.

The hand opened once.

It dropped me into the sea. The sea made a shore. The shore taught mud, hunger, kings, and traps. Kala kept me. Marrow answered me. Yara gave me speech. Drevan gave me chains and thought chains were the end of learning.

I count the hatch hinges, the keys at the guard's belt, the distance from Marrow's cage bar to my foot.

The hand will open again.

When it does, I will not be the boy it closed on.
