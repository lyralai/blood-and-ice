# Chapter 4: The First Fire

Night belongs to the jungle.

Day is cruel, but day can be read. Light shows the channel, the roots, the birds, the color of fruit, the places where mud lies about how deep it is.

Night takes the edges off everything.

The water becomes sound. The trees become weight. Insects rise in clouds from the swamp and stitch the dark with their whining. Bodies move beyond the rock shelf with a patience I hate.

The first nights, I survive by staying small.

I crawl into the crack under the stone, knees against my chest, back pressed hard to the wall. The place smells of wet mineral and old rot. It is barely large enough for me. That is why I choose it. Large places let too much in.

But sleep does not come cleanly there.

Every sound wakes me. A splash in the channel. A branch creaking. The soft patter of feet over leaves. Once, something breathes at the mouth of the crack for a long time, wet and slow, before moving on.

I have no knife.

I have stones. Teeth. Hands.

Not enough.

I need fire.

Men had fire on the ship. The slavers kept it above, near their voices and their meat. Down in the dark hold we got the smell and nothing else. Smoke. Fat. Warmth that belonged to other people.

I watched anyway.

A boy with nothing learns by stealing with his eyes.

I saw men feed flame. Dry threads first, then sticks. Too much too soon and the little light died. I saw sparks leap when steel struck stone. I saw a cook curse wet wood and throw it aside.

Now I have no steel and all the wood is wet.

That is the problem.

Problems can be worked.

I spend the morning looking down and learn nothing. Every branch on the ground is soft with rot. Some split open under my fingers and spill white worms. Some are full of ants. One log looks sound until I lift it and it comes apart like old bread.

Wet wood will not burn.

So I look up.

Dead branches hang caught in the living trees, lifted away from the rot. Sun reaches them. Wind touches them when wind touches anything here. I climb for the first one and nearly fall twice. The bark peels under my hands. My bad leg trembles. Sweat blinds me.

The branch cracks loose with a dry snap.

Dry.

The sound is almost beautiful.

I gather more. Thin branches first. Then thicker ones. I drag them to the rock shelf and stack them where rain will have to work to reach them. I strip pale fiber from vines and spread it on stone to dry. I scrape bark into curls with a sharp flake of black rock.

By sunset I have a little pile of wood.

No fire.

The next day I try stone.

I strike one rock against another until my hands ache. Nothing. I find a darker stone with a glassy edge and strike that. Chips fly. One cuts my palm. Blood wells, bright and immediate.

Still no spark.

The cut closes before evening.

I watch it happen. Not all at once. Not like magic from a tale. The bleeding slows. The edges tighten. The pain folds down into a clean line. By dark it looks a day old. Maybe two.

It should not.

I press the cut open again with my thumb because anger is sometimes stronger than sense.

Blood comes.

The small wound begins its quiet work again.

I hate it then. Whatever lives in my blood, whatever keeps fever and rot from taking me, it will not answer when I ask. It gives me no fire. It gives me no knife. It only keeps me alive in a place that seems insulted by the fact.

"What are you?" I whisper.

The jungle drips.

Nothing answers.

Good. I had not meant to speak.

I go back to the wood.

Stone will not give me flame. So I try friction.

The memory comes from before the hold, or maybe from the ship, or maybe from watching some man make fire while I pretended not to watch. A stick spun against a board. Smoke rising. Hands moving fast.

I cut a notch in a flat piece of dry wood. I set a straight stick into it and roll it between my palms.

Nothing.

I roll faster.

Heat comes. Not enough.

The stick slips. My hands blister. The blister tears. Blood slicks the wood and ruins the work.

I stop before I throw the whole fire-kit into the channel.

Rage is easy. Rage has always been easy. It gives the body something to do when the world refuses to open. But rage spends strength and leaves nothing cooked.

I wash my hands. Bind them with strips torn from my shirt. Start again.

This time I brace the board with my foot. I keep the stick straight. I lean my weight into it and roll until my shoulders burn.

Smoke comes.

Thin. Gray. Almost not there.

I freeze.

The smoke fades.

"No."

The word comes out rough and small.

It is not the first word I have spoken on the island, but it is the first I have spent on something that might answer. The sound hangs between my hands and the dead wood. I hear a woman in it for half a breath, a low voice correcting my fingers around a spoon, or a cup, or a toy I cannot see clearly anymore.

The memory is gone before I can catch it.

I want it back with a hunger that has nothing to do with meat.

I start again.

By the time the coal forms, the light is failing and my arms feel hollow.

It is only a black speck in a nest of dust. A dull red eye. I bend over it with both hands cupped around the place and breathe softly. Too much breath kills it. Too little and it dies alone.

The red deepens.

I tip it into the dry vine fiber.

For a terrible second nothing happens.

Then a thread catches.

The flame is smaller than my thumb.

It is the most powerful scrap of life I have ever owned.

I feed it shavings. Then twigs. Then split sticks no thicker than my finger. I remember the cook drowning a flame with greed and do not make his mistake.

The fire grows.

Gold light pushes against the green dark. Smoke climbs into the leaves. The insects lift away from it. The mouth of the rock crack glows. Shadows move back.

Not gone.

Back.

That is enough.

I laugh once.

The sound startles me. I clap a hand over my mouth, but there is no one here to punish joy. No slaver. No guard. No man above the hold with a boot ready for the boy who made too much noise.

Only the fire.

Mine.

I learn quickly that fire is greedy.

It eats the little sticks faster than I expect. It sulks when I give it wood too green. It spits when rainwater hides in bark. It brightens when I shave dry curls from the heart of a branch and feed them one by one, slow enough for the flame to choose.

By the second hour I am no longer making fire.

I am keeping it.

That is harder.

Making is a shout. Keeping is a promise said again and again in small ways. Turn the fish before it burns. Move the coal before ash chokes it. Shelter the flame when the wind slips under the leaves. Do the work while tired. Do it while hungry. Do it when the dark outside the light makes every muscle want to curl around the spear and wait.

I think of the slavers' lamps then, hanging beyond the bars.

They never guarded the flame. They guarded us from it.

Heat for them. Smoke for us. Cooked meat for them. The smell for us. Light on their faces, dark on ours.

I take a burning twig from my little fire and hold it up.

It shakes in my hand because my arm is worn out, but the flame stays. Small. Stubborn. Mine.

"See?" I whisper to the dark where no slaver waits. "Mine now."

No answer comes.

That makes the victory cleaner.

When the first moths find the light, I almost strike them away.

They are large as my palm, with wings clear as wet glass and blue veins running through them. They circle the fire slowly, not stupid enough to throw themselves into it. Light passes through their wings and paints moving water on the stone.

One lands on the back of my hand.

I hold still.

Its feet are softer than ash. Its body trembles with a life so slight I can feel each beat through my skin. The blue veins in its wings darken near my healing cuts, then pale again. It turns once, as if tasting me through its feet, and lifts away.

More come. For a while the whole space under the rock is full of clear wings and firelight, blue shadows opening and closing over the mud.

It is beautiful.

The word comes to me with difficulty, as if beauty is another tool I have let rust. I know useful. I know dangerous. I know edible, sharp, rotten, weak, strong, owned, free.

Beautiful is harder.

Beautiful asks nothing and still takes breath.

I try to remember my mother's face and find only pieces. A cheek in lamplight. A dark braid over one shoulder. Fingers tapping twice on a cup before she gives it to me. Was that real? The memory trembles like the moth on my hand.

My name presses behind my teeth.

For one dangerous breath I want to say it into the fire. Not to give it away. To hear it alive somewhere outside me.

I press my tongue to the roof of my mouth until the want passes.

Not yet.

The fire makes faces of the trees.

All day they were trunks, leaves, vines, walls. In firelight they become watchers. Old ones with twisted mouths. Thin ones leaning close. One broad root looks so much like a bent man that I throw a stone at it before sense catches up.

The stone bounces off.

The bent man remains a root.

I tell it to stay dead.

My voice sounds strange and rough and almost pleased. Talking to roots is foolish. No one here knows enough to mock me for it. That makes foolishness feel like a stolen luxury.

I cook a second fish and hold it up toward the root-man.

"None for you," I tell it.

The root-man accepts this with dignity.

After that I name the crooked stone by the door Captain, because it sits there doing nothing and looks important. I name a beetle Tax, because it steals a crumb of fish and leaves without asking. Tax survives my judgment by being too quick for the slap.

I smile with half my mouth.

It is an ugly smile. Three missing teeth make every smile into a broken gate. I still like the feel of it. There is no one here to own the shape of my face.

For a little while, the fire and I are very rich.

I cook a crab first. Then one of the clear fish the birds will not eat. I hold it over the flame on a green stick until the flesh goes white and splits. The smell is rich enough to hurt.

The fish stays down.

Fire changes the law.

By midnight I know three new truths.

Smoke keeps the biting clouds away.

Cooked clear fish does not poison me.

And light makes the dark honest.

Not safe. Honest.

Bodies still move beyond the ring of firelight, but now they have edges when they come close. Eyes shine. Leaves stir. Mud prints show where something crossed and thought I would not see.

Near dawn, I wake to the sound again.

The low call from the high green.

It rolls through the trees, deeper than any bird, shaped enough that my chest tightens around it. Another call answers. Farther off. Then a third, almost too low to hear.

Not beasts calling territory.

Voices.

I sit by my fire with a cooked fish in my hand and forget to eat.

There are people here.

Not human. I know that already. I saw the huge scarred one across the ravine. I saw the smaller shapes behind it. But knowing with the eyes is different from hearing voices in the dark.

Voices mean memory.

Voices mean law.

Voices mean I am not alone.

The thought opens inside me too fast. Hope is dangerous because it runs before the feet know the ground.

I crush it down.

People can be worse than jungle. People can put hands on a boy's neck and call it ownership. People can feed you and count the debt in your skin.

Still, when the call comes again, I lean toward it.

I hate myself for that.

Then I feed the fire before it can die, because hating myself will not keep the dark back.

The sun lifts white behind the leaves.

Smoke threads upward.

Somewhere in the green, the voices move.

And for the first time since the sea gave me to this place, I choose a direction for a reason other than food.
