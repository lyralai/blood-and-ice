# Chapter 21: The One Who Sees

Yara comes back.

Not the next low tide. That would be too easy, and nothing around the camp is easy. The next low she stays near the fires, carrying bowls between owners and cage yard while I watch from the mangroves until my legs cramp and Marrow's patience goes sour beside me.

The low after that, she comes.

She walks openly, water skin over one shoulder, knife at her belt, eyes lowered until the last watcher turns away. Then she slips down the spit to the wet line where roots begin and crouches as if checking a pool.

Her first word is shell.

She says it twice. Touches the shell. Sets it down.

Then mud. Tide. Root. Water. Fire.

I give no answer.

The first rule of the camp is that sound carries iron behind it. A voice from the green would bring watchers, nets, hooks, and men with orders to burn what they cannot cage. So I lie in shadow and take her words like stolen food.

Yara seems to understand the bargain. She never asks for my voice at first. She builds the road one stone at a time and pretends not to see me at the other end.

Camp.

Cage.

Pain.

Captain.

That word she lays down like a blade.

Sometimes she gives me a word and then takes it back.

Not aloud. Not where any watcher can follow. With her hands.

She points to a guard and says the camp word. Her fingers answer low against the mud: leashed tooth. She points to the slate man. Bought memory. She points to the black banner, gives the formal word with an empty face, then spits where no one can see.

That teaches me more than the word.

I learn not to trust the first word. The guard is a tooth. The slate man is bought memory. The banner is a warning everyone pretends to respect.

The Vorth have that too, but theirs lives in weight and posture, in who eats first, in how far a body sits from flame. Human lies can wear cleaner clothes. They can stand upright and call themselves order.

Yara teaches me both, though I cannot tell whether she means to.

Once she points toward herself and says the camp sound the captain uses for her.

Her mouth hates it.

Her fingers press to her chest afterward and give another sound, so small the rain almost eats it.

Her own.

I store both and understand that a name can be a collar if someone else fastens it.

On the fourth lesson, I give her something back.

Not my voice.

A stone.

She has no word for the pale markers I use along the rot paths, or she does not think to give it. When she says root and points at the path behind me, I push one pale stone into the open with the tip of a reed. She goes still, then looks at the ground around it. She reads the warning before I know how to teach it.

Soft.

Sink.

Bad ground.

Her mouth parts. Not fear. Understanding.

She says a word over the stone, then a different word over the mud beyond it. I take both.

The road is not only hers now.

Hunger teaches. Soon I know water and warning, fire for warmth and fire as threat. Rope. Net. Iron. Owned.

That last one makes her look away.

Marrow hates every lesson.

He waits above the line with his shoulder low and his crooked hand tight against his chest. Some evenings he brings pieces of home and sets them near my elbow: a river snail, a strip of roasted meat wrapped in broad leaf, a shard of bright shell from the high stones, once a little twist of dried moss from Kala's sleeping place. He does it without drama. Complaint would be easier.

Remember, each gift says.

I eat the meat.

I keep watching Yara.

She has rules of her own. Never the same path twice. Never more than a handful of words when the watch is fresh. Never fear on her face where the camp can read it. Around the owners she lowers her eyes. Around the caged she softens her voice. Around the captain she goes still enough to pass for obedient.

But at the mud line, when the dark hides her mouth from camp, she changes.

Not free. No. Freedom is too large a word for someone who must measure every breath against the nearest lash. But more herself. The eyes lift. The voice loses its flatness. Sometimes anger breaks through before she catches it. Sometimes pity. Once, when the caged Skell screams behind her and an owner laughs, she closes both hands in the mud until the fingers sink.

I understand that better than words.

I also see what happens after.

When Yara leaves the mud line, she does not return straight to the cage yard. She circles by the water rack, stops at the slate table, says two words to the older owned man, then carries a bowl to the captain's black awning. The first time I think it is only work. Everyone in camp carries something to someone above them. The camp breathes by carrying fear upward.

The second time, the captain asks her questions.

I am high in a leaning mangrove, wet bark pressed to my belly. I cannot hear every word. I hear enough rhythm to know he is not asking for water or cloth. His hand draws the line of the shore in the air. Then the mangroves. Then the high green. Yara's face stays empty.

She answers.

She lies.

She points to a false rise, one that floods at high tide and could hide nothing heavier than crabs. The captain watches her finger. He says something. She answers again. Her hand moves closer to the true path, not on it, not away. Close enough to feed his hunger. Far enough to keep mine alive.

My stomach knots.

The captain moves one patrol from the false rise to the creek mouth. Close enough.

Yara sees where he sends them and does not look at me again.

The captain lets her finish. Then he takes the bowl from her and drinks as if they have discussed only weather. When she turns away, his eyes go to the trees.

Not the false rise.

Higher.

Nearer.

He has learned from the part of her lie she could not hide.

Yara knows it too. I see it in the tightness of her shoulders as she walks back to the cages. She does not look at my hiding place. That mercy is almost cruel.

At the mud line that night, she teaches me the word for trap.

Then she teaches it again.

Then she points to herself.

Trap.

No. Not only that.

Used.

I cannot tell which meaning she wants me to keep.

The camp sickens by small degrees.

A man who laughed at swarms on the first day now scratches his neck until blood runs. Two owned men take turns vomiting behind the water barrels. A guard limps on a foot gone red around a thorn cut. The captain has ordered shade built over the Mure, boiled water for the marked men, smoke pots for the insects, dry sleeping racks above the mud.

He reads faster than the rest.

Not fast enough.

The island is already inside them.

One evening Yara brings a new word and does not point to anything.

She touches her chest.

Says the sound she uses for herself.

Then she points toward the place where I hide.

Waits.

My name rises so hard it hurts.

My name.

The one possession no hand took because no hand found it. My mother's gift, held behind broken teeth through hold, shore, green, fever, blood, crown.

Yara waits with the patience of someone offering, not taking.

Marrow's hand closes on my ankle from the shadow behind me.

Not hard.

Enough.

I say nothing.

Yara lowers her hand. The disappointment is small and quick, gone almost before I see it. She nods as if silence is also an answer, then points to me again and gives a different word.

Hidden.

Maybe.

Or ghost.

Or beast.

Or the one who sees.

I keep it anyway because she gave it to the dark without chains on it.

Then she points at herself and gives her own sound again.

Not Yara.

The true sound, maybe, or the camp sound, or one she can afford to spend.

I cannot give mine. The refusal tastes like old blood.

So I push another pale stone from the dark and set it beside the shell.

Path marker. Warning. Gift.

She looks at it, then at the shadow where I lie. Her face softens in a way that hurts more than disappointment.

She understands enough.

The next lesson, she brings the stone back.

For one cold breath I think it is rejection. She sets it where I can see it, then places three sticks beside it. One stick points to the camp. One to the mud line. One to the high green. She moves the stone along the wrong stick first and shakes her head. Then the camp stick and shakes her head harder. Then the high green. Her hand hovers there.

Home.

Her word for it is not the Vorth sound. It does not carry Kala's chest warmth or Marrow's shoulder at my knee. It is smaller, human, made of roof and fire and remembered doorways. Still, when she says it over the stick that leads uphill, the word cuts.

She knows I have somewhere to go.

That should comfort me.

Instead it makes me afraid.

Yara sees the fear. Her fingers move quickly.

Hide home.

Hide self.

Hide mark.

Then she touches the bruise on her cheek, old yellow now under new purple, and gives a small broken smile.

Bad at hiding, that smile says.

It startles a breath from my nose.

Not laughter. Close enough that Marrow, from the roots behind me, goes rigid with betrayal.

Yara hears the breath. Her eyes widen. For a heartbeat the mud line holds a warmth so thin it would tear under a finger.

Then a watchman coughs behind her, and the warmth is gone.

She leaves the stone.

After that, she teaches the words that move men. Come. Go. Hide. Eat. Hurt. See. Know. I understand pieces when the camp shouts. Not enough to be safe. Enough to know when the circle tightens.

One owner tells another the green is cursed.

One collared man whispers that the captain will not leave until he has what he came for.

One guard calls the Mure worthless, and the captain has him struck for damaging cargo with bad water.

Cargo.

That word I understand before Yara teaches it.

Rain comes early on the seventh lesson.

The camp blurs behind gray curtains. Watches hunch under hides. Smoke runs low. Yara comes anyway, hair slick against her face, bowl in hand as her excuse. She crouches closer than before because rain covers small sounds.

Her cheek is bruised fresh.

I move before I mean to.

A handspan. A leaf shifts wrong. Her eyes snap to me. Stay hidden.

I stop.

She touches the bruise, then points toward the captain's tent, then shakes her head once as if forbidding my anger before I can spend it.

I hate that she is right.

I hate more that she knows I am angry.

She speaks quickly then. Words I half know, half guess.

Captain.

Looking.

Mark.

Green.

I reach for the disc under my shirt without thinking.

The cord catches on wet cloth. The eye-and-thorn slips free.

Yara sees it.

Everything changes.

Her breath catches. The still mask vanishes. For one heartbeat she is only a girl in rain staring at a ghost from a story she hoped was not true. Her hand rises, not toward me, toward the mark. She draws it in the air: the open eye, the thorn crossing through.

Then she names it.

The word is low, old-sounding, and careful.

The word is closed to me.

I know the weight she puts on it.

She knows the mark.

The need in me surges so violently I nearly crawl into the open. I want to seize the word from her mouth and crack it apart. Ask why dead men carried it. Ask why the captain burns it onto cages. Ask why the seer bowed to it on my chest. Ask whether it is a name for me or a price or a curse or a key.

But I have no safe question.

And Yara is already afraid.

Not of me.

For me.

She wipes the mark she drew in the mud. Too late. Rain helps, but not enough. Her eyes dart once toward camp, then back to me.

She points at the disc.

Then the camp.

Then closes her fist slowly.

The hand is coming.

I understand.

She adds another word before Marrow pulls me back.

Not captain.

A name.

Two sounds. Hard first, soft second. The sound men make when they need the captain's attention and fear getting it.

Drevan.

Then another.

Sool.

She says them like a door she should not open.

The name enters me with the mark's old word still ringing behind it. A named danger is worse than an unnamed one. It can be followed. Remembered. Hated in a cleaner shape.

Yara points toward the awning.

Drevan Sool.

Then the mark.

Then me.

The hand is coming, yes.

Now it has a name.

Marrow pulls at my ankle. This time hard.

I slide back into the roots, heart hammering.

The next day Yara does not come.

I watch the camp from a higher blind and learn what absence means. She stays near the owner fire, not by choice. The captain speaks to her twice. Once he lifts his hand and does not strike.

A blow spends itself. A held blow owns the air.

Near dusk, he walks to the wet line himself.

Not alone. Men with hooks keep distance behind him, trying to look bored. The captain carries no net. No bow. Only a walking staff cut from a branch the camp stripped smooth. He plants it in the mud where Yara crouches for lessons and studies the roots above it.

I am not there. I am higher, where wind takes my scent.

Still, my skin tightens.

He looks at the pale stone I left beside the shell. He does not touch it at first. He circles it. Reads the mud. Reads the roots. Reads the height a watching body would need. Then he picks it up with two fingers and turns it once in his palm.

He smiles.

Small.

Certain.

The stone was a gift. In his hand it is a ruler laid against my throat.

She has told him.

Or he has read her.

Or someone saw rain move where rain should not move.

It does not matter. The camp knows something in the green wears the mark.

When Yara comes again, a watcher follows at a distance.

She knows.

Her body is tight with it, but she comes anyway. She crouches at my line and speaks ordinary words loudly enough for the watcher.

Shell.

Rain.

Tide.

Root.

Her low hand draws where only the mangrove shadow can see.

Eye.

Thorn.

Broken apart.

Then she points back toward camp and closes her fist.

The hand is coming.

Again.

This time she adds another sign. Two fingers walking. Then many fingers closing around them.

Not just one hand.

Men.

Nets.

Soon.

Yara looks straight into the place where I hide. Not at a spirit. Not at the island. At me.

She knows I am a person.

The knowledge lands gentler than Kala's hand and more dangerous than the captain's mark.

The watcher calls.

Yara rises at once. The mask returns. She walks back to camp without looking over her shoulder.

I lie in the roots after she is gone, holding too much behind my teeth.

My hidden name.

The brand-sound from the hold.

The road of words Yara is laying stone by stone.

The mark's old name, closed to me.

Her warning.

The seer saw the mark and bowed. The captain sees it and reaches. Both know value when it hangs from a boy's neck. Neither tells the boy what price he carries.

That night I return late to the high stones.

Kala is waiting. So is Marrow. The seer stands near the dead shelf with both hands on his staff, his blind eye turned toward the coast as if listening to a storm still beyond the horizon.

Kala smells the camp smoke on me.

She smells Yara too, maybe. Human fear. Rain. Rope. The strange new road opening under my skin.

This time she holds me, but not as before. Her arm comes around me with care, and the care has a question inside it.

Where do you go?

Marrow crouches on my other side, angry enough not to touch me.

I have words now. Shell. Fire. Cage. Captain. Pain. Hand. Hidden.

Not enough for this.

So I say nothing.

For the first time since fever brought me into her arms, Kala holds me and cannot close all the space between us.
