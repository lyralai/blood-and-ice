# Chapter 9: Taken In

The fever finds me at the channel after bad eggs, red insects, salt cuts, and the disc.

For six days I watch the young one come down with the tide and climb back with its gleaning. I learn its path. I learn where it pauses, where it looks, which calls make it hurry and which make it answer with annoyance.

On the fifth day it leaves three cracked shells on a flat stone beneath my ridge.

Not waste. Not forgotten. Set.

I stare at them until the tide comes in.

The meat is gone, but the gesture remains, and gestures are more dangerous than knives because they ask you to guess the hand behind them. I leave the shells untouched. All night I think of them sitting white under moonlight, an offering or a trap or a joke made by a creature that should not know I am there.

On the seventh day, the island turns on me.

It begins as heat behind the eyes. Then the throat closes. The gut knots. My legs go weak beneath me while the sun is still high. I drink rainwater from a leaf and tell myself the wrongness in my blood will win again.

It always has.

That does not make the burning easier.

By dawn I am out of the den and crawling downhill. Fever hates walls. It wants open air, running water, mud cool against the skin. I know better than to expose myself, but knowing is a small lamp in a storm.

The channel stones are bare when I reach them.

I collapse in the mud and drink like an animal.

It stops at the edge of the stones with a crab in one hand.

I lift my head. The world swims. The young one's dark shape doubles, blurs, becomes one again. Its small eyes fix on me. Not hungry. Not cruel. Afraid, maybe. Curious.

My lips peel back.

The sound in my chest is meant to warn it away. It comes out cracked and weak.

The young one answers with a low note.

Soft. Rising and falling. I have heard the same sound from the high stones when the small ones cry. It is not command. Not threat. A sound for calming another living creature.

That frightens me more.

Cruelty I understand. Kindness has too many hidden doors.

The young one sees the flat stone where the shells were.

The tide took them. Or the young one thinks I did. Its gaze flicks from the stone to my mouth, my hands, the mud dragged by my knees. Some bright thought moves behind its eyes, too quick for fever to catch.

Then it backs away, turns, and runs up the green toward the tribe.

To bring the many.

I try to crawl.

My hand finds a root. My knees slide. Mud fills the fresh cuts on my shin. The green tilts around me, bright and sick. I get three lengths from the water before my arms fail.

Better the jungle than chains.

That is my last clear thought.

Then the fever takes the light.

I wake against warmth.

Not stone warmed by sun. Not rot. Not the little fire.

Living warmth.

It moves under me with breath. A slow heart beats against my ear. A heavy arm lies across my back, not pinning, not crushing, only holding me in place while I shake.

I know the hand that owns. The hand that closes on the neck. The hand that says stillness or pain, breath or pain.

This is not that hand.

The arm over me is vast. It could break me without effort. Instead it keeps me from rolling into the cold edge of the stone. It holds warmth around my fever and makes the soft rising sound again and again, until even the frightened animal part of me has to admit the truth.

I am being kept.

I burn for two days among the Vorth.

I have not learned that word yet. It will come later from their mouths, one sound at a time. At first they are only the people of the high stones. The many. The ones with fire.

They lay me beneath leaning trees near the shelf of the dead. Their bodies move around me in pieces: a young face peering too close, a gray hand pushing it back, the shadow of the king above the fire, the scarred ambitious one watching from the edge as if measuring whether I am omen or meat.

Mostly there is the keeper.

She is old, gray along the muzzle, heavy in the chest, with one torn ear and hands broad enough to cover my whole back. She smells of smoke, wet hide, milk-sour grief, and old leaves. When the fever throws me, she gathers me in. When I snarl, she waits. When I sleep, I wake to find her still there.

She has lost a child.

I learn that before I learn her name.

On the clean stone above us lies a small body, laid with the others. The keeper goes to it at dawn. She touches its still head, then her own chest. Her sound changes there. It deepens. Breaks. There are no words in it I know, but grief does not need translation.

Afterward she comes back and folds herself around me.

I am not her lost child.

She knows that.

I know that.

For a few fevered nights her holding and my not-being-held fit close enough to save my life.

The tribe does not all agree.

Fever carries me in and out, but I see enough. The missing-thumb male brings water once and sets it near Kala without looking at me. The one-clouded female brings bitter leaves and spits in the dust after she lays them down. A younger mother with milk-heavy breasts stands above us for a long while with her own small one clinging to her side. She looks at the dead shelf. Then at me. Then at Kala.

Her sound is soft.

Soft does not mean kind.

Kala's answer is softer.

The younger mother leaves shaking her head, and that night she sleeps with her back to us. In the morning she still sends a strip of mashed fruit through a child too small to be blamed for kindness.

I do not understand the words. I understand the bodies: lowered heads, hands kept from touching, Kala's grief held so tight it shakes.

Kala's arms around me are not free. Every hour she spends keeping my fever from pulling me apart is an hour she is not sitting beside the small dead one. Every scrap she presses into my mouth comes from some other mouth's patience. Every time she shields me from curious hands, she reminds the tribe that her own child is beyond shielding.

Once, at dusk, the old seer calls from the dead shelf.

Kala rises. I feel the absence of her before my eyes open. Cold enters the hollow she leaves. I hear her climb the stone, slow for once, as if each step is a choice with teeth in it.

The dead-sound begins above.

I cannot see the ritual from where I lie, only the red edge of sunset and the shapes of heads turning upward. The tribe answers. The note moves through the stones and into my sick bones. Kala's voice is in it, larger and more broken than the rest.

Then my fever turns.

The old hold opens. The planks are wet. A man is laughing with a rope in his hand. I am small under him, too hot, too cold, and my hidden name is a coal in my mouth. I claw at the stone beneath me and make a sound that tears my throat.

The dead-sound stops.

Kala comes down before the last note is finished.

That is the cost I remember when the fever clears. Not fruit, water, or glares from those who think a stranger has been given too much.

She leaves her dead to hold the living.

No one says this to me.

No one needs to.

On the second morning after that, I wake enough to see what leaving costs.

Kala carries water to the dead shelf in a bowl of folded bark. Her steps are heavy. Fever has made the world bright and cruel, and every movement cuts itself into me. I watch her climb. Watch the old seer move aside. Watch the one-clouded female turn her face away.

The small body on the shelf has changed.

Not much. Enough.

The wet heat has begun its work at the edges. The leaves around it have browned. Tiny flies lift and settle. Kala makes a sound I have never heard from her before, not loud, not broken, but empty in a way that pulls the air from my chest.

She was away too long.

For me.

The seer reaches to help.

Kala refuses with one sharp motion.

She washes the little face herself. She cleans the ears, the fingers, the soft folds where damp has gathered. Her hands remain gentle. Her shoulders shake only once. When the flies return, she drives them away with such fury that one child below begins to cry.

I try to rise.

My arms fail.

The young gleaner, the one who found me, sees and scrambles to my side. It pushes me down with both hands, then bares its teeth as if daring me to disobey. The gesture is so like a tiny bad copy of Kala that I almost laugh, but fever has stolen laughter for now.

Above, Kala finishes the washing.

She puts no shell beside the small hand. The shell is on my chest.

Instead she breaks one bead from a cord around her own wrist and lays that beside the dead child. The cord is old, dark with wear. When the bead leaves it, the remaining knots hang loose.

The one-clouded female sees.

Her mouth tightens.

She does not turn away this time.

Later, when Kala comes back to me, one wrist is bare where the cord used to hold its pattern. I touch the empty place before I think.

She lets me.

Then she takes my hand and presses it flat against my own chest, over shell and mark and heart, as if to say: know what was spent here.

I know.

Not enough. Never enough.

But I begin.

It is not simple. Nothing living is.

Once, when the fever throws me hard and I strike her jaw with my fist, she catches my wrist. Her grip closes and the old hold opens under me all at once: dark planks, iron stink, breath counted out by another's hand. I bite her.

The tribe snarls.

She does not.

She lets my teeth stay in her hide until I taste blood, hers and mine together. Then she loosens one finger at a time and lays my wrist against my own chest, where my heart is hammering like a trapped bird. Her sound comes low and stern.

Still.

Not obedience. Not pain. A wall put between me and the place I have fallen into.

I hate her for needing it.

I cling to her before the hate has finished.

When the fever breaks, the tribe sees.

The green's sickness has taken their own. I understand it from the way they watch my skin cool, the way they touch the air near me but not me, the way whispers pass from throat to throat. They carried me up expecting death. I wake instead. Too soon. Too clean.

The island tried to eat me in front of witnesses and failed.

That changes the air.

The king watches me longer.

The scarred one watches me harder.

The small ones creep nearer until the keeper bares her teeth and scatters them.

The young gleaner is among them.

It holds one of the white shells from the channel. I know the chipped lip of it. Somehow it found the same piece the tide took and brought it up the stone. It sets the shell beside my hand, then darts backward when I move.

I should not care.

My fingers close around it anyway.

Then the oldest comes.

She is nearly white around the face, all angles under loose hide, her eyes clouded but not blind. The tribe makes room for her. Even the king lowers his head a little.

The old one crouches beside me.

I force myself not to move.

She studies my face, my hands, the healed cuts, the fever-sweat drying on my skin. Then her gaze drops to my chest.

To the disc.

The eye-and-thorn.

Her hand stops in the air.

For the first time since I saw the Vorth, all their sounds die at once.

The old one reaches with one long finger and touches the mark. Not my skin. The mark. Her breath leaves her in a low note that makes the keeper go still beside me.

She knows it.

The wanting in me rises so fast I nearly grab her wrist.

Tell me.

The words are in my head, useless. I have no shared speech. No bridge. Only my stare and the metal at my chest and the old one looking at me as if a dead story has crawled out of the jungle wearing a boy's face.

She bows her head.

Not much. Just enough.

The others see it.

Something passes through them. Fear, reverence, suspicion. Maybe all three. The scarred one's lips draw back. The king's eyes narrow. The keeper's arm tightens around me.

The mark still keeps its answer.

But now I know it means something here.

The bow is not safety. I learn that from the way the scarred one watches it settle over me.

After the old seer leaves, the scarred one steps closer.

The tribe parts for him in a way it does not part for curiosity. He is not king, but the path opens as if practicing. His side scar catches firelight. His hands hang loose. No weapon. No lifted lip. Only attention sharpened to a point.

Kala shifts between us.

The scarred one looks at her, then at the shell beside my hand, then at the wet hides under me. His sound is low. Too measured to be a snarl. He points to the dead shelf, where the small body waits in morning's old light. Then he points to me.

Even fever cannot hide the meaning.

Which dead are you keeping?

The air tightens.

Kala's shoulders rise. For one breath she looks old enough to crumble. Then she takes the white shell from my hand and sets it on my chest, directly over the eye-and-thorn. Her palm covers both. Metal, shell, heart.

Her answer is a growl too deep for softness.

This living one.

The scarred one does not strike. That would be easier to hate. He only gives a short sound that makes several adults lower their eyes.

Waste.

No. Not exactly. The word is not mine yet, but I know its taste. Meat used where meat should not be used. A hand busy where the tribe needs it elsewhere. A mother's grief turned sideways.

Part of me agrees with him.

If I were watching from the ridge, I might think the same. The green kills. Children die. Food rots. Fire needs tending. Why spend strength on a fevered stranger with a dead man's mark?

The scarred one's anger has a clean bone inside it before I know his name.

Almost right is more dangerous than wrong.

The old seer gives me weight I did not ask for. The keeper gives me warmth I have not learned how to carry. The young gleaner gives me a shell. The king gives me nothing.

All of them are gifts. I keep my hands closed because gifts have bitten me before.

That night, the kept fire burns low and red. I lie against the keeper's side while the tribe settles around us. Their voices move through the dark, rough and living. The small ones sleep in knots. The dead rest on the high stone. Smoke climbs into the stars.

I go behind my teeth and touch the name hidden there.

My name.

I could give it to the keeper. Part of me wants to. Wants to set the sound in her great careful hands and see if she holds it the way she holds me.

I keep it back.

Not yet.

A name is not the first gift you give strangers.

So I keep mine hidden and take what she gives instead: the gentling sound, low and warm, laid over me whenever the old fear rises.

The tribe's silence closes around it.

I know what it does.

For the first time since the hold, I sleep among other bodies and wake unchained.
