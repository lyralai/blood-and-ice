# Chapter 24: The Two Hungers at War

I tell myself I go down for answers.

The mark. Drevan Sool. Yara's words. The dead expedition. The men sickening in the camp. The old name she spoke when she saw the eye-and-thorn at my chest.

All true.

Truth can still be a lie if it hides the larger truth behind it.

I go down because I want to.

Because the food is warm.

Because Yara looks into the dark and knows a person is there.

Because human voices move through the camp like a river I was born beside and lost before I learned its name.

Because some part of me still believes a hand from my own kind might stay unclosed.

That part is a fool.

It is also me.

The camp is quiet when we reach the roots. Too quiet. No low argument near the water barrels. No owner's laugh. No caged Skell scraping at the wood. Even the swarms seem to hold farther back from the fires, black knots circling without landing.

Marrow stops before I do.

His head turns once toward camp, once toward me.

Trap.

I know.

I go lower in the mud.

Dusk thickens. The wet flats turn from bronze to gray. Behind us the high stones are hidden by green and slope and all the choices I have already failed to make.

No meat comes.

No water.

Yara comes instead.

She walks alone, which means she is not alone at all.

Her hands are empty. Her face is pale under the grime. She keeps her eyes lowered until she reaches the mud line. Then she looks straight into the roots where I lie and begins to speak.

Not the teaching voice.

Not the camp voice.

Something between warning and plea.

I know more words now. Enough to catch pieces.

Danger.

Mark.

You.

Captain.

Come.

That last one lands badly. Not command. Not exactly. A question with rope under it. A plea sharpened into a hook. Maybe she has been ordered to say it. Maybe she means it. Maybe both are true, and she has been given a knife with no clean handle.

Her hand, low in the mud, draws the closing fist again.

Then two lines.

Camp.

Green.

Between them, the eye-and-thorn, split by one finger.

I understand pieces. Drevan will come. The mark has made me too valuable to leave in the dark. If I stay hidden, he will stop feeding the line and start cutting toward it.

Yara's fingers move faster.

Men.

Nets.

Fire.

High.

I miss the next word and hate myself for missing it.

High what? High stones? High path? High watchers?

Behind me, Marrow makes a sound I have never heard from him.

Small. Hurt. Angry.

He knows this is the true bait.

So do I.

Yara looks into the roots and whispers another word.

Name?

The world narrows to the space behind my teeth.

My name waits there. Warm. Hidden. The one possession never taken because I never gave the world a chance. My mother's gift, carried through hold and green and fever and blood, through Kala's arms and Gull-Marr's lip, through every place that wanted to rename me as owned, beast, omen, cargo.

I could give it to her.

She is shaped to catch it. She could say it back. She could make it real in air, not just in the dark of my own mouth.

The need is so strong my lips part.

Marrow's hand closes around my ankle.

Not hard.

Enough.

I stay silent.

Yara sees the almost-answer. Her face folds inward, not with surprise. With grief, maybe. Or relief. She knows what it costs to keep a name out of reach. She nods once, as if I answered after all.

Then she does something beyond my understanding.

She touches her own chest and gives her true name.

Not the camp name I have heard owners use. Not the sound I made for her in my head. A smaller word. Older. Hers.

She gives it to the dark quickly, before fear can take it back.

The name is not beautiful because of its sound.

It is beautiful because it is hers and she is afraid when she gives it.

I have seen men throw meat to earn a step. I have seen Drevan lay bait and call it welcome. This is not that. Or it is, and it is also braver than that, and the two truths cut across each other until I cannot separate wound from gift.

Then she turns and walks toward camp.

The watchers move too late for anyone but me to see: two shapes in the cut brush, one near the water, one behind thornwood. A third farther back, patient enough not to shift until she passes.

Drevan has set her out and watched the line.

He did not catch me.

He learned where my silence lives.

That is almost as bad.

I leave the offering place untouched after she goes. There is nothing there. That was the point. The captain has changed the food again. Warm meat. Salt. Words. Name.

Now choice.

I go back to the high stones before full dark for the first time in many days.

The tribe is waiting.

Not gathered by accident. Gathered the way bodies gather before a challenge, before judgment, before a pressure everyone has felt finally takes shape. Kala stands near the dead shelf. The seer waits before my lip. Voss-Marr stands near the far fire with his young strong ones, his head high and his mouth almost gentle.

That gentleness is the danger.

He does not need to rage. I have brought him better weapons.

Camp smoke in my hair.

Human salt on my tongue.

The mark cold under my shirt.

The seer touches the eye-and-thorn on my chest. Points toward the coast. Points toward the dead shelf. Then lifts both hands and lets them fall open.

Meaning.

Danger.

Death.

No metaphor. No omen fog. A warning as plain as old hands can make it.

Voss-Marr gives a short hard sound from across the fire.

Some of the tribe answer.

I know enough Vorth now to hear the shape.

Let him go.

Human trouble.

Human king.

The words hit differently because I understand them. Ignorance is a kind of hide. Language takes it off.

The first answer comes from one of Voss-Marr's young strong ones, a broad male with a split brow and new scars laid proudly across his chest. He makes the sound for coast and spits into the ash. Another answers with ship. Another with iron. They are not only insulting me now. They are building a path the tribe can follow.

Coast brings ship.

Ship brings iron.

Iron brings dead.

Then the broad male points at the disturbed edge of the shelf where the youngest ones sleep and makes the sign for danger carried in fur.

Me.

The discarded ones bristle. Stub lumbers halfway to his feet with a little one tucked under each arm. Torn-Ear bares the broken side of his mouth. Grist makes a rasping sound deep in the chest, the sound old bark makes when fire has found it.

Voss-Marr lets them answer.

That is clever too.

If he speaks alone, it is jealousy. If the young strong ones speak and the center listens, it becomes concern. A tribe can forgive concern almost anything.

The scarred female steps forward from the edge of my fire.

She does not speak often. Her throat was torn long before I came, and many of her sounds scrape raw. But when she points to me, then to the Hesh skull kept above the challenge stones, then to the little ones under Stub's arms, everyone understands.

He saved.

Voss-Marr's eyes move to her.

Not cruelly.

Worse. Patiently.

He touches the Hesh scar on his own shoulder, where my trap saved him from dying under the apex's mouth. Then he touches the dead shelf. Then the coast.

Saved once.

Doomed now.

A murmur runs through the bodies around us. Not agreement. Not refusal. The awful middle where fear has enough room to grow roots.

I want to hate him cleanly.

I cannot.

He is using the truth.

Every trip down has pulled human attention closer. Every offering eaten has taught Drevan where to lay the next one. Every word Yara gave me has given the camp a road back into my wanting. Voss-Marr twists those truths toward his own hunger for the lip, but the truths remain sharp.

That is what makes him dangerous.

Not lies.

Truth without love.

Marrow lunges before I can move. He goes for Voss-Marr with teeth bared and crooked hand hooked like a claw. Two young males block him. He is small, but fury gives him speed. For a breath I see the whole shelf tilt toward violence.

"Marrow."

My voice stops him.

That hurts too.

He freezes with his teeth a handspan from a young male's arm. The whole tribe sees him obey me when every part of him wants blood.

Voss-Marr sees it best.

His almost-gentle look deepens.

He has found the clean place to put his anger. Not in a blow, where I can answer. Not in a challenge, where the tribe would remember the lip and the Hesh and the day Gull-Marr stepped aside. He puts it in the tribe's oldest fear.

Human trouble belongs with humans.

A king who leaves the fire leaves the right to warm his hands at it.

There is enough truth in both that the tribe can stand near them without shame.

Kala rises.

The shelf quiets.

She does not threaten Voss-Marr. She does not need to. She crosses to me, each step heavy with age and authority and the grief I have put into her bones. She touches the eye-and-thorn. Then she presses her hand over my hidden heart.

Her sound is low, torn with more than age.

Stay.

That word I know.

Stay.

The two roads stand open inside me.

Behind one: Kala, Marrow, the dead shelf, the tribe I won and am losing, the small ones sleeping under a safety I helped make.

Behind the other: Yara, the mark's name, human speech, the captain's closing hand, the answer that might finally tell me why my blood lives wrong.

The right choice is obvious.

So obvious it becomes useless.

I take Kala's hand and put it against my cheek.

For a moment I let myself be the fevered boy she carried from the mud. The kept one. The one who had not yet learned that home creates new ways to be afraid.

Her thumb moves once along the scar at my jaw.

I could stay.

The words open inside me with such force that I almost believe them.

Then I remember Yara's sign.

Men. Nets. Fire. High.

High.

High stones?

If Drevan has read the line all the way back to home, staying may only mean waiting for the hand to close around everyone instead of me.

That thought might be truth.

It might be the hunger wearing truth's face.

I cannot tell anymore.

I step back.

Kala makes no sound.

Marrow does.

It breaks out of him like something torn open.

I cannot look at him.

If I do, I will stay.

I go down from the high stones while the tribe watches. Not running. Not sneaking. A king walking away from his lip toward the one hand everyone can see closing.

I tell myself I am choosing before Drevan chooses for me.

Maybe that is true.

Maybe bait becomes choice if you walk into it with open eyes.

Or maybe that is what every trapped animal tells itself in the last breath before the noose pulls tight.

At the first bend, Marrow catches up.

Of course he does.

His face is wet. Rain, maybe.

Not rain.

He puts himself in front of me and bares his teeth.

No.

One small body against the whole down road.

I touch his crooked shoulder. He shakes under my hand.

"Stay," I tell him, using Kala's word.

He spits at my feet.

Then he strikes me.

Not hard enough to wound. Hard enough to turn my face. His curled hand catches my cheek with the awkward strength of something never made for gentleness and giving it anyway in the only shape it has left.

I let the sting sit there.

He points uphill.

Kala.

Home.

Then he points to me.

Fool.

That word he does not have, but his body speaks it perfectly.

I almost laugh. If I do, it will become something else, so I swallow it.

"I know," I say.

He hates that answer.

So do I.

For a long moment neither of us moves. The green breathes around us. Far below, the camp smoke rises through leaves. Above, home waits with its fires and its wounded silence.

Marrow steps aside.

Not because he agrees.

Because he knows I am already gone.

I leave him there and walk toward the coast with my name locked behind my teeth, the mark cold on my chest, and both hungers tearing at the same heart.

I have not gone twenty paces when Marrow begins following again.

I keep my face toward the coast.

This time I let him.

We walk without speaking.

After a while his shoulder brushes my leg once. Hard enough to be an accusation. Soft enough to be company.

That is Marrow's mercy.

It does not absolve.

It stays.
