# Chapter 8: The People

There are people in the green.

I see them from the high ridge after a hunt, when the morning steam lifts enough to show the low country. At first I think they are shadows moving under the canopy. Then one steps into a break of light and the shape of it goes through me like cold water.

Upright.

Not human.

Not beast.

People.

The word rises before I can stop it.

They move along both sides of a brown channel in a loose line. Big shoulders. Long arms. Dark wet hide. Sometimes they go on two legs, sometimes on knuckles and feet, switching as the ground asks. They are larger than men, heavier through the shoulders, but their movement has thought in it.

A herd follows pressure.

These choose.

I flatten against the stone.

The old fear comes fast. Hands. Chains. A voice making a command out of my flesh. People are the shape of want, but they are also the shape of ownership. I learned both in the hold, where every voice belonged to someone stronger.

My legs want the den.

The ache under my ribs wants the ridge.

I stay.

For three days I watch them from hiding.

They have made their place on a shelf of pale stone across the low country. The shelf rises above the wet green where the air moves and the sun reaches first. Great trees lean over it. Vines hang like ropes. Smoke lifts there in the evening.

It is not a nest. This place is made for returning. Paths come to it from many sides: a worn lip down to the channel, a slant of stone toward the fruit trees, a narrow crack where the small ones squeeze through and the large ones pretend not to see.

Smoke.

They keep fire.

Not a coal in a fist of fiber. Not a little flame coaxed under a stone roof. A true kept fire, red at the heart, fed by many hands. At night it glows against the rock and paints their bodies in copper.

They have a king. I know him without needing the word. The largest one sits highest, gray at the muzzle, old scars crossing his chest. Others make room around him. Their eyes go to him before they move. Power bends the air the same in every tribe.

Near him waits another, younger, with a pale scar down his side and ambition in the way he stands too still. I have seen that stillness in animals deciding whether to bite.

There are wounded among them. One drags a leg, and the others slow for her. No herd I know would do that for long. The jungle teaches the strong to leave the weak. These people disobey the jungle.

There are small ones too.

Little dark bodies cling to backs and chests. They tumble over stone, get scooped up, get pushed away, come back again. A broad-handed female lifts one by the scruff and folds it into her lap while it kicks and complains. Another carries two, one clinging to each side like burrs.

I watch until my throat hurts.

No one ever lifted me that way after the hold.

The dead have a place among them.

On the highest clean shelf, where the sun lays its first white hand, they set the dead out in rows. Not abandoned. Not eaten. Set. A big gray female sits beside them for hours, touching one still face, then another. Her shoulders move with a sound I cannot hear from so far, but I know grief when I see the shape of it.

They keep fire.

They keep children.

They keep their dead.

Tribe.

The word comes slowly, from the old place under the old pain. I cannot remember learning it. I only know it fits.

The fit hurts.

A word can be a blade if there is nowhere to put it.

I learn their days before I learn their names.

At first I think they all move by hunger. Dawn comes, and the adults go down to the channels or into the green. Noon comes, and they sleep with one eye open in whatever shade the stone gives. Evening comes, and meat or fruit or shell is brought to the fire. That much any pack might do.

Then I see the rest.

One broad male with a missing thumb sits every morning near the little crack where the smallest ones squeeze through. He does not hunt. He does not tend the fire. He watches the children with a patience so deep it looks like sleep until one climbs too high, then his hand is there before falling becomes harm. The small ones climb over his shoulders when they pass. He grumbles at each of them. They ignore him with joy.

An old female with one cloudy eye breaks shellfish with a stone, but she gives every third shell to the lame one who drags her leg. Not every second. Not when others are watching. Every third, as exact as tide. The lame one never thanks her. The old one never asks thanks. They both pretend the shells arrive by weather.

A young hunter with red-brown streaks in his hide brings back fruit, not meat, and gives it first to the mothers with milk. A larger male cuffs him for taking too much from the high branches. The young one bares his teeth. The mothers make a low sound together. The larger male backs away with pride stiff in every joint, pretending he has chosen mercy.

Rules. Favors. Small thefts. Shame. Pride. Hidden ropes tying the tribe together and tripping anyone who cannot see them.

I watch until my knees ache and my hunger complains.

The high stones are not peaceful. That is another truth I must learn. The strong shove. The small bite. Two young males wrestle until one has blood on his lip and the watching adults do nothing. A mother snarls at another mother for letting a child steal marrow from her pile. A gray male takes the best place by the fire because no one dares ask him to move.

But even their cruelty has edges.

The loser of the wrestling is mocked, then given water. The thief child is cuffed, then fed. The gray male sleeps, and one of the little ones paints his foot with berry juice while the others shake from silent laughter. When he wakes, he roars so loudly birds leap from the trees, but he leaves the red mark on his foot all evening.

I have no practice with people who can hurt and keep in the same breath.

They argue over water too.

A spring seeps from the stone above their fire shelf, not much more than a shining thread, but the Vorth have shaped a hollow beneath it with clay and leaf. Rain fills the world, yet clean water still has order. Children cannot put muddy hands in the hollow. Hunters drink after mothers. Wounded drink before the proud, unless the wound is from foolishness and everyone saw it happen.

I learn this because a young male with a fresh bite on his shoulder tries to drink before the lame female.

The missing-thumb watcher blocks him.

The young male bares his teeth. He is larger by half. The watcher is old, broad, and missing strength where the thumb should be. For a breath I think blood will come.

The watcher points to the bite. Then to the young male's own teeth. Then to a laughing pair of youths with blood still on their knuckles. He makes a dry sound that carries.

Fool wound.

The children repeat it at once because children are mercy's enemies when mockery is free.

The young male endures this for three breaths, then gives way to the lame female with rage crawling under his hide. She drinks without thanking him. The watcher drinks last, when the hollow has nearly emptied and must fill again drop by drop.

They are kept by rule, by witnesses, by old hands willing to stand where young strength wants to pass.

No wonder the scarred young male watches rules the way I watch snares. He marks who drinks first, who lowers their eyes, who dares stand in the path while the hollow fills drop by drop.

I cannot know all of it yet.

But some part of me hears the spring drip and understands that water can start fights before anyone is thirsty.

The dead shelf teaches me most.

At dawn the gray female goes up with a bowl of water and a bundle of leaves. Others follow only to the first step below, then wait. A small one tries to climb after her and is pulled back by the missing-thumb male. The child wails. The old female looks down, says one sharp sound, and the child goes quiet as if a law has touched its spine.

She washes the faces of the dead.

Not because they are dirty. Rain has already washed them. She does it because the hand remembers what the mouth cannot ask for anymore. She wets the leaves, wipes eyelids, ears, fingers, the hollows where the living smell gathers and then leaves. Each touch is slow. No hurry. No hunger.

When she finishes, she lays a small gift beside each body. A shell. A red stone. A stripped twig bound in grass. One dead male gets a broken tooth. The old female presses it into his palm and makes a sound so low I feel it through the ridge more than hear it.

The tribe below answers.

Every throat. Even the children.

A single rough note rises, held until the air seems to bow under it. I feel the sound in my teeth. It is not weeping. It is not song. It is a weight carried by many backs so one back does not break.

I press my hand over the disc at my chest.

In the hold, the dead went overboard if the slavers were not hungry enough to sell them sick. In the jungle, the dead became beetles, root, heat, smell. Here the dead remain people for a little while. They are given morning. They are given hands.

The wanting gets a new tooth: to be counted when heads turn toward the fire.

It is no longer only hunger for warmth.

It is hunger to be counted after I am gone.

On the fourth day, one comes down alone.

Young. Not small, but not fully grown. Long-limbed, quick, curious in a way that would get a lesser creature killed. It leaves the high stones when the tide pulls back and descends toward the channel below my ridge.

My channel.

My mud.

I should retreat.

Instead I follow from above, silent through the broken green.

The young one crouches at the bared stones and begins to glean.

It turns rocks with care. Snatches crabs before they fold away. Picks pale shellfish from roots. It works the low tide as I worked it when I first came ashore, with patience and hunger and hands that know the difference between bad mud and food.

Then it finds one of my old shell piles under a root.

The sight strikes through me. I left those shells there three nights ago, cracked and scraped clean, a secret mark of my own passing. The young one crouches over them. It sniffs the pile, tilts its head, and looks up toward the ridge.

Toward me.

I hold my breath until my ribs ache.

It could call. The whole tribe could pour down with stones and hands and teeth. Instead the young one picks through the shells and takes a piece I missed, a stubborn curl of meat hidden deep in a spiral. It eats my mistake, makes a pleased grunt, and keeps gleaning.

The insult is so small and so perfect that I nearly make a sound.

The sight cuts me more deeply than fear.

I thought the island taught me alone.

Here is another creature reading the same lesson.

A call comes from the high stones.

The young one lifts its head and answers.

The sound is shaped. Not human words, but language all the same. Need, warning, affection, place. A line thrown across the green from the high fire to the lone forager, saying: we know where you are. Come back.

My own mouth opens.

Nothing comes out but a rough breath.

I could say my name. I have it. I have kept it through hunger, shipwreck, fever, and the hold. It waits behind my broken teeth, warm and hidden.

But a kept name is not the same as a shared one.

To throw it across the channel would be to hand it to strangers. To people with hands large enough to close around me. To a tribe that might keep children or might keep slaves if the hunger turned.

My tongue stays still.

My name remains where my mother put it.

The young one gathers its food and climbs back toward the fire. Halfway up, an older voice calls again, sharper this time. The young one hurries, and when it reaches the shelf, another Vorth cuffs it lightly and then pulls it close.

Punishment and welcome in one motion.

I know what a blow means. I know what arms mean. I have never seen both answer the same mistake.

That night I sit in my den beside a smaller fire than theirs and listen to the far sounds of the tribe: low calls, cracking wood, the small ones' thin cries, the deeper rumble of adults settling. The eye-and-thorn rests cold against my chest.

I want to go to them.

I want never to be seen.

Both wants are true. Both have teeth.

Before sleep takes me, I hear one small voice cry out on the far stone. A larger voice answers at once.

Where.

Here.

The line crosses the dark and does not break.
