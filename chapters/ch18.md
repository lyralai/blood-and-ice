# Chapter 18: King of the Wild Things

Gull-Marr clears the stone at first light.

The tribe moves to the edges. Small ones are held high. The dead look down from their clean shelf. The seer stands apart with his pale head lifted.

Voss-Marr watches from the side.

This should have been his future. His father on the stone. His own strength waiting below. The line whole and clear. Instead I stand in the center with bare feet, a dead man's knife at my hip, and no strength worth naming.

Gull-Marr comes slowly.

The slow walk is part of the fight. It says I have already measured you. It says the stone is mine until I choose to let your blood touch it.

I believe him.

That helps.

Believing a danger lets you read it.

He rushes only at the end.

Old strength becomes sudden strength. One arm swings to take me from the stone. I drop under it, not away, in. The arm cuts air over my back. I go low at his knee with all my weight, not striking muscle but borrowing it. His leg expects the ground to hold one way. I ask it another.

The knee folds.

Gull-Marr falls.

The whole tribe inhales.

He is already rising.

Of course he is. I cannot break him. The fall only proves the lesson I brought with me from the mud: strength can be read. Strength can be turned. Strength can be made to answer ground it never noticed.

The second rush is not the first.

Gull-Marr does not spend anger where I expect it. He slows. One broad hand lowers, fingers spread, and he begins to circle. The stone is not large enough for circling, but he makes it feel large by making me choose where to give ground. Behind me is the lip. To my left, slick ash. To my right, the shallow groove where rain gathers and makes a foot lie.

He sees all of it.

So do I.

For a moment we are both reading the same page.

Then he tears it.

He stamps into the ash, not slipping, making the gray dust leap into my eyes. I turn my face too late. His backhand catches my shoulder and throws me across the groove. Pain lights my arm from neck to fingertips. The tribe barks sound. Kala's sound cuts through them, half roar, half grief.

I roll because falling bodies taught me to roll long before anyone taught me to stand. Gull-Marr's foot comes down where my ribs were. Stone cracks under his heel.

If that lands, I am meat.

I come up half blind, one arm numb, mouth full of ash.

Gull-Marr waits.

Not mercy.

Instruction.

He wants the tribe to see I can be touched. He wants the young hunters to see that cleverness bleeds. He wants me to know the first fall did not make him small.

It works.

I am afraid again.

Good.

Fear keeps count.

My left arm hangs badly. Not broken, I think. It does not answer well enough to know. The dead man's knife rests at my hip, allowed by no rule anyone has spoken. I could draw it. A sharp edge solves distance.

It also answers the wrong question.

Not killing. Witness.

Gull-Marr comes again, low this time, one hand feinting high while the other sweeps for my leg. I let him have the leg.

Not all of it.

Enough.

His fingers close around my ankle. The grip is iron. He yanks, and the stone leaps sideways under me. I fall onto my back and he is over me, all gray hide and old scars, one hand rising to pin my throat.

The hold comes back.

Dark planks. Wet breath. A hand on the back of my neck. The command to become small or be made smaller.

The Red opens so fast it has no heat at first. Only clarity.

Bite the wrist.

Take the eye.

Open the soft place under the arm.

No.

His fingers brush my throat.

I twist toward them, not away. My good hand catches his thumb and folds it back against the joint. Not enough to break. Enough to turn the whole arm for one breath. My knee comes up under his ribs where the boar tusk once taught me how even strong bodies make hinges.

Gull-Marr grunts.

That sound is worth more than blood.

I slide out from under him and leave skin from my shoulder on the stone.

The tribe has gone silent again.

Voss-Marr is leaning forward now despite himself. He wants his father to crush me. He also wants to know how I keep finding doors in walls.

Gull-Marr rises slower.

So do I.

My breath tears. My shoulder screams. The world has too many edges. The Red stays close, offended that I will not use it properly. It offers me an easy road every time Gull-Marr's throat turns bare.

Easy roads often lead to cages.

I spit ash and blood onto the stone.

Gull-Marr sees the blood.

He smiles.

Not kind. Not cruel. A king's smile, old and tired under the teeth.

Then he comes for the end.

For one breath his neck is open.

The red wanting rises.

Take it.

Make him small.

Put your hand where the slavers put theirs.

I see it clearly. My fingers in the gray hide at his neck. My knee on his back. The king held the way I was held, made into property by the fear he gave others.

I step away.

I take the lip instead.

Before Gull-Marr has fully risen, I am on the king's stone above him. His place. The place from which he weighed every body on the shelf and let it stand or not.

Now I stand there.

Gull-Marr rises below me.

He could come up.

He could take me. Everyone knows this. I know it. He knows it.

But the tribe has turned.

Not because I am stronger. Because I put him down and did not take his neck. Because the wrong boy on the lip has brought meat, fire, paths through rot, the Hesh's head, and now a new answer:

The strong may rule.

But the tribe has seen where Gull-Marr's weight goes, where pride blinds him, where mercy leaves him standing.

Gull-Marr looks at the tribe.

At Torn-Ear, who bows his head to me.

At Kala, still as stone below the dead shelf.

At the seer, whose old eyes are fixed on the mark at my chest.

At Voss-Marr, rigid with a wound no hand has touched.

Then the old king lets it stand.

He turns from the lip and walks down into the green alone.

No one follows.

The jungle takes his shape by degrees: gray hide, scarred back, old shoulders, then nothing.

I watch until he is gone.

Mercy feels nothing like softness.

It feels like holding a blade by the edge and refusing to drop it.

Voss-Marr does not bow.

He does not need to. His hatred is clearer than any bow would be. I spared his father and took his future in the same breath. Worse, I spared him too. I stepped over the heir, defeated the king, and left the line alive beneath me.

That kind of mercy makes enemies who remember.

He stands with one hand pressed to the place where the boar opened him. His blood has dried black there. Mud from the Hesh hunt still clings in the fur at his thigh, though he has washed twice. He could scrape it out. He has not. Maybe he wants the tribe to remember he was there. Maybe he wants me to remember he fell.

The tribe comes to the lip.

Torn-Ear first. He stands beside me and faces the green. Others follow. Hunters. Mothers. Half-grown males with uncertain eyes. The discarded ones come too: the limping, scarred, crooked, too-small bodies the old order pushed toward the edges. Marrow climbs last, using one hand and his teeth where the rock is steep, and plants himself at my side as though he has always stood there.

Then come the disputes.

Not later. Not after song or rest. At once.

A young hunter lays a strip of Hesh hide at the foot of the lip and pounds his chest for the right to wear it. Torn-Ear answers with an old sound that gives it to the hunt, not the hunter. Two mothers bare teeth over a sleeping hollow near the fire, each with a small one coughing in the wet heat. A scarred male drops old bone counters on the stone and waits for me to read a debt I have never seen made.

The crown is not a clean height above the tribe.

I solve the first badly. I give the hide to Torn-Ear because he is oldest in the hunt, and the young hunter's face goes dead-flat with insult before I understand the Hesh head was mine to give and his to ask for in front of everyone. Kala rumbles low behind me, not anger, warning. Marrow taps twice on the stone and points with his chin at the young hunter's bleeding palms.

Right.

Not hide. Blood.

I cut a narrow strip from the hide and bind it around the hunter's wrist myself. Then I set the rest by the fire for all who held the line, even those who held only by waiting. The young hunter lifts his wrist. Torn-Ear grunts approval.

One knot loosens.

I breathe again.

The twisted Skell comes after Marrow.

No one calls it.

No one stops it either, because the whole tribe is trapped inside a moment larger than its old laws. It climbs badly, claws scraping stone, bad leg dragging. Halfway up, a young Vorth hisses and lifts a hand to knock it down.

Marrow bites him.

Not hard enough to wound. Hard enough to make a point.

The young one jerks back, outraged. Marrow plants himself between him and the Skell with his curled hand high and his teeth bared.

The tribe waits to see what I will do.

So does Voss-Marr.

So does the Skell.

This is the work, then. Not the lip. Not the sound they give me. A hungry Skell, a stolen shell, Marrow's teeth bared, old lines crossing under my feet.

I look at the Skell.

It lowers its narrow head and sets the shell it once gave me on the stone between us.

The same shell I placed near the small dead one. It must have stolen it back from the shelf, or taken another like it from the shallows. Either way, it offers what it has.

The Vorth mutter.

The dead shelf is sacred. The shell is not meat. The Skell is not tribe. Every old line crosses here.

I pick up the shell.

Then I set it on the king's lip beside my bare foot.

Allowed.

The twisted Skell crawls the last length and crouches below Marrow, shaking with fear and triumph.

The smell of it brings the old order up in every Vorth throat. Carrion. Mud. Fear-sweat. The edge of dead meat. It knows how it smells to them. I see that in the way it keeps its belly low and its eyes down. The Skell have survived by making themselves smaller than hunger.

When it reaches my foot, Voss-Marr's mouth curls.

Not disgust only.

Judgment.

His body says: these over blood, leavings over heirs.

He is wrong.

The judgment still has teeth.

Later I will need a name for him. Not now. A name is not a stone thrown to make a moment pretty. It must fit the body it lands on.

But I know it already.

Grist.

What the strong grind off and leave.

What still feeds a fire if someone bothers to gather it.

Kala comes after them.

She folds down beside me and lays her arm around my back. Not to hold me up. Not to keep me in place. Simply because she can, and I allow it.

She gives the stilling sound.

Be held.

Nothing comes.

This time it is true for the length of the sound.

The seer touches the eye-and-thorn, then lays his palm over my heart. He gives the meaning-sound with a question hidden inside it.

He still does not know what I am.

Neither do I.

But I know what I am to the Vorth now.

I am the one on the lip.

The one who brought down the Hesh.

The one who read the king and let him live.

The one the weak gather to first because I remember the taste of being thrown away.

King is not the word the Vorth use. Their sound is older, rougher, full of tooth and shelter both. I take it in and keep my human name hidden behind my teeth.

The lip is warm under my bare feet. Kala watches as if I might fall. Marrow leans against my leg and pretends he is not leaning.

Beyond the fire, another hand will come. I know that much.

I stand on the lip while the sun rises over the green, with Kala's warmth at my back, Marrow at my side, the tribe below, and my name still hidden where no one can take it.

For the first time, I have a place no hand is holding for me.

Every place I have had before was taken by a hand. That is how I know to watch for one.
