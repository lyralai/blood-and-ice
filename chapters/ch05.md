# Chapter 5: The Rot and the Den

Rain kills the fire on the fourth night.

It comes all at once, not in drops but in a wall. The jungle disappears behind it. Leaves flatten. The channel turns white where the water strikes it. I throw myself over the coals, arms curved around the little red heart I bled for, but my body is not a roof.

The fire hisses once.

Then it is gone.

For a while I crouch over the blackened pit with rain running down my face and my back and into the raw places on my hands. I hate the sky. I hate the dead coals. I hate myself for thinking a flame could belong to me just because I made it.

The jungle gives no time for grief.

Water comes into the crack under the stone before midnight. First a dark gleam on the floor. Then a stream. Then brown water full of leaves and pale worms sliding over my feet. The crack that saved me from the first nights becomes a drain for the whole hill. The smell rises with it, sour and alive.

I back out into the rain with the last coal dead behind me.

I need a place that does not drown.

Not a hole the island gave me.

A place I make.

The next morning the world steams. White breath lifts from the leaves. Every low place stinks of rot. I stand above the drowned crack and read the hill the way I have learned to read mud, tide, bird, and branch.

Water tells the truth after rain.

It pools where a man should not sleep. It runs where stone is sound. It drips from the branches that will stay wet for days and beads on the leaves that shed it quickly. The old crack was low, warm, hidden, and wrong. I chose it because I was afraid of open ground.

Fear makes bad houses.

So I climb.

The slope above the channel is broken with pale stone. It cuts through the green in shelves and hard ribs. My feet slip. My thigh aches where the old wound pulls. Twice I stop and rest with my hand against rock warm from yesterday's sun.

Halfway up, I find a tree split by lightning long ago. The inside is black and dry under a curled lip of bark. Char without fire. I scrape it out with my fingers and laugh once, short and mean, because the sky killed a tree and left me tinder.

I thank the dead tree.

Then I feel foolish and thank it again, quieter.

Near the top I find the place.

A slab has fallen from the ridge and lodged against another stone, making a dry pocket beneath. Not large. Barely high enough to crawl into. But the ground under it is powder-dry, and when I press my palm down, no damp rises between my fingers.

Dry.

The word feels almost as rich as meat.

I crawl inside and lie on my back. Stone leans over me. Stone holds behind me. Rain would have to work to reach this hollow, and work is more than rain likes when easier ground lies below.

It is not safe.

Nothing here is safe.

But it can be kept.

Fire is not made once. Shelter is not made once. Anything worth having must be defended against the rot every day.

I spend the next three days learning how to build badly, then less badly.

Stone first. Big pieces at the base. Smaller pieces wedged between. A stone set round side down will roll and punish the hand that trusted it. A flat stone wants a flat stone under it. Heavy stones do not care if a boy is tired.

One catches my fingers and crushes them hard enough to whiten the world.

I curl around the hand, breath locked behind my teeth. Blood gathers under the nails and runs down the split skin at the knuckle. By any law I know, the hand should swell and stiffen. By the green's law it should sour.

It does neither.

By evening the bleeding has slowed. By the next dawn the torn skin has drawn tight and pale, too fast, too clean. I stare at it until anger rises in my throat.

"Why?"

The word is smaller than the rain.

No answer comes. No answer ever comes. Whatever lives wrong in my blood keeps me alive and tells me nothing.

So I put the hand back on the stones.

After stone comes vine. The fresh vines rot almost as soon as I cut them, so I learn to strip them and lay them across sun-hot rock until they toughen. I weave them through the wall and fill the gaps with dry leaves, cane, bark, and mud. I leave a crawl mouth low enough that I can block it from inside with a stone.

By the third night I have a den.

Not a crack.

Not a gift.

Mine.

I mark the stone beside the crawl mouth with three scratches. Not letters. Not a name. Only proof that a hand chose this place and came back to it. The scratches look small against the rock. They are small. I run my thumb over them anyway until grit packs under my nail.

The slavers marked crates.

I mark home.

I make fire again inside it. Slower this time. Careful. I build a nest of dry fiber and coax the coal up until flame licks the little sticks. Smoke curls along the stone and escapes through a gap I left high in the wall. Heat gathers under the slab instead of vanishing into the trees.

For the first time since the sea spat me out, warmth stays where I put it.

Then the insects come.

They rise from the swamp at dusk in black clouds. The biting flies. The ants that find blood through mud. Soft white worms that drop from leaves and search blindly for skin. They climb the wall. They cover the outer leaves. They find the fish bones near the fire and turn them black.

But they do not land on me.

The cloud bends around my arm.

I hold my hand out, slowly. The flies part before it as if my skin gives off smoke they cannot bear. Ants run over the stone beside my hip and circle back. A fat pale worm drops from the roof, writhes near my knee, and turns away.

The den did not do this.

I did not do this.

Something in me makes the island refuse me even while it tries to kill me.

I sit very still in the firelight and watch a clean space hold around my body. A hand's width. No more. Enough.

I should be glad. Some low, tired part of me is. The part that has been bitten and opened and taken from its whole life is glad for one night of whole skin.

The rest of me is afraid.

Not of the flies.

Of the reason.

The swarm teaches me another law.

It will not touch fresh blood from my hand, but it loves old blood on bone. When I set a crab shell too close to the door, the black cloud gathers over it until the shell seems furred. When I move the shell with a stick, the cloud follows. When a drop from my knuckle falls onto the same shell, the cloud breaks apart as if wind has passed through it.

Not all blood, then.

Mine is different.

The den is tested before I trust it.

The test comes two nights later, in rain so steady it seems less like weather than a hand pouring water from the dark.

I wake because the clean space around me breaks.

Not the insects. They are gone from the crawl mouth, gone from the wall, gone from the meat strips hung above the smoke. The quiet they leave is wrong. A hundred little hungers have agreed to be elsewhere.

Then the stone at the entrance moves.

Only a finger's width.

But I set that stone myself. I chose it because it is flat and heavy and wedges under a lip of rock. It should not move unless a hand moves it from outside.

I take the spear.

The fire is low, red under ash. Good for warmth. Bad for seeing. I hook dry bark into the coal and breathe until flame lifts. Light pushes toward the crawl mouth.

Something is pressed there.

Not Vorth. Not swine. Too low. Too long.

I see a pale muzzle through the narrow gap where the stone has shifted. Wet black nose. Whiskers stiff as fish bones. Behind them, one yellow eye opens and looks in at me.

The mouth that follows has too many teeth for its face.

The creature pushes again.

The door-stone grinds inward.

I throw myself against it from inside, shoulder first. Pain bursts through my bruised ribs. The stone stops. The pale muzzle withdraws, then rams forward. The whole den shudders. Dry leaves sift down onto the fire.

I am not strong enough to hold long.

That truth arrives cleanly. No shame in it. Shame can come later if I survive.

The creature digs at the crawl mouth. Claws scrape stone. Mud spatters through the gap. I thrust with the spear and hit fur, then hide. The point skids along bone and comes back wet. The creature screams into the den, a thin furious sound that makes my teeth ache.

The scream stirs the warmth under my ribs.

Not fire.

Red.

It lifts its head in me. It likes the narrow doorway. It likes a fight with no room to run. Its plan is simple: open the way, meet teeth with teeth, turn fear around.

Then I see a boy's teeth in the hold, red after a guard's boot, and the way all of us went smaller because smaller survived.

I am not opening the door.

I shove the spear through the gap again, not to kill. To make room. Refusing the Red costs time. Smoke thickens. My chest locks. When the creature snaps at the shaft, I grab a smoking brand and jam it into the dry leaves beside the crawl mouth.

Smoke blooms.

At first it blinds me too. My eyes pour water. I cough until my bruised ribs feel split. But the smoke finds the gap and climbs into the pale muzzle outside.

The creature coughs.

I feed the leaves.

More smoke.

The yellow eye vanishes. The claws scrape backward. A long body crashes through wet brush outside, then stops. I hear it breathing beyond the wall. Angry. Unhurt enough to remember me.

I sit with my shoulder against the door-stone until dawn.

When light comes, I find blood on the spear point and pale fur caught in a crack. The track outside is long, low, and splayed, with claw marks deep enough to hold rainwater. The door-stone has a groove cut across it where teeth scraped the edge.

The den held.

I made it badly, then less badly, and fear taught my hands to check work twice.

I sleep after sunrise with the spear across my chest and wake at midday to find a small gray lizard near my foot.

It has slipped through a gap in the wall and frozen on the warm stone as if it helped with the watch. It is missing half its tail. One eye is clouded. It studies me as if I am the intruder, which is fair.

"Pay rent," I whisper.

It blinks.

I flick it a beetle. The lizard takes the beetle in a flash and stays.

So the den has two mouths breathing in it for a while. Mine, and one small thief's. When I wake before dawn, it is gone, leaving a curl of shed skin beside the fire pit like a pale little rope.

I keep the skin. The reason stays out of reach.

The thought is too large again, and this time I hold it longer. I sit with it while the insects circle the clean space around my skin.

Different can mean valuable.

Different can mean cursed.

Different can mean both, if the wrong hands learn it.

I eat fish with shaking hands. The meat is hot and smoky and good. I chew on the side where my teeth remain, careful of the gap where three were knocked loose long ago.

Behind that gap, hidden in the dark under my tongue, I keep my name.

My name.

I keep it silent. A name spoken can be heard. A name heard can be taken. The slavers had their own sounds for me, ugly commands shaped like a hand closing on the neck. Those were not my name. They were hooks. They sank them in and pulled.

My name is my mother's sound.

I hold it against the warmth in my mouth while the fire lives and the insects crawl over everything but me.

The den keeps out rain. The fire keeps back night. Fresh blood keeps insects off until it dries; old blood draws them. Healing leaves me hungry enough to chew bark.

Then, far below the ridge, a call moves through the dark.

Low. Shaped. Too heavy for a bird. Too deliberate for wind.

I go still with a strip of fish in my hand.

The call comes again, closer this time.

Not a beast crying at the moon.

A voice.
