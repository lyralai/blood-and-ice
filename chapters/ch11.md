# Chapter 11: The Weaker, the Smarter

Among the Vorth, I am weak.

A half-grown female moves me from a warm stone one morning because she wants it. She takes my wrist and lifts me aside with no anger, no effort, no thought that I might resist. By the time my feet find the ground again, she has already forgotten me.

On the shore, I was the longest tooth.

On the high stones, I am something that can be moved.

Good.

A hard fact can be used.

The Vorth hunt with weight. They pour down both sides of a channel, drive swine and deer toward the arms waiting below, and close around the prey like a fist. Their strength is terrible. A blow from one adult can break a neck clean. Their hands can tear roots from mud that would hold me all day.

But strength has edges.

The Vorth do not like deep rot. Their feet sink. Their weight is too much for the skin of moss and leaf that floats over black water. They do not climb the thin high branches where small meat nests. They cannot catch the burrow-runners on the channel flats, quick gray animals that live between hole and hole and vanish before a heavy hand can fall.

I can go where weight fails.

So can Marrow.

I take him down at low tide before the others wake. He follows close, rocking and quick, his curled hand tucked against his chest. At the channel flat I show him the pale line in the mud where a burrow-runner travels. I lay a loop of plaited fiber across it. Bend a green branch. Set the trigger light.

Marrow wants to touch everything.

He touches the knot before it is ready and snaps the branch into my cheek.

For a breath we stare at each other. My eye waters. A red line opens under the bone.

Then Marrow makes a horrified little sound, points at the branch as if it acted alone, and glares at it with such injured dignity that I cannot hold my anger. I cuff him lightly on the shoulder. He cuffs the branch. The branch does not learn.

Marrow watches once.

The second loop he sets himself.

His crooked fingers are better than mine.

We hide in the reeds. I send him wide to scratch near the burrow mouth. The runner bolts from its hole, low and fast, exactly where its own feet have worn the mud. It hits the loop. The branch snaps up. The small animal kicks twice and hangs still.

For a moment Marrow and I only stare.

Then he makes a cracked, delighted sound.

I answer with one of my own.

Laughter, I think later. The word takes time to come. At first it is only a bright break in the chest, useless and good.

We bring the runner up to the high stones.

The Vorth gather around it, puzzled. They look for the blow. The broken neck. The place where strength entered meat. There is only the loop.

Kala sees first.

She crouches and follows the fiber with one finger, from the dead animal's neck to the knot to the spring of the branch. Her eyes lift to mine, then to Marrow's curled hand.

She makes the question sound.

What is this?

I show her.

At the next low tide she comes with us, great and silent, stepping in my prints where the ground will hold her. I set the run. Marrow lays the loop. I guide Kala's huge hand to the bent branch so she can feel the stored force in it.

Then we wait.

The runner comes. The loop takes it.

Kala's laugh is a deep bark that startles birds from the trees.

After that, the tribe begins to look at me differently.

Not kindly. Not all of them. But with use.

When prey goes into soft ground, heads turn toward me. When small meat vanishes into holes, Marrow is sent for. When rain threatens the fire, I bring dry heartwood from the fallen giant, fat-soaked fiber, and the trick of feeding flame slowly from its own center.

The Skell come after the first three burrow-runners.

I smell them before I see them: sour hide, old blood, wet bone cracked for marrow and left too long in heat. They wait where the high stone spills into scree, low shapes among low rocks, ribby and gray, their eyes bright as wet seeds.

The Vorth hate them.

No. Hate is too simple.

The Vorth do not look at them until they must, and then they look as the strong look at what feeds from what they leave behind. Not enemy. Not kin. Necessary ugliness. A reminder that meat can become scrap and scrap can still move.

A half-grown Vorth throws a chewed haunch bone down the slope. The Skell scatter, then flood back over it in a low snarling knot.

Marrow watches them too closely.

I know why.

Leavings know leavings.

One Skell does not join the knot. It is smaller than the others, one hind leg twisted, hide scored by old bite marks. It waits until the fight is done, then creeps toward a splinter of bone no bigger than my finger.

A larger Skell sees and snaps.

The small one flinches but does not run. It lowers itself flat and gives up the bone before it has touched it.

Something in me goes hard and quiet.

I take a strip of runner meat from our pile and toss it past the larger Skell, not to the center of the scramble, but to the shadow where the twisted one waits.

The meat lands at its feet.

Every Skell freezes.

So do the Vorth above me.

Food falls as waste, not as gift. Waste has no chooser. Gift does.

The twisted Skell looks up at me.

Its face is not Vorth. Too narrow. Too hungry. Too much tooth for the thin head. But thought lives there. Old fear too. It waits for the trick.

I make the stilling sound Kala gives me.

Be still.

The Skell does not understand the sound.

It understands my hand staying open.

It takes the meat and vanishes between stones.

The silence after is worse than snarling.

An old female Vorth makes a disgust sound low in her throat. A young male laughs. The laugh dies when Kala looks at him. Gull-Marr does not move on the high lip, but I feel his attention like sun on the back of my neck.

I have not fed a hungry mouth.

I have chosen a place in the order and moved meat across it.

Marrow makes a small pleased click in his throat.

Above us, Voss-Marr bares one tooth.

"Waste," Voss-Marr's posture says, even before I know the word.

Maybe.

The Vorth have kept fire longer than any living mouth remembers.

They have not always understood how to remake it.

That matters in the wet season.

One night their kept fire shrinks to a dull red under rain and bad wood. The adults crowd close, uneasy. A kept fire can die. If it dies in the long wet, the tribe becomes sicker, smaller, pushed closer to the rot.

I go down to my old stores and return with dry pith wrapped in hide, dead heartwood, and rendered fat. I build the flame as I built my first one: small, patient, never greedy. Fiber. Splinter. Stick. Heartwood. Fat only when the coal can bear it.

The red stands up.

Light fills the stones.

The tribe goes quiet.

Even Gull-Marr looks down from his bare lip.

Voss-Marr comes close enough to see the work of it. Rain beads on his shoulders. His face is hard, but his eyes follow the order: dry heart, patient fiber, fat only after coal. He understands quickly. Too quickly for comfort.

He reaches toward the pile.

Marrow makes a warning click, small but sharp.

Voss-Marr looks at him. Looks at me. Then he takes one wet stick from the edge and sets it where the heat will dry it without choking the flame.

Correctly.

I hate that.

I am still weak. A young Vorth could lift me aside tomorrow. But now they know weakness is not emptiness. It has its own roads, and some of those roads lead to meat, fire, and ground the strong cannot cross.

The seer comes to me after the fire is high.

She touches the eye-and-thorn at my chest, then lays one pale finger against the side of my head. Thinking. That is what she means. Or maybe old blood. Or maybe a story I cannot read.

The mark.

The mind.

The blood that does not die of the green.

She sets them near each other with her touch and bows.

I hate how much I want her meaning.

I have sounds now, more every day, but not enough for the questions that matter. Why does the island spare me? Why does this mark matter? Why does the oldest Vorth look at me as if I am less a boy than a door left open?

Marrow sleeps beside me that night with his curled hand open in the firelight.

I watch the flame I saved burn clean through the wet.

The strong take what they can reach.

The weak learn how to make the world reach farther.

Near dawn, when the fire has settled into red eyes under ash, something clicks once from the scree.

I open my eyes.

The twisted Skell crouches at the edge of the light. It has dragged something with it: a pale shell from the warm shallows, spiral-curled and polished by tide. Not food. Too clean for that. It sets the shell on the stone, backs away, and lowers its narrow head.

Marrow wakes and sees.

For once, he does not turn himself into a joke.

I take the shell after the Skell is gone. It fits in my palm like a small listening ear.

The Vorth give meat for strength, meat for place, meat for hunger.

The Skell has given me a gift no hunger can explain.

By morning there are seven Skell below the stones.

Not close. They know better than close. They crouch among the scree and pretend to be shadows with teeth. The twisted one is there too, smaller than the rest, keeping to the left where a split boulder gives it room to flee. It watches me. The others watch the meat piles.

The Vorth wake into tension.

No one throws a bone.

A child runs too near the lower stones and two mothers snatch it back at once. The missing-thumb male takes his place by the crack earlier than usual, shoulders high. Torn-Ear comes down with a haunch in his hand, sees the waiting Skell, and lets the haunch hang at his side until blood drips on his knuckles.

Voss-Marr sees me seeing it.

He comes to the edge of the fire shelf and points down. His words are still mostly closed to me, but enough has opened.

Waste brings waste.

He points to the children. Then to the meat. Then to the Skell.

His meaning is clean enough to cut.

You fed one. Now all come. When all come, children cannot play. Meat cannot dry. The dead cannot rest without watchers. Your soft mercy has made work for everyone.

The old heat rises in me because I want him wrong.

The accusation has teeth.

The twisted Skell did not ask the others to come. Hunger called them. My hand opened the road. But the road is real now, and every road leads both ways.

Marrow comes to my side. He has the pale shell cupped in his crooked hand. He offers it to me as if it can answer.

It cannot.

A gift does not erase a danger.

I go down the first slope with no meat in my hands.

Kala makes a warning sound. Voss-Marr makes no sound at all. That silence follows me harder than a snarl.

The Skell shift as I come. Their bodies are low, their mouths wet. One darts forward and back, testing whether my softness has meat hidden inside it. I bare my teeth and strike the stone with the butt of my knife. Once. Twice. Three times.

The sharp sound cracks through the morning.

The Skell freeze.

I take one old haunch bone from yesterday's pile, stripped clean enough that no Vorth will miss it, and set it on a flat rock halfway between their hunger and our fire. Then I back away.

Not thrown.

Set.

The difference matters. I make it matter with every motion. This stone. This bone. This distance. No closer.

The twisted one understands first.

It creeps forward with its belly low and noses the bone. A larger Skell lunges. I slap the rock with the knife again and snarl hard enough that my throat burns. Marrow echoes me from above, a ridiculous fierce noise that turns heads even in danger.

The larger Skell stops.

The twisted one takes the bone and retreats.

I point to the old waste hollow below the scree. Then the flat rock. Then the Skell. I strike the stone once more.

Even with no shared tongue, law can be built from hunger and fear.

Torn-Ear grunts.

Not approval.

Interest.

That evening, the Vorth throw the bones to the flat rock instead of down the slope. The Skell fight there, away from the fire shelf. The children creep back toward their crack. The missing-thumb male keeps watching. Voss-Marr stands above the new feeding stone for a long while, then sets two heavy rocks on either side of it, shaping a boundary I had not thought to make.

Better than mine.

I hate that too.

The twisted Skell comes after dark.

It does not bring another shell. It brings a warning.

I wake to Marrow's hand patting my face. Not his usual rude slap. Quick. Careful. His eyes shine in the firelight. He points toward the lower water.

A clicking sound comes from the dark.

The twisted Skell waits near the root path, limping in place. When I rise, it turns and moves downhill. Stops. Looks back.

I follow with Marrow behind me, because trying to leave him would only make more noise.

At the channel, one of my snares is gone.

Not sprung. Gone.

The branch has been cut or chewed through. The loop taken. Mud holds the track of webbed fingers and a dragging tail.

Mure.

The word is not mine yet, but the shape from the ruin returns to my blood. Smooth pale head. Silent mouth. Water that does not ripple.

Marrow hisses.

From the black channel comes a small splash, then nothing.

The Skell crouches beside the ruined snare and makes three clicks. It taps the cut branch with one claw, taps the water, then bares its teeth toward the high stones.

Not gift.

Warning.

The Mure have noticed our traps.

The cut branch weighs almost nothing. It changes the whole shore. If the Mure learn our loops, low tide is no longer ours. If the Skell warn us, then the discarded can guard what strength cannot see.

At dawn, I carry the branch up myself. Voss-Marr is the first to understand.

He takes it from me. Smells the wet end. Looks toward the channel.

For a moment, we stand on the same side of a fear.

Then he hands it back and makes the useful sound again.

This time it is not only for me.

His eyes flick to the scree where the Skell hide from morning.

Useful has widened.

Trouble does not need a shout. Sometimes it starts with one mercy that works.
