# Chapter 17: The Challenge

Gull-Marr waits.

After the Hesh, the tribe turns toward me too brightly for him to move at once. The dead head lies by the fire. Small ones creep close to touch the teeth. Hunters speak my sounds more often. Torn-Ear will not cross rot without looking for my mark stones.

The king lets the brightness fade.

I keep it bright.

Each day I make myself useful where everyone can see. I mark unsafe ground with pale stones. I reset the traps with Marrow. I show the half-grown hunters how to read a mud skin before it opens. I feed the fire through rain. I catch a falling child from a branch before his mother sees the limb crack.

None of it is accident.

Power is not only strength. Power is where eyes go when danger starts.

More eyes come to me now.

They come with needs, not songs.

A mother brings a young one with a swollen foot and waits to see if I know which thorn did it. A scarred male drops two fish at the fire and watches whose hand I send them to. The old she with the torn lip grunts over a sleeping place taken by a stronger cousin, and the whole shelf looks up to see whether my new law has teeth when no monster is bleeding in front of it.

Meat first to the hurt foot. Fire space back to the torn lip. The strong cousin sent to the outer ledge with Torn-Ear watching. None of it wins a roar. It only keeps the night from bending wrong.

Gull-Marr sees all of it.

So does Voss-Marr.

Voss-Marr feels it.

He stands at the edge of every lesson like a storm deciding where to break. The line of succession was supposed to run through him cleanly: Gull-Marr, then Voss-Marr, then whatever sons his strength made. I have stepped onto that line without being bred for it, without even understanding all its words.

He cannot forgive that.

He is not alone.

Hatred by itself is easy to count. One body. One mouth. One set of hands. This comes quieter. A glance held too long. A grunt answered from the wrong side of the fire. Meat passed around my place instead of through it. Young hunters standing nearer Voss-Marr than they used to, not touching him, not making a promise anyone can point at, but leaving their weight in his shadow.

There are five I begin to count.

Red-Shoulder, who has not forgiven the narrow Hesh wrist-binding. Bent-Jaw, old enough to have followed Gull-Marr through more seasons than I have survived. Two scar-knuckled brothers who laugh after Voss-Marr laughs. A lean one-eyed female who watches Kala as if measuring whether a mother's claim can be broken.

They are not fools.

When I give extra meat to coughing young, Red-Shoulder bares his teeth at empty-handed hunters. When I forbid a lower path after rain, Bent-Jaw grunts that river-pig will fatten without us. When Grist creeps near the fire, the blind-eyed female lifts her lip and points toward the dead shelf.

Skell eat the dead if hunger drives them hard enough.

I know that.

So do the Vorth mothers sleeping under bones they keep clean with their own hands.

Red-Shoulder's hungry hunters are real. Bent-Jaw's river-pig are real. The dead shelf is real.

So is the way they look at Marrow when they say these things.

Voss-Marr does not speak first. He lets them carry the argument out where everyone can smell it. Then he stands behind them, wounded ribs wrapped, mouth shut. He is good at silence. Better than I want him to be. His quiet makes the old order look injured. It reminds the tribe that before I came, every body knew where to stand.

I have brought meat and paths and Hesh teeth.

I have also brought confusion.

One rain-sour afternoon, the confusion breaks open.

A child from the lower mothers carries a strip of roasted liver to Grist where he crouches under the lip. He means kindness. Or mischief. With children those roads cross often. Red-Shoulder catches the child by the wrist and yanks him back hard enough to make him cry.

Kala rises.

The blind-eyed female rises too.

All at once the fire has two sides.

Grist freezes under the stone, liver fallen in the ash at his clawed feet. Marrow starts toward him, and I catch his shoulder before he can put his small body where every large anger wants to land.

Red-Shoulder points at Grist and gives the old carrion-sound.

Not tribe.

Not fire.

Voss-Marr stands behind him, not smiling, not stopping him.

The child sobs against his mother's leg. The mother looks at me, then at Kala, then at Gull-Marr on the lip.

Gull-Marr says nothing.

He wants me to spend myself first.

So I do.

I take the liver from the ash, wipe it clean on a leaf, and break it in half. One half I give to the child. One half I set on the stone between Grist and Red-Shoulder.

Then I point to the outer ring of the fire.

There.

Not inside. Not among the sleeping young. Not under the dead shelf. But not driven into the rain either.

Grist stares at the meat. Red-Shoulder stares at me.

It is not enough for anyone.

No one cheers. That is how I know it may hold.

Grist takes the meat and backs to the outer ring. Red-Shoulder spits into the ash but does not strike. Kala lowers herself slowly. The blind-eyed female stays standing one breath longer than Kala, long enough to make sure the tribe sees she chooses to lower herself, not obey.

Voss-Marr's gaze meets mine across the fire.

He has won part of that moment. So have I. That means neither of us has won enough.

I try, once, to give part of it back.

It happens after a hunt in the east slope, where the fruit trees crowd so thick the air smells drunk. Voss-Marr corners a young boar against a root wall and holds it there without killing it, waiting for the half-grown hunters to form correctly around him. His command is good. His hands are good. No one moves until he moves.

I see it, and because I see it, I step back.

Let the eyes go to him.

Let the meat be his.

Let the tribe remember the future it expected.

Marrow looks up at me, confused.

The boar breaks left.

A half-grown hunter slips. Voss-Marr catches the error before anyone else. He throws himself across the path, takes the boar's shoulder in his ribs, and drives it back into the waiting arms. Clean. Brave. Costly.

The hunters roar for him.

I roar too.

His eyes find mine through the bodies.

For one breath I think he understands.

Then Torn-Ear claps my shoulder and makes the sound for my mark stones, telling the young ones that the kill worked because the east slope path had been cleared earlier by me.

The eyes split.

Some to Voss-Marr.

Some to me.

There it is again. The theft I cannot stop committing by being useful.

Voss-Marr's ribs bleed where the tusk opened him. He presses one hand to the wound and looks at me as if I put the tusk there myself.

He had done it right. That is the worst of it. No foolishness. No empty showing. He took the hit a leader takes, and the meat would have been his moment if my usefulness had not already laid itself under the ground like a root.

I want to give him the whole kill.

Wanting does not move the eyes back.

The seer makes it worse.

Before dawn, the old one comes through the sleeping tribe and touches the eye-and-thorn at my chest. He gives the meaning-sound. Then he points to Gull-Marr on the lip and gives it again.

This one.

That one.

One telling.

His finger passes over Voss-Marr without stopping.

The heir is awake enough to see it.

So is the king.

So are the five who have made a weather around him.

Red-Shoulder's mouth opens, then closes when Bent-Jaw's hand touches his arm. The blind-eyed female makes a soft click in her throat. The two scarred brothers look at Voss-Marr first, not Gull-Marr.

The insult is not that I may stand there. The insult is that Voss-Marr must watch me be allowed.

The seer has not only skipped Voss-Marr.

He has made everyone watch everyone else notice.

Voss-Marr's face becomes so still it is almost beautiful. All youth and anger pressed under the mask of a future king who has just been told the future walked around him.

For a breath I pity him.

Then I remember pity is also a height one body stands on above another, and he would rather be struck than pitied by me.

Gull-Marr comes down.

The whole shelf tightens. Small ones vanish into adult arms. Marrow slips off my shoulder. Kala rises behind me with a sound in her chest I have never heard, low and dangerous.

She would stand between us.

I put my hand on her arm.

For once, I give her the stilling sound.

Be still.

It hurts her. I feel that. She has kept me through fever, fear, and night. Every law in her body tells her to put herself between danger and the life she chose.

But if she stands for me, my standing is only her shadow.

I step out from under her arm.

Onto the open stone.

Gull-Marr towers over me. Old strength, scarred strength, the whole law of the high stones in one body. He could kill me quickly. He will not. Quick killing would read as fear. He needs the tribe to see strength put cleverness back in its place.

He gives the challenge-sound.

Low. Full. Bare.

Here.

This stone.

You and me.

Every Vorth goes still.

I should step back.

The part of me that survived the hold knows how. Yield. Flatten. Make no shape. Let the hand pass over.

But the home behind me is not lent anymore. Kala gave me a place among the dead. Torn-Ear lifted my wrist. The tribe followed my marks. Marrow carries my name for him like a coal.

If I step back, the lip was never mine. Only lent.

I touch my name behind my teeth.

Then I answer.

My sound is rough, smaller than his, scraped raw by a throat that still remembers silence. It carries the meaning anyway.

Here.

This stone.

You and me.

The tribe hears it.

Voss-Marr hears it most of all.

I have answered over his head. The challenge that should have been his to give one day has passed him by and found me instead.

Gull-Marr watches me for a long breath. His flat eyes do not soften, but they stop looking through me.

Then he turns and walks back to his lip.

Not refusal.

Promise.

The fight is named now.

Kala folds down around me after he goes. She does not say nothing comes. She will not lie to a child she has chosen.

Instead she rests her great head against mine, and for a little while we breathe together.

Marrow creeps back to my side.

Across the stones, Voss-Marr stands very still.

I have gained the king's answer.

Across the stones, the heir learns where to put his hate.
