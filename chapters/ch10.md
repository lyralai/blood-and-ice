# Chapter 10: Among Them

I learn the high stones the way I learned the jungle.

First with my feet.

There are places I may stand and places I may not. The dead have the highest clean shelf, where the morning sun strikes first. No living foot goes there except the keeper's and the old seer's. The king has a bare lip of stone above the fire, and the air around it is as clear as if a wall stands there.

I learn that by crossing it once.

A great arm comes down across my chest. Not a blow. A boundary. The Vorth who stops me turns me aside without anger, the way I would move a branch from a path.

The king watches from above.

Gull-Marr. I learn the sound later. At first he is only weight and old silver hide, a scarred muzzle, flat eyes that do not warm. He does not snarl at me. He does not welcome me. He looks at me as he might look at a strange bone washed up by rain, something to be left where it lies until its use or danger proves itself.

Kala chose me.

The seer bowed to the mark at my chest.

The king has chosen nothing.

That matters.

The second country is sound.

The Vorth tongue is rough in the throat and soft at the edges when they want it soft. I steal it one piece at a time. The gentling note Kala gives me is not one sound but many. One means be still. One means back from the edge. One means where are you, answered by here.

They throw those calls across stone and green all day.

Where.

Here.

Where.

Here.

A line of sound tying one life to another so no one falls out of the world unnoticed.

I have lived too long without such lines.

The small ones teach me fastest because they have not yet learned fear properly. They climb over me, poke my scars, tug my hair, bare their teeth in delight when I bare mine back. They put their faces close to mine and give me names for everything.

Fire.

Water.

Stone.

Sky.

Dead.

Kala.

They also teach me games.

At first I cannot understand spending strength for no meat, no safety, no escape. A small one throws a round nut at another small one's head and then runs shrieking when the nut comes back twice as hard. Three of them chase each other around the fire until an adult cuffs the slowest into the dust. Two hang by their hands from a low branch, swinging their feet at each other and laughing when both fall.

I watch with the deep suspicion I give to all waste.

Marrow sees me watching.

He climbs the leaning root above the fire and hangs by one good hand and one crooked one, his bad shoulder riding high, his teeth bared in challenge. The small ones hoot at him. He swings, lets go, catches a vine with his curled hand, and lands badly enough that he rolls three times down the slope and comes up covered in ash.

The whole circle erupts.

Not alarm. Not anger.

Delight.

Marrow bows as if he meant every part of it.

I feel the bright break in my chest again.

He points to me, then to the root.

No.

He points harder.

The small ones turn toward me with their bright hungry faces. Even Kala watches from the dead shelf, pretending not to. I climb the root because refusing would make Marrow unbearable for a whole night.

The first swing goes well.

The second does not.

My hand slips. My heel catches a vine. I turn in the air and hit the dust flat on my back. For one breath the old fear strikes. Down means exposed. Down means boot, hand, hook.

Then Marrow drops beside me and copies the fall badly on purpose, all limbs and teeth and dust. The small ones collapse around us, laughing so hard one of them hiccups.

He does it again when I try to sit.

This time he lands with his rear in the ash.

That is a mistake. The ash is cold on top and hot underneath. He leaps up with a yelp, slaps both hands to his backside, forgets the bad hand does not open cleanly, and turns in a frantic circle trying to see the wound on a part of himself no eye can reach.

The small ones fall into each other.

I laugh until my chest hurts.

Kala makes the stern sound from the dead shelf, but her mouth is tucked in at the corner. I see it. Marrow sees it too and bows toward her with both hands spread, one crooked and one clean, as if he has offered the tribe a great kill.

I lie there under a sky full of green leaves and smoke and Vorth laughter, and nothing bad happens.

No one owns the fall.

No one uses it.

It is only foolishness, and for that reason it feels rarer than meat.

After that they decide I am allowed to lose.

This is a great honor among children, and a terrible burden.

The smallest ones come at me with games as if games are raids. A round-bellied child called Nomi by the others brings smooth stones and sets them in a crooked line. A long-armed boy called Brak knocks half of them aside and howls when she bites him for it. Luta, who has one white splash over her eye, explains the rules in a flood of sound, realizes I understand none of it, and explains again louder.

Marrow watches my face with grave disappointment.

Then he shows me.

The game is simple enough to wound pride. Each player throws a nut along the stone and tries to strike the farthest shell without knocking over the little tower in the middle. If the tower falls, everyone else may throw leaves at you until Kala makes the enough-sound.

I am bad at it.

The children delight in this with pure hearts.

Nomi hits the far shell twice. Brak misses everything and claims victory anyway. Luta throws with her feet for no reason except that she can. Marrow misses on purpose the first time to make them laugh, misses by accident the second time, then becomes so offended by the nut that he stalks it across the stone and slaps it into place with his curled hand.

It works.

The shell clicks.

The tower stands.

The small ones go silent.

Marrow looks at his hand as if it has betrayed him by being useful.

Then he lifts his chin and struts the length of the fire shelf.

Nomi copies him. Brak copies Nomi. Luta copies Brak badly. Soon half the children are strutting in a line, shoulders hunched, chins high, every one of them pretending to have Marrow's crooked hand and rocking walk.

My chest tightens.

Mockery can be a hook. I know the bite of it. But Marrow turns, sees them, and bares his teeth in a grin so wide it becomes answer. He makes his walk larger. Ridiculous. Proud. A king of crookedness. The children shriek and follow.

By evening they have made his weakness into a game they must practice to play well.

It does not erase cruelty.

But it steals one knife from it.

Later, when Luta trips and splits her lip on the stone, Brak laughs too hard. Blood runs down her chin. Her face folds toward tears, not from pain, but from the sudden loneliness that comes when laughter turns sharp.

Marrow reaches her first.

He shoves Brak with his good shoulder, not hard enough to start a fight he cannot finish, hard enough to make the laughing stop. Then he presses his curled hand over his own mouth and makes the same foolish, wounded face Luta has made, exaggerating until even Luta snorts through blood.

He gives her the last nut from the game.

Not because he is sweet.

Because he knows the exact moment when a circle becomes a pit, and he refuses to let her fall into it.

I see him more clearly then.

He is not only the crooked one kept warm by my naming. He is a reader of hurts. Small ones learn fast or they do not last. Marrow has learned where shame enters. Sometimes he opens the door himself for laughter. Sometimes he bars it with his shoulder.

They say her name often. Two falling notes. Ka-la. When they need comfort, they call it. When the dead must be tended, the adults call it. When I hear it enough, the great gray female beside me stops being only warmth, only keeper, only the arm that held fever from my bones.

She becomes Kala.

A name changes what the eye can hold.

There is one small one who comes more than the rest.

He is crooked. One shoulder rides high. One hand curls inward and will not open cleanly. His walk rocks side to side until suddenly it becomes speed, and then he is through a gap before any larger creature thinks to close it.

The tribe has a sound for him. Short. Flat. Thrown downward.

Leavings.

I know the shape of that sound before I know its meaning. I have been named downward. I have worn sounds made to push someone out of the circle.

The crooked small one does not fear me. He rides my chest and names the world into my face, waiting for me to take each sound. When I stay silent, he gives another. Fire. Stone. Hand. Eye. Kala. My name holds still behind my teeth, silent and hidden, while this small wrong-made creature fills the air between us with names.

He is greedy with it. If I learn one sound, he demands two. If I shape a word badly, he shoves his face against mine and shows me his tongue and teeth until I do it better. When I fail too long, he throws himself backward in despair, as if the whole tribe has been cursed with the stupidest soft-skin ever dragged from a channel.

I begin to look forward to disappointing him.

One night, while he is asleep against my ribs and Kala hums the stilling note above us, I answer.

Not with my name.

With her sound.

Low. Broken. Barely shaped.

The noise scrapes my throat raw. I have used my voice for warning, breath, pain. Not for return. Not for gentleness.

Kala stops breathing for one beat.

Then she makes the sound again.

I make it back.

Better this time.

Across the fire, the old seer lifts his milk-pale eyes.

The tribe hears.

I have not given them my name. I have not opened the last hidden door. But a sound has gone out of me toward another living creature and landed without becoming a command, a hook, or a chain.

That is enough to change the night.

Later, the crooked small one wakes and presses his curled hand against my throat, where the sound came from. He gives his downward tribe-noise, then points to himself.

I shake my head.

That is not what I will call him.

I reach into the old human tongue, the one beneath the slavers' commands, and find a word from bone.

"Marrow."

It comes out rough, but I mean it.

Marrow: the rich center the strong crack the bone to reach, then miss because they are careless.

He does not know the word. He knows the throw of it. Upward, not down. Calling, not dismissing.

His curled hand stills.

"Marrow," I say again.

The name settles on him before either of us understands what I have done.

It is the first name I have ever given.

Marrow carries it like stolen fire.

For three days he makes me say it whenever he can catch my eye. He points to himself while eating. While climbing. While hanging upside down from Kala's arm. Marrow. Marrow. Marrow. Each time I give it, he straightens as if the sound has put bone under his skin.

The old downward tribe-noise does not vanish. Others still use it when they are careless or cruel. Voss-Marr uses it once as he passes the fire, low enough that most do not hear.

Marrow hears.

So do I.

The little shape beside me goes still. Not hurt in a simple way. Measuring. Learning where the ground has broken under him.

Voss-Marr does not look back. He does not need to. A creature like him has been obeyed since before he knew the shape of wanting. He speaks downward because the world has always been arranged beneath his feet.

I say, "Marrow."

Loud enough for the nearest heads to turn.

The crooked hand opens against the dirt.

Voss-Marr pauses at the edge of the firelight. Only a moment.

Then he walks on.

Afterward Marrow pretends not to care.

He pretends with his whole crooked self. He climbs too high. He steals a strip of liver from a sleeping male's hand and nearly loses his tail-bone for it. He hangs upside down above me and makes the downward sound in a foolish deep voice until I bare my teeth and say his chosen name for him again.

Not true the way mine is true.

True because I chose it.

That is the first time I understand that a name can be a kindness and an insult in the same breath. It can lift one creature and strike another. It can be a small fire set on a line of dry leaves.

The next day, Marrow uses the fire.

Not with flame. With sound.

He gathers Nomi, Brak, Luta, and two others near the root where we fell. He points to himself and says the downward tribe-noise in a voice so deep and foolish it belongs to a sleeping old male. The children laugh. Then he points to himself and waits.

They say it again.

He drops to the ground as if slain.

More laughter.

Then he sits up, points to me, and demands the chosen name.

"Marrow," I say.

He makes them try it.

Their mouths shape it badly. Marru. Mah-wo. One makes a sound like a frog choking. Marrow accepts none of them. He grips each small face in both hands, one crooked, one clean, and turns their mouths with great seriousness until the sound comes closer.

By dusk, five children can say it.

By the next morning, three adults have heard it.

By the next rain, one of the mothers uses it without noticing when she calls him away from the fire.

Marrow goes so still that I fear he has been struck.

Then he runs straight into the green, climbs the nearest tree, and hangs there upside down until the whole tribe must look at him.

"Marrow," he says to himself, using my human sound in a Vorth mouth.

The word is ugly there.

It is also alive.

Voss-Marr hears the children say it.

His face gives away nothing. His eyes give away enough. A name that spreads without the heir's leave is not a child's game. It is a small law growing roots.

I wanted to lift Marrow.

I did not understand I was also teaching the tribe that names can come from me.
