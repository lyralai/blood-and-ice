# Chapter 14: Standing

The rot-thicket takes Torn-Ear at midday.

He should have waited.

He knows it before the mud takes him. His ears flatten. His head turns a finger-width toward me when the wallowing beast breaks cover. Then hunger moves through him faster than wisdom.

The old male has followed my tracks for weeks, learning which moss lies, which root mat holds, where the black water waits under green skin. But strength has its own pride. A wallowing beast breaks cover within scenting distance, fat and slow, and Torn-Ear goes after it before I can read the ground.

His first step holds.

The second sinks to the knee.

The third opens the thicket beneath him.

He drops to his chest in black soup.

The Vorth gather at the bank and stop. They know that ground. They have watched it eat. Torn-Ear thrashes, and the rot takes him deeper for every effort. Strength is making him drown.

Someone makes the gone-sound too early.

Kala snarls it quiet.

I hear the gone-sound begin in the tribe.

Too soon.

Marrow is beside me. He looks at the thicket, then at me, and his curled hand opens once.

Go, then.

So I go.

Light feet. Wide steps. Never straight toward the drowning Vorth, because he has churned that ground to nothing. I run the long firm line where old roots knot beneath the surface. The mud breathes under me. One slow step would lose me. Speed is part of the path.

Torn-Ear sees me and goes still.

That stillness nearly breaks me.

I know that stillness. I wore it in the hold when the hand closed on my neck.

I cannot pull him out.

If I take his arm, he will drag me under.

So I make the thicket give him back.

A vine as thick as my wrist hangs from a drowned tree. I cut it with the dead men's knife, throw one end across the soup, and slap it against his shoulder.

He stares.

"Take it," I rasp.

The words are not Vorth. Not quite human either. But want has its own tongue, and he understands that.

His hand closes on the vine.

The first pull nearly kills me.

He is too frightened, too strong, too deep in the sucking dark. The vine jerks tight and drags me one step from the root-line. Mud opens cold around my ankle. For one breath the thicket has both of us.

Marrow screams from the bank.

Not warning. Anger.

He flings himself onto the root-line behind me, light enough to cross where the others cannot. His curled hand catches the back of my hide wrap. His good hand hooks a root. He is not strong enough to pull me free.

He is strong enough to keep me from losing the next breath.

I run my end over a broken limb high on the leaning tree, then down. A turn. A lift. The same law as snare and branch, only larger. The tree becomes the arm I lack.

Then I call the strong.

The Vorth cannot cross the thicket, but they can pull from the firm line I show them. One by one they come, setting their feet where mine went. Huge hands close on the vine behind me.

Voss-Marr comes too.

Of course he does. He will not let the tribe see fear in him where a soft-skin has gone first. He takes the vine behind Torn-Ear's eldest daughter and sets his feet exactly in my prints, his face flat with concentration.

I am the eye.

They are the force.

Together we pull.

For a moment the rot holds him.

Then the soup breaks with a wet sucking roar, and Torn-Ear rises inch by inch from the black. Mud pours off his shoulders. His torn ear is flat against his skull. His eyes are wild and alive.

The Vorth drag him onto the bank.

He lies there heaving.

The tribe has gone silent.

Everyone saw.

Marrow stands dripping on the bank, chest pumping, mud hanging from his crooked arm. No one laughs at the arm now. No one uses the downward sound.

Torn-Ear gets to his knees.

He comes to me dripping black water and smelling of the thicket's belly. His great hand closes around my wrist.

Not to move me.

To lift me.

He raises my arm before the tribe and makes a sound deep enough to stir the mud.

This one.

The meaning hits harder than any blow.

Their full language is still beyond me, but I have this. He is naming me before them. Not as Kala's kept stray. Not as omen. Not as the marked soft-skin the seer bows toward.

As the one who did the work.

Marrow presses against my side, trembling with pride he is trying not to show.

Then the air changes.

Voss-Marr comes down from the stones.

Gull-Marr's son. Prime blood. The heir in everything but death. He is young, huge, clean of scars because the world has not yet found courage to mark him. I have watched him from the corner of my eye for weeks. He is strength without apology, and the tribe has been shaped to make room for his future.

Now he looks at my lifted wrist.

At Torn-Ear bowing his head.

At the others looking toward me.

No anger shows. That is worse. Anger spends itself. Calculation saves its teeth.

Voss-Marr speaks to Torn-Ear, not to me.

One short command.

Torn-Ear's ears flatten. My wrist lowers. The pride in his shoulders folds away.

Just like that, Voss-Marr reminds the bank whose stone this is, and whose it will be.

Only then does he look at me.

I have been looked at by predators. By slavers. By men deciding price. His gaze is none of those. He looks as one rival looks at another before either has earned the word.

He sees what happened here.

The missing-thumb male is still testing my root-line. Torn-Ear's daughter has one hand on the vine I threw. The small ones stare at the mud as if it has become a lesson instead of only danger.

Voss-Marr looks from their hands to mine.

Useful would have been safer.

Possible is dangerous.

Voss-Marr turns away without touching me.

High above, Gull-Marr has watched everything and moved not at all.

The tribe does not scatter at once.

Torn-Ear's daughter, a thick-armed hunter with a split lower lip, kneels beside her father and scrapes rot from his chest with both hands. She keeps glancing at me as if thanks has lodged in her throat and will choke her if she lets it out. The missing-thumb male checks the root-line I crossed, pressing each place with his foot, learning the path after danger has passed. Nomi and Luta are being held back by three adults because children think survival is a game until it swallows someone they know.

Marrow, of course, is telling the story already.

He throws himself flat in the mud, kicks, makes Torn-Ear's drowning sound with such disrespect that Torn-Ear's daughter bares her teeth at him. Then he leaps up, becomes me, runs in six directions at once, becomes himself, becomes the vine, becomes Voss-Marr pulling with great dignity, and finally becomes Torn-Ear bursting from the mud by launching his whole body backward into a puddle.

No one laughs at first.

Then Torn-Ear does.

Weakly. Mud still in his teeth. Alive enough to be foolish.

The laugh breaks the bank open, but not fully. It has fear inside it. Everyone can smell how close the dead shelf came.

Voss-Marr lets the laughter spend itself.

Then he speaks.

More words than I can hold, but not so many that the shape is lost. He points to Torn-Ear. To the rot. To me. To Marrow. To the line of footprints across the green skin. Then he slaps his own chest and points to the firm bank where the strong waited until I called them.

His meaning cuts through the wet air.

A rescue that needs a soft-skin path is still a Vorth rescue. A vine pulled by Vorth hands. A life returned to Vorth stone. Do not forget whose strength lifted the old male free.

Some of the young strong ones answer him.

Red-Back, the fruit climber with brown streaks in his hide, thumps his chest. Long-Jaw, who once took Marrow's food and learned my manners-knife, gives a low agreement. Two others follow because agreement is easier than choosing.

I feel anger rise.

Then I force myself to look at the vine still trembling over the branch.

Voss-Marr is not wrong.

My path alone would have left Torn-Ear drowning. Marrow's hand alone would have left me under the mud. Cleverness found the road. Strength dragged death back from it. A tribe that forgets strength because it loves the clever weak will bury many strong bodies.

My hands tighten on the vine. Mud squeezes between my fingers.

He does not only protect the tribe with the truth. He loops it around my throat and pulls.

Gull-Marr descends then.

No hurry. No drama. Gull-Marr comes down the slope, and every mouth closes. He touches Torn-Ear's shoulder, receives his daughter's low grateful sound, then turns to the vine.

He studies the knot.

My knot.

Marrow's grip mark on the hide.

The places where Vorth hands burned bark smooth by pulling.

Gull-Marr takes the vine in both hands and breaks it.

For one terrible breath I think he is rejecting the work.

Then he gives one half to Torn-Ear.

The other half to me.

A murmur moves through the bank.

I hold the wet vine and understand very little except that Voss-Marr has gone utterly still.

The king has split the rescue.

Not mine.

Not theirs.

Ours.

Gull-Marr's eyes remain cool. But Torn-Ear's life is tied now to the hand that crossed and the hands that pulled. If anyone mocks one side, he mocks the king's division.

Voss-Marr lowers his head.

Only as much as he must.

Red-Back lowers his faster. Long-Jaw follows after seeing which way safety has gone.

Marrow looks from my vine to Torn-Ear's and makes a small awed click.

Then he reaches up and tries to chew the end of mine.

I knock his hand away.

He looks wounded, as if kingship has ruined me.

A laugh breaks out of me. Short. Unwise. Torn-Ear hears it and laughs again, which makes his daughter curse him in a voice that needs no translation.

The bank breathes.

But Voss-Marr has learned too.

He has seen his father answer a new kind of usefulness in front of everyone. If he is to beat me, he cannot only bare teeth. He must make the tribe need his answer more than mine, or break the place where mine begins.

Later, when the bank clears, Kala comes to me by the thicket. She settles her vast weight beside mine and gives the stilling sound.

Be held.

Nothing comes.

I answer it because she needs me to answer.

But I am looking up at the stone where Voss-Marr stands with the green behind him and the future around him like a hide no one has yet dared cut.

Kala follows my gaze.

Her hand settles on the vine half across my knees. She turns it once, feeling the broken fibers. Her eyes are wet, though no tear falls. Vorth grief rarely spills. It stays in the chest and changes the sound of breathing.

She makes two sounds.

Son.

Danger.

At first I think she means me.

Then she touches her own chest. Points up at Voss-Marr. Points to Gull-Marr's stone. Her face folds with a grief I have not understood before this moment. Not only for the child she lost. For another child she cannot hold because his place is already armored.

Voss-Marr was small once.

The thought feels foolish because he seems born from stone and pride, but of course he was small. Someone lifted him. Someone kept fever from him. Someone laughed when he fell, or failed to. He was shaped to inherit a tribe that survives by strength, and now a marked stranger teaches the tribe to look elsewhere.

Kala does not ask me to forgive him.

She asks me to see the wound before it becomes teeth.

At dusk, Torn-Ear's daughter comes for me.

She stands at the edge of Kala's warmth with rot still dried on her forearms. Her name is not easy in my mouth yet. It sounds like stone cracking under water. I call her Split-Lip in my head, which is not kind, but names begin where the eye can hold them.

She holds the broken vine half her father received.

Not asking for thanks. Asking for work.

She leads me to the lower stones. Voss-Marr is already there with Red-Back, Long-Jaw, and three young strong ones who pretend not to be listening too hard. Gull-Marr sits above, shadowed by the last light. The missing-thumb watcher has brought Nomi, Luta, and the other children close enough to see, far enough to live.

The thicket lies dark below us.

Torn-Ear sits wrapped in leaves, furious at being alive and unable to stand. His daughter places the vine at my feet, then points to the rot. Her meaning is plain.

Show us.

My first answer is no.

Not aloud. In the hand that tightens on my knife. In the old animal keeping its roads hidden. A path no one else knows is life. A safe root-line through death is power. If Voss-Marr wants to make me small, why should I give him the map that made me useful?

Marrow touches my wrist.

He is watching Torn-Ear's daughter, not Voss-Marr. Her face is hard, but fear leaks from her in small places. Her father nearly drowned in ground he thought he knew. Tomorrow another hunter may chase hunger into the same green skin. A secret path kept for pride can kill as surely as a bad command.

I hate how often belonging asks me to open my hand.

I step to the edge.

I show them the first sign: moss bright as new bile means water under leaf. Moss dull and torn means root close beneath. I show where bubbles rise when the mud breathes. I show the little white reed that grows only where the skin is thin. I place my foot on the first safe knot, then the next, then leap back before the ground can decide I am a meal.

The young strong ones watch with narrowed eyes.

Voss-Marr watches best.

He sees patterns quickly. Too quickly. After the third crossing mark, he points to a place I have not named, a brown patch between two black roots.

Safe?

I almost say yes because the lie would be useful.

The thought is there. Clear. Shameful. Easy.

If he steps there later, he sinks to the waist. If he lives, he learns fear. If he dies, the future changes.

The Red under my ribs lifts its head. It would enjoy the stumble, the waist-deep panic, the future made simpler by one swallowed body.

I bite the inside of my cheek until blood touches my tongue.

Then I shake my head.

I take a stick and push it onto the brown patch. The ground swallows half its length in silence.

Voss-Marr looks at the patch. Then at me.

He knows.

Maybe not the whole of it. Enough.

Split-Lip exhales through her nose. Torn-Ear makes a low sound from his leaf wrappings. The children whisper the reed-sign to each other. Nomi says it wrong. Luta corrects her with great authority and no understanding.

Voss-Marr steps forward at last.

He points to the safe root-line. Points to the strong ones. Then to the children.

His words come clear enough.

No child crosses.

No hunter crosses alone.

No rescue begins without vine.

He takes my lesson and makes a rule from it.

He is good at that.

The tribe hears. The rule settles. It will save lives, and it comes from his mouth. My knowledge. His shape around it. Our rescue already becoming useful to him.

I should be angry.

I am angry.

I am also relieved.

Power that feeds only pride is a hook. A rule can hold a child back from mud.

Voss-Marr looks at me once more before he climbs away. Not grateful. Never that. But the look has changed by a hair.

I am not only rival.

I am resource.

Among Vorth, a resource can be guarded, used, stolen, or broken so no one else can use it.

Something has started.

Not now. Not tomorrow. But it has seen me, and I have seen it.

I earned standing today.

The high stones answered by giving it an enemy.
