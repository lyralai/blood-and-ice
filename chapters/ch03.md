# Chapter 3: Hunger Law

Hunger teaches faster than fear.

Fear makes noise. It throws pictures at the mind. Hands in the dark. Teeth in leaves. The brown water opening under a body and closing again.

Hunger is simpler.

Get up.

Find food.

Live.

By the second morning, it has stripped me down to those three commands.

I wake under the rock shelf with my tongue stuck to my teeth and my belly folded in on itself. Rainwater waits in leaf cups above me. Clear water beads on the stone where the seep runs. Water I can find now. Water I can keep.

Food is harder.

The strip of dried meat from the dead boy is gone. I remember chewing it too fast. I remember keeping it down by force. I remember the snake watching from the branch as if it had already decided which of us owned the body.

The jungle is full of meat.

It moves everywhere: brown channel, bark, leaves. The whole island is eating or being eaten.

I am the only living body here that does not know the rules.

So I watch.

The birds teach first. Small bright birds with hooked beaks and hard black eyes. They drop from the canopy in quick bursts, land on branches, take fruit, tear at pale grubs under bark, and vanish before larger shadows pass overhead.

They avoid the clear fish.

That matters.

The fish drift in the shallows almost without color, easy to see, easy to catch. Too easy. I lie near the water and watch three birds land within a handspan of them. Not one bird strikes.

Easy food no one takes is not food. It is bait with no string showing.

I remember that.

The fish remember me too.

At first I think the current moves them. Then I cut my thumb on a shell and one red drop falls into the pool. The clear bodies turn at once. Not toward it. Away. Every fish slides backward into the shadow under a root, and the water between my hand and them opens clean.

Blood should call hunger.

Mine sends it away.

I hold my thumb over the pool until another drop falls. The fish scatter harder, striking mud and root in their panic.

I draw my hand back slowly.

The cut has already begun to close.

For a while I forget hunger.

The pool is small. The island is large. Still, the pool knows something about me that I cannot name. That is a lonely feeling, to be read by fish and not by yourself.

I follow the birds until my legs shake. They eat a soft yellow fruit split open on the branch. I wait. They do not fall. They do not stagger. They wipe their beaks and come back for more.

I climb for it.

The bark is slick. The trunk sweats under my hands. Halfway up, something with too many legs runs over my fingers and disappears into a crack. I nearly let go. I hold. Pride has nothing to do with it. Falling wastes strength.

The fruit smells sweet and rotten at once. I peel back the skin with my thumbnail and touch the pulp to my tongue.

No burn.

I eat.

The first fruit is gone in three bites. The second I eat slower because I have learned one lesson at least. The third I carry down tucked inside my shirt and eat under the rock shelf where nothing can see how badly I want it.

It stays down.

For an hour, I feel rich.

Then hunger opens again.

The jungle has a law for that too. Nothing fills for long.

By midday I find the grubs. Fat white curls under dead bark where the birds have been tearing. They burst bitter between my teeth. I gag once and keep chewing. I have eaten worse from kinder hands.

The grubs stay down.

That becomes the day. Watch. Test. Wait. Eat more if the body does not rebel.

Fruit. Grub. Rainwater. A brown frog the birds take whole from a leaf. I break its neck against a stone and eat it raw, fighting the slide of it in my throat. It stays down too.

I begin to make a map of what wants me dead.

Bright red berries: left alone by birds, left alone by me.

Clear fish: left alone.

Black water: left alone.

Yellow fruit: safe for now.

White grubs: safe, ugly.

Brown frogs: safe if I can catch them.

The frog teaches a smaller lesson. When I reach for the next one, it puffs itself round and pisses on my hand. The smell is sharp enough to make my eyes water. I drop it, and it lands in the mud with the dignity of a little king, then springs away.

I laugh before I can stop myself.

It comes out thin and cracked. It hurts my ribs. The jungle takes no notice. No guard turns. No boot comes. No one says quiet.

The second frog does not get away.

By evening, I am tired enough to shake but not empty enough to lie down and stop.

Small victories are still food.

The fever comes after dark.

At first I think it is only the heat. The jungle holds the day long after the sun is gone. Warm mud. Warm stone. Warm air pressing down into the crack where I sleep.

Then the shaking starts.

Not cold. Not fear. The body turning against itself.

My stomach knots. My skin burns. The rock above me blurs, sharpens, blurs again. I crawl out from under the shelf and vomit until there is nothing left to bring up. The fruit comes first. Then bile. Then strings of spit that shine in the firefly dark.

The clear fish, I think, though I did not eat them.

The frog.

The grubs.

The air.

The jungle has too many knives. I cannot tell which one is in me.

I curl on my side in the mud with both hands over my belly. The fever climbs. I have seen men die like this in holds and camps and swamp ports. First the heat. Then the shaking. Then the eyes go loose and the mouth begins to ask for mothers, gods, water, anything.

I keep silent.

There is no one here to answer.

I hold my name behind my teeth.

My name.

If the jungle takes everything else, it can choke on that.

The dark folds over me.

When I wake, it is morning.

That is wrong.

I lie still and take the count.

Mouth dry. Belly sore. Hands steady. Head clear. No shaking. No fever. The bites on my arms have gone down. The cuts across my palms have pulled into thin red lines. I cannot remember falling asleep. A black piece is gone from the night.

I sit up too fast and almost faint.

Weak, yes.

Dying, no.

I look at my hands for a long time.

Cuts do not close that fast. Men do not breathe this air, sleep in this mud, and wake cleaner than they were.

But I have.

The thought is too large to hold. It slips away when I try to grip it.

Good. Let it go for now.

Understanding can wait. Food cannot.

The tide teaches next.

I notice it because the brown channel pulls back from its own banks and leaves the mud shining. Little pools appear among the roots. Small bodies trapped in them flick and burrow, betrayed by water that has remembered the sea.

Crabs first. Dark, heavy shells with claws too slow once I learn where to put my hand. I smash them with stones and eat the meat raw. It is sweet. It stays.

Then a pale eel buried under mud, all muscle and slime. I almost lose it twice before I pin it with both hands. It twists so hard I think it will break my wrist. I bite behind the head because I have no knife and no shame left for the work of staying alive.

It stays too.

At low water, the jungle gives.

At high water, it takes back.

The tide also shows me roads.

When the water pulls away, the mud keeps every passing. Bird feet. Crab scratches. The belly-line of a snake. Once, a set of prints like small hands, too long in the finger, pressed beside the pool where the clear fish hide. They come out of the water and go back in. No bird has feet like that. No child should be here.

I crouch over them until the tide creeps around my toes.

The pool behind me dimples once.

I leave before I see what makes the second mark.

The next low tide takes me farther than sense should allow.

The channel empties itself into shining flats where the mud turns silver under a skin of water. Roots stand exposed to the knee, black and tangled, and between them lie pockets of sand firm enough to bear my weight if I test every step first. The air smells of salt now. Not open sea. Hidden sea. The kind that slips under the island and comes back carrying secrets.

I go because the easy pools near the rock have learned me.

Crabs dive sooner. Frogs keep higher leaves. Birds strip the yellow fruit before I reach the branch. A place remembers hunger. If I eat from one hand too often, that hand closes.

So I follow the new flats.

The first treasure is a field of little clams buried under the sand. I find them by watching a gray bird with legs like sticks stab its beak into the flat. The bird pulls up a clam, cracks it against a root, and swallows the meat. It does this three times and does not die.

I take that as permission.

The clams are hard to open without a knife. I use a stone and mash more shell than meat. Sand gets into my teeth. One shell pinches my fingertip hard enough to make me curse, and I spend a foolish breath glaring at a food with no eyes.

"You lost," I tell it after I pry it open.

The clam offers no argument.

That makes me feel better than it should.

Farther on, the flats become strange.

The mud is covered in round holes, each one rimmed with little pellets. When my shadow crosses them, eyes vanish down every hole at once. Hundreds. Maybe more. The ground itself seems to blink.

I kneel and wait.

One eye returns. Then another. Then a small pale crab lifts itself out and waves one huge red claw at me, angry as a lord with one arm too fine for work. Another crab answers with its own claw. Soon the whole flat is full of red waving hands.

I laugh again.

The sound sends them down their holes.

Hunger would have me dig. Sense keeps me still. Too many holes. Too much open ground. No cover. The tide will return, and I have not learned how fast. The crabs can keep their kingdom for today.

Then the wind shifts.

Something sour reaches me.

Not rot. Not dead fish.

Egg.

I find the nest under a shelf of root where the mud has dried in a black crust. Six eggs lie inside, speckled green and brown, each one warm to the touch. I look up at once.

The mother stands ten strides away.

At first I think she is a fallen branch propped on two legs. Then her head turns, and I see a beak long as my forearm, a white mask around both eyes, and a crest of red feathers lying flat against her skull. She is taller than I am. Thin, but not weak. Her knees bend the wrong way for comfort. Her feet spread wide on the mud, each toe ending in a black nail.

She opens her beak.

No sound comes out.

The silence runs colder than a cry.

I lower the egg.

"I am hungry," I say.

She does not care. I like her for that. She knows her law.

Six eggs. One starving boy. One mother with a killing beak.

I take one.

The bird strikes before I clear the nest.

I throw myself sideways. The beak punches into the mud where my throat was. She tears it free and comes again, all legs and blade and white-eyed fury. I scramble under the root shelf, the egg clutched against my belly. She cannot reach me cleanly there. She tries anyway. Her beak stabs through gaps in the roots. Once it catches my shoulder and opens a line of fire across the skin.

Blood runs down my arm.

The bird freezes.

Her head pulls back. The red crest lifts. She smells the air with quick jerks of her beak, then steps away from the root shelf.

Not fear.

Recognition would be worse.

She gathers the remaining eggs under herself with careful feet and backs toward the taller reeds, never taking her eyes from the blood on my arm. I stay under the roots until she is gone.

Only then do I break the egg.

Warm yolk runs over my tongue, rich enough to hurt.

I eat it raw. I lick the shell. I hate myself a little and am alive.

When I wash the cut later, the clear fish flee from the red thread again. The bird's beak should have left a wound for days. By evening it is only a bright seam. A beetle touches the watered blood, curls its legs, and drops from the stone.

I keep failing to read myself.

For three days I live by it. Sleep under the rock. Drink from the seep. Work the low pools when the channel draws away. Stay out when it rises.

My leg heals.

That becomes harder to ignore.

The cut from the wreck should be swollen and green by now. Instead the skin pulls tight and clean around it. Red turns pink. Heat leaves. When I wash it in the seep, old blood flakes away and new flesh shows beneath.

Too fast.

I press a thumb beside the wound until pain comes. Pain is honest. Pain says the leg is mine.

The healing says something else.

I cannot name it.

That night I dream of the slaver's hand on my neck. In the dream his fingers are cold. Not dead-ship cold. Human cold. Worse, because he is alive and choosing it.

When I wake, my name is in my mouth.

I nearly say it.

I bite down until blood comes instead.

The jungle does not get my name.

Not yet.

On the fourth evening, as rain gathers in the leaves and the channel rises brown around the roots, I hear a sound far above the water.

Low.

Long.

Made by a throat too large for any man.

The hunger in my belly goes still.

Another hunger opens under it, one I have no food for.

Not meat.

Not water.

Someone.

I crouch under the rock shelf with crab shell in my hand and listen until the sound comes again.

It is not close.

But it is not alone.

Before sleep takes me, rain clears from the eastern sky and a line of pale lights drifts over the channel.

Not fireflies. Larger. Blue-white, soft as breath, floating above the water on thin wings that hardly move. They pass over the clear fish pool, and the fish rise under them in a silent cloud, not eating, not fleeing. Only following the light until the last wing goes dark beyond the roots.

For one moment the island is beautiful in a way that does not ask anything of me.

Then something screams in the trees and beauty runs for its life.

A laugh climbs up and dies behind my teeth.

The island cannot bear to be gentle for more than a breath without remembering itself.
