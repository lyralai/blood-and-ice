# Chapter 6: First Blood

The voice does not return with the morning.

I wait for it longer than I should. Crouched at the mouth of the den, spearless, hungry, listening while the jungle sweats and birds scream in the high leaves. Nothing answers. Whatever called in the dark has gone back into the green, and the green has closed behind it.

My belly has less patience than my fear.

The pools near the channel are empty.

I work them one by one in the low tide, knees sunk in black mud, fingers searching under roots. The first pool gives me nothing. The second gives me a shell no bigger than my thumb. The third only leeches and foul water. I have eaten the easy edge down to silence.

If a place feeds you, it can be emptied.

By midday the hunger is sharp enough to make the world simple. I climb to the high stone above the channel and look for what else can be eaten.

They wallow in a brown basin where the channel widens. Four of them. Heavy bodies sunk to the belly in mud, dark backs shining, blunt heads rooting through the muck. Their tusks are yellow. Their little eyes are quick.

They are fat.

The sight of it hurts.

For a while I only watch. Hunger wants me down there now, teeth first, stupid as a starving dog. I make myself stay still. The swine are slow until they are not. When a heron lifts from the reeds, one jerks its head up and the others freeze. When danger passes, they settle again. Always near the deeper water. Always with an escape behind them.

The smallest has a torn ear and a scar white across its snout. The others shove it from the best mud. When it finds a fat root, a larger sow shoulders in and takes it. The torn-ear snaps once, bold and foolish, and gets driven backward for the trouble.

I should choose the weakest because weak meat costs less.

Instead I watch it too long.

Hunger can use pity as well as greed.

To kill one, I have to stand between it and the channel.

Hands will not be enough.

I go back to the den and make a spear.

The branch I choose is hard, dark wood from deadfall that has not yet softened. I strip it with a stone flake. I grind one end against rock until the point forms. The work takes the rest of the day and most of the skin from my palms.

Blood stains the shaft.

I bind a sharp stone chip below the point with root fiber, pulling the cord tight with my teeth. It is an ugly weapon. Crooked. Crude. Mine.

By firelight I test the point against my thumb.

It bites.

Good.

The next morning I go down before dawn with mud on my skin and fear under my ribs.

The wind comes toward me. I keep it in my face so my scent runs behind. Every step is chosen. Roots. Stones. Places where mud will hold my foot instead of sucking it down with a sound.

The swine are where I left them.

I choose the smallest, though small is a lie. It is still heavier than I am twice over, thick with meat, its hide scarred by old fights. It roots at the edge of the basin three long strides from deep water.

"Sorry," I mouth.

The swine does not care. That is good. If it cared, I might fail.

I move around it.

Slow.

Low.

Between the swine and the channel.

It sees me.

The head snaps up. Mud flies from the tusks. For one breath we stare at each other across the brown shallows, two hungry lives caught in the same narrow place.

Then it charges.

It is not slow now.

The mud explodes under it. The body that looked heavy as a fallen log becomes speed and anger and yellow tusk. I plant the butt of the spear against the ground and throw all my weight behind it.

The point goes in.

The swine screams.

I nearly let go.

It is a terrible sound, high and broken, too close to a child. The shaft bucks in my hands. The animal slams into me and drives me backward. One tusk opens my thigh. Pain flashes white. Mud fills my mouth.

I hold on.

The swine fights for the water. I fight for the spear. There is no clean killing, no brave shape to it, only two bodies in black mud, one trying to live and one too hungry to let it.

Blood comes hot over my hands.

So much of it.

The smell fills me. Iron. Salt. Heat. The swine weakens by degrees, then all at once. Its legs fold. The scream becomes a wet grunt and then stops.

I stay on top of it long after it is dead, both hands locked to the spear, shaking so hard my teeth click.

I have killed fish. Crabs. Small lives I could hold in one hand.

This had a voice.

The belly-hunger goes quiet. The Red wakes when blood runs. It wants warmth leaving the body. For a breath I enjoy it, and my mouth goes dry.

That frightens me more than the tusk.

I drag the carcass up to the den over half a day, inch by inch, resting whenever my legs fail. The cut on my thigh bleeds, then tightens. It should swell. It should poison. Instead the edges pull together as if some hidden hand inside me is stitching too fast.

I hate that hidden hand.

I need it.

At the den I cut the swine open with stone and sharpened shell. I have never seen so much meat belong to one death. Fat under the hide. Dark muscle. Organs slick as river stones. I make a mess of the butchering, but the fire forgives ugly work.

I waste more than I want to. That hurts almost as much as the cut in my thigh. The hide tears wrong. The gut splits and fouls good meat. My hands shake with tiredness, and the knife-stone slips twice. Each mistake is a mouth I cannot feed later.

The river-swine teaches after death too.

Its hide is too thick for my poor edge until I learn to cut from inside out. Its sinews pull into cord if I scrape them clean and twist them while they dry. The bladder holds water once I rinse it enough to stop gagging. The ribs can become hooks. The jawbone makes a better scraper than the stone I have been using.

No part wants to be useful.

I have to fight each piece into purpose.

By midday I am slick to the elbows. Blood dries on my arms in brown scales. Fat gets under my nails. Flies gather at the edge of the clean space around my skin and hang there in a trembling wall, furious at being denied.

"Wait your turn," I tell them.

They answer by humming louder.

The lizard from the den returns at the smell. It appears on the stone wall with its half-tail lifted and its cloudy eye bright in the firelight. I toss it a scrap no bigger than a fingernail. It snatches the meat and retreats to a crack.

"Rent paid," I say.

It comes back for more within five breaths.

So it is a thief and a liar. I respect that. I feed it again.

The work makes me rich in ways I barely understand. Rich with grease for the fire. Rich with cord. Rich with bones that can become tools if my hands learn patience. Rich with more meat than I can eat before the rot comes asking for its share.

The richness frightens me.

In the hold, food meant someone had chosen who would weaken first. A crust passed to one boy made three others watch him with flat eyes. A cup of water held too long could start a fight. Enough was never only enough. Enough was danger wearing a kinder face.

I catch myself hiding strips of smoked meat in three places. Under the stone lip. Behind the dry bark. Inside a hollow root near the den. Then I catch myself checking them, again and again, as if hunger has hands and might steal while I look away.

At sunset, I take one strip from each hiding place and lay them together beside the fire.

All in one place.

My hands hate it.

I make them stay open.

"Mine," I say.

Not a whisper this time.

The word sounds too large under the stone roof. I almost look over my shoulder for the man who will punish it.

No one comes.

Only the lizard, bold with rent and lies, creeping toward the edge of the meat.

"No," I tell it.

It freezes with one foot raised.

For a moment we stare at each other across the fire.

Then it backs away with great dignity and pretends the whole plan belonged to someone else.

I laugh until my ribs hurt.

By dark I have strips hung on green sticks over smoke, a pit scraped for scraps, and the skull set beyond the den where its eyes can look somewhere else. The torn ear lies flat against the mud.

I touch it once.

"You were hungry too," I say.

The words change nothing. I say them anyway.

That night I eat until my belly aches.

Fat runs down my wrist. Smoke gathers under the stone roof. Outside, the insects find the scraps and swarm over them, black and eager. They leave me untouched.

For the first time, my body is full.

Still I keep looking at the dark wet place where the throat opened. Still I hide the best strip from no one but myself.

The other hunger watches from under my ribs, awake and unsatisfied.

Near midnight the jungle cuts off.

The flies lift from the meat. The frogs stop. Even the leaves seem to hold their breath.

Something passes outside the den.

I see nothing. I hear weight placed carefully on wet ground. A slow foot. Another. Too heavy for swine, too quiet for anything so large. A smell slides through the wall: rot, old meat, and a heat like breath from a mouth full of teeth.

My hand finds the spear.

The hunter stops where the swine bled into the ground.

For a long time, nothing moves.

I lie in the dark with the fire low and my own blood going cold in me. The hunger that leaned toward killing all day curls down small and silent.

At last the weight moves away.

The jungle begins again by pieces. One insect. Then another. Then the frogs.

Sleep does not come.

In the morning I find the print.

It is sunk deep in the mud below the den wall, wider than my chest. One claw is missing at the outer edge, leaving a blunt gouge beside the clean marks. Rot and wet stone. Not swine. Not anything I know.

I crouch above it until the sun climbs high.

Yesterday I thought the swine was the largest danger in my world.

Today I know the swine was meat for something else.
