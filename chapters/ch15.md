# Chapter 15: The Apex

The jungle goes silent before the Hesh comes.

Not quiet. Silent.

The insects lift from the meat. Birds vanish into the high green. Even the Skell leave their midden piles and crawl into dark places. A hole opens in the sound of the island, and every living creature knows the shape of what belongs there.

The Vorth know before I do.

That is what frightens me.

I have seen them hungry, angry, grieving. I have seen young males cuff each other bloody beside the fire and females bare teeth over meat. I have not seen the tribe afraid.

Now fear moves through them like cold smoke.

Small ones are gathered up. Half-grown hunters leave the lower paths. Torn-Ear will not go beyond the first bank. Even Voss-Marr stands quieter, contempt pressed over something he does not want anyone to see.

The seer lifts his ash-pale head toward the rot-thickets and gives the long meaning-sound over empty air.

Grief for what has not happened.

The Hesh shows itself the next morning.

I am below the stones with Torn-Ear and Marrow, reading a rot edge where burrow-runners cut across the mud. Marrow clicks once from his root perch.

Look.

The thicket parts.

The Hesh comes through.

It is longer than two Vorth lying head to foot. Low, black, ridged, wet with swamp water. Its head is broad and flat, its eyes high, its mouth too long. When it opens that mouth, the teeth are not made for one clean bite. They are made to hold, to roll, to drown, to take a body apart while the water fills its throat.

It wears the swamp on it. Weed hangs from the ridges of its back. Tiny white crab-spiders cling in the plates behind its jaw, feeding on old blood and scraps of skin. Along one flank, scars cross over scars, pale ropes under black hide where spears, teeth, claws, maybe Vorth hands, all failed to matter. The tail moves last and moves least, a slow rudder through mud.

The smell reaches after it.

Rot. River. Old fish. A musk thick enough to taste.

Every small biting creature in the reeds lifts away from that smell.

It does not hurry.

Nothing has taught it haste.

Torn-Ear makes a sound I have never heard from him. Fear, crushed down in a strong chest. Then he runs for the bank.

The Hesh turns after movement.

I stay motionless.

The old hold-cunning saves me. The lesson beaten into small bodies by cruel hands: be nothing, make no shape, let the eye pass over. I flatten into the mud beside a root while the Hesh drags its weight past me, close enough that I smell old meat between its teeth.

It follows Torn-Ear to the edge of firmer ground and stops.

There.

That is the first useful fact.

The Hesh is king of water, mud, and soft bank. It does not climb. It will not spend its long body on high stone. Its strength has a border.

Borders matter. A leech owns a wound until salt finds it. A vine owns a tree until fire comes. The strongest jaw in the green is still only strongest where the ground agrees with it.

The Hesh tests the border anyway.

It lays its flat head on the first stone and keeps it there, watching Torn-Ear pant above it. The mouth opens a little. Water slides from the teeth in bright strings. One front foot comes forward, claws spreading on rock slick with algae.

For one breath I think the island has lied to me.

Then the foot slips.

The Hesh does not fall. It is too old a killer to spend balance foolishly. It only draws back and strikes the stone with its tail hard enough to crack a white vein through the surface. The sound pops through the bank. Torn-Ear flinches. I flinch too, deep in the mud where no one can see.

The Hesh waits after that.

Not confused. Not angry in the way Vorth anger is loud and immediate. It waits as if the stone has made an argument and the Hesh is considering how long hunger can sit beside insult. Its eyes hold on Torn-Ear. Then, slowly, they slide along the bank.

Over the roots.

Over the mud where I lie.

Past me.

My lungs burn. A mosquito feeds at the corner of my eye and I let it. Mud crawls into my ear. My fingers ache from keeping still. There is a kind of fear that makes a body flee, and another that nails every part of it to the world.

The Hesh teaches the second kind.

At last it turns back into the rot pool.

It does not leave quickly. It drags itself through the soft bank with such weight that the mud folds after it like a mouth closing. Tiny fish leap from the brown water and strand themselves rather than stay under its passing shadow. A frog, bright blue and stupid with panic, lands beside my wrist. I could eat it. I stay still.

The Hesh moves.

The frog vanishes.

Not eaten. The water takes it when the tail sweeps past. A small life caught in the labor of a larger one.

That is the Hesh too. Not only teeth. A wake of harm that keeps harming after the hunger has gone.

The second fact comes after it leaves.

The Skell come out of hiding first.

Not to feed. Not yet. They move wrong for feeding, low and spread, eyes fixed on the path the Hesh took. The twisted one I fed limps at the edge of them. It sniffs the mud where the great belly dragged, then jerks back as if the smell has bitten it.

One by one, the Skell press their narrow heads to the ground.

Not bowing.

Listening.

Marrow looks at me.

Neither answer is in me.

Then the brown channel below the bank ripples.

A Mure hand rises through the water, pale fingers spread against the mud. Another follows. A face breaks the surface, eyes black and round, hair slicked flat. The Mure does not climb out. It only watches the Hesh track, throat fluttering with silent breath.

Skell above.

Mure below.

Vorth on the stone.

All three peoples marking the same path and none willing to cross it.

The Hesh is not only a large beast, then. It is a border in the island's mind. When it passes, even the old hatreds make room.

The twisted Skell sees me watching and scratches three lines in the mud with one long claw. Then a fourth line crossing them.

I understand less than I feel.

Three peoples.

One hunger above them.

The Mure sinks before I can read more.

The Skell scatter when Torn-Ear groans.

Only the lines remain, already filling with black water.

Later, when I crawl back to the place after Torn-Ear has been taken up the bank and carried home half-staggering, I find more signs.

The Hesh's belly has crushed a nest of reed-rats without eating them. Their small bodies lie pressed flat in the mud, pink mouths open. Around them, the Skell have placed bits of shell in a half circle. Not a feast marker. Not a warning I know. A count, maybe. Or a kindness. Or only the shape grief takes when hands are made for tearing.

At the waterline, Mure fingers have drawn loops in the silt. Each loop breaks on the side facing the pool.

Do not enter.

Or, entered and not returned.

I make myself look at every mark until the fear becomes knowledge.

The Hesh has been here before. Not once. Many times. It moves through the lower green in seasons, when heat thickens and river-pig breed in the mangrove shallows. It takes Mure from channels, Skell from midden paths, Vorth from the low hunts. The island does not forget. It has built manners around the mouth: when to vanish, where to stand, which pools go silent, which branches are too low over brown water.

The Hesh is not an interruption.

It is a harvest.

That is why the old ones fear it so cleanly. Fear without surprise. Fear that has had children.

It lifts its head and bellows.

The sound moves through the mud into my bones. Up on the high stones, the tribe falls silent under it.

The Hesh is naming the lower green its own.

Torn-Ear lives because he reached the bank in time. I live because I was small enough to be missed. Neither feels like victory.

That night the tribe crowds close around the fire.

Kala holds Marrow and me under one arm. Her stilling sound starts, then fails before the last part. She cannot say nothing comes when something has come and named the edge of our home.

Gull-Marr stands on his lip and looks outward, not at us.

The king has no answer.

Strength cannot answer a mouth that turns strength into meat.

He tries to make one anyway.

At moonrise he sends three hunters down the old stone throat toward the first bank. Voss-Marr goes with them. Of course he does. His ribs are not yet opened by any boar. His pride is whole enough to be loud. They carry heavy spears with fire-hardened points and slabs of river-pig fat wrapped in leaves. Bait and courage. The tribe watches them go as if watching men walk into weather.

Kala's arm tightens around me.

I slip free before she can stop me.

Marrow slips after me because he is a fool and my shadow, and because there is no rope strong enough to tie him to wisdom when danger has my shape.

We follow high along the root shelf, above the hunters and downwind. I tell myself I only need to see. That is nearly true. I also need to know whether strength can do what I cannot yet imagine.

The hunters set the fat at the edge of the lower mud and retreat to stone. Voss-Marr stands too close. The others know it. He knows they know. That keeps him there.

For a long while nothing comes.

Then the mud breathes.

The Hesh rises where no beast so large should have been hidden. Brown water sheets from its back. It does not go for the fat. It goes for the far hunter, the one whose spear tip dips one finger lower than the rest.

The strike is too fast for its size.

Stone saves him. Not skill. Not courage. Stone. The Hesh's teeth close on air and splinter the spear instead of the arm behind it. The hunter falls backward, screaming. Voss-Marr roars and drives his own spear down into the flat skull.

The point bites.

It breaks.

The Hesh turns its head with the broken shaft standing in the hide above one eye. It looks at Voss-Marr as if he has touched it with a reed.

Then it takes the river-pig fat and sinks.

The whole attack has meant less to it than the bait.

Voss-Marr stands shaking on the bank, empty spear in hand. The other hunters pull the fallen one back. Blood runs from his palm where splinters have opened him. No one is dead.

That is the victory Gull-Marr can claim.

No one is dead.

The tribe receives the hunters with hard relief and no song. Voss-Marr throws the broken shaft into the fire. It burns badly, wet wood hissing. His eyes find me in the crowd. I have mud on my knees from where I crouched on the shelf. He knows I followed. He knows I saw.

His shame wants somewhere to go.

It chooses me.

I lie awake against Kala's side while the tribe sleeps badly. The Hesh owns the lower paths now. It will take from the edges. A careless hunter. A child too near water. Torn-Ear, if pride leads him down again.

Gull-Marr will hold the high stone and call the losses necessary.

That is what kings do when they meet the edge of their power.

I am not king.

I am weak. Light. Hard to see when I choose stillness. I read mud better than any Vorth alive.

The Hesh is too strong to fight.

It is not too strong to read.

Before dawn the seer comes to me. He touches the eye-and-thorn on my chest, then points into the dark where the Hesh vanished. The meaning-sound rises from him, first over the mark, then over the dark, joining them in some old grammar I cannot read.

This one.

That one.

One telling.

The seer thinks I am an answer.

Whether that makes him wise or mad is beyond me.

But by morning I have decided to hunt the Hesh.

Marrow decides it too, though no one asks him.

He appears at my side before dawn with two coils of fiber over his good shoulder, a stone knife in his teeth, and the solemn face of someone doing a very bad job of seeming older than he is.

I point back to Kala.

He points at the Hesh track.

I bare my teeth.

He bares his wider.

For a moment, despite the fear waiting in the green, I almost laugh.

The island is full of old gods, drowned warnings, hungry mouths, and stone marks no living tongue can explain.

It is also full of Marrow, who has decided the largest killer in the low country is best answered with rope and attitude.

That almost makes the fear bright enough to bear.

I put my forehead against his for one breath, the way Kala does when she wants him still. He goes solemn at once, pleased with himself and trying not to show it.

Then he trips on one of his own rope coils and nearly falls down the path.

I catch him by the scruff. He pretends he meant to do it.

For the first time since the Hesh came through the thicket, the green lets me laugh.
