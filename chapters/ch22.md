# Chapter 22: The Sigil

The captain stops searching the dark.

He starts feeding it.

The first gift appears just before dusk.

Two owned men carry it down to the mud line while an owner watches from the rise behind them. Cooked meat rests on a strip of bark. Bright water fills a gourd. A twist of salt sits beside both, white and impossible, richer than bone.

They set everything where Yara usually crouches.

Then they leave.

No watcher stays in the open.

That makes it worse.

Bait does not always need teeth showing.

I know bait. A noose under leaves. A crying animal tied near a thicket. Easy meat on ground too clean. The lesson is simple: when the world gives freely, it means to close around the taking hand.

I lie in the roots until full dark.

The meat cools. The smell does not. Smoke, fat, salt, human fire. My belly pulls toward it. That hunger I understand and can master. Hunger is honest. It wants, and if denied it curses, weakens, waits.

This is not only belly-hunger.

Marrow knows it too.

He will not look at the food. He stands uphill with his body turned toward home, high shoulder sharp in the dark, and waits for me to follow. When I stay where I am, he comes down, picks up a stone, and throws it into the mud beside the bark.

Not at the food.

Near it.

Enough to say what he thinks of gifts left by men.

I almost smile.

Almost.

There is a smallness in his anger that makes it sharper. He cannot forbid me as Kala can. He cannot shame me as the seer can. He cannot stand before the tribe and make the old males listen. He has only a stone, a crooked hand, and the certainty that I am being foolish.

He spends all three.

That should be enough to turn me back.

Instead I watch the place where the meat waits, and hate how badly I want the world to mean gift when it has always meant trap.

The next dusk, more comes.

This time the owned men bring roasted fish, a little clay cup of sweet pulp, and water clear enough to see the moon in before night has fully risen. The owner behind them does not watch the jungle. He watches the ground as if that proves he is not watching.

I leave it.

The third dusk, Yara comes with them.

Her face is empty in the camp way, but her hand gives warning low against her thigh.

No.

Then another sign.

Drug.

I know the second from the caged Mure, from the slack-eyed Skell, from the bitter smell around the bowls given to creatures men want quiet but not dead.

The owner speaks. Yara repeats his words louder toward the trees.

I know enough now to catch pieces.

Gift.

Friend.

Marked one.

No harm.

Friend is the ugliest word in the sentence.

They leave the food and take Yara back with them. She does not look over her shoulder. She does not need to. Her warning remains in the mud where her fingers drew it and then smoothed it away.

Marrow presses a strip of dried Vorth meat into my hand that night when we return to the high stones. His own portion, I think. He gives it angrily, as if kindness disgusts him but not as much as letting me hunger after camp scraps.

I take it.

That should be enough.

It is not.

The captain has read me.

Not my paths through the green. Not yet. Not my blood. Not fully. He has read the emptiness I thought I had hidden even from myself. Force will not draw me out. Nets thrown blind will lose men and teach me caution. Fire would drive the island against him as much as me.

So he makes the trap look like welcome.

That is sharper than iron.

He is not only reading me.

He is reading his own camp.

The sickness has made the owners quarrel. I hear it in the night, voices cutting through canvas, a man with rank speaking too loudly because he feels rank slipping under his feet. The cages are not full enough. The island takes men and gives back fever, broken hooks, animals that bite through bars, and rumors of a marked wild boy who eats poison and vanishes.

Drevan cannot show doubt to them.

So he makes patience look like command. He feeds the dark in front of every watcher. He turns failure into a plan the others can see.

That is a kind of strength.

I hate him more for having it.

One afternoon I watch him spend that strength on his own men.

The camp is wet with heat. Rain has fallen and gone, leaving the canvas roofs sagging and the paths between them churned into brown paste. Men move slowly. Even the owners walk as if the air has weight. Two collared rowers carry a barrel of boiled water between them, and every free man they pass looks at the barrel with the same thirst and contempt.

A young owner knocks the barrel with his hip.

Not hard. Hard enough.

Water spills into the mud.

The rower on the left drops to one knee and tries to cup it back with both hands before sense catches him. The owner laughs because the shape of power is sometimes no larger than making another person reach for what cannot be saved.

Drevan sees.

The camp sees Drevan seeing.

For three breaths no one moves. Then the captain walks down from his black awning. The fever has put a shine on him by then. His scar is red at the edges, his left hand trembles once before he closes it around the spear shaft. Still, the camp parts for him as if his sickness has only made him more dangerous.

He asks one question.

I miss most of the words. I know tone. I know the owner answers badly because he is frightened and ashamed of being frightened before collared men.

Drevan strikes him across the mouth with the butt of the spear.

The sound is flat and ugly. Not a fight. Not rage. Order.

The camp holds its breath.

Drevan points to the mud, then to the barrel, then to the cook fires. The young owner goes pale. He drops to his knees beside the collared rower and begins scraping water from the mud into a cracked bowl, knowing every man sees him do it. Knowing the collared men see it too.

Mercy, some might call it.

Justice, if they were hungry for clean names.

I watch Drevan's face and see neither. He has lost water. He has lost order. He chooses the cheaper loss to mend the dearer one. Shame one owner in public, and the collared men carry barrels tomorrow without flinching. Let the owner laugh, and water becomes another war inside the camp.

The rower does not smile.

He is too wise for that.

He keeps his eyes on the mud while a free man learns humiliation beside him, and the whole camp remembers who allowed the lesson.

This is how sickness makes new ladders. The strong are still above. The weak are still beneath. But fever saws at the rungs, and Drevan spends each break before anyone else can climb through it.

Yara stands near the cage line with a bundle of wet cloth in her arms. She watches too. Her face gives nothing to the men around her, but when Drevan turns away she looks at the rower, then at the shamed owner, then at the trees where I hide.

Her eyes say what her hands cannot.

See him whole.

Not good.

Not simple.

Whole.

Later, when the camp thins toward sleep, I see why she wants me to see it.

Another man has power there. Not captain. Not marked. A narrow man with a shaved head and a ledger board wrapped in oiled cloth. He moves through the camp counting what Drevan spends: water, rope, dried grain, hooks, men still strong enough to row. The owners make space for him though he wears no fine coat. Even Drevan listens when the narrow man speaks.

Quartermaster, Yara called him once, shaping the word slowly because I had no place to put it.

The quartermaster wants the boats gone.

I understand that without knowing all his words. He points to the sick tents. To the low water barrels. To the cages with more empty space than cargo. His finger cuts toward the sea again and again. Leave with what we have. Sell the Skell. Sell the Mure. Sell the marked boy if he comes easy, but do not let a story eat the ship before coin can be made from it.

Drevan wants the mark.

The quartermaster wants survival.

The owners want proof that their dead were not spent on a rumor.

The collared men want food, water, and one more dawn.

Yara wants me warned. Yara wants Drevan satisfied enough that he does not put a hook through her warnings. Yara wants a road out of the camp and knows every road here is made from someone else's ribs.

No one wants one clean answer.

That is what makes the camp more frightening than a Hesh. A Hesh hungers in one direction. Men hunger in many, and call the crossing of hungers order.

On the fifth dusk, I go down.

Marrow follows until the last root and then blocks my path. Teeth bared. Curled hand shaking. Small body set against mine as if he can hold back every boat on the water by standing there.

"Stay," I whisper in Vorth.

His face changes when I use Kala's word.

I hate myself for that.

I step around him anyway.

The offering waits on the bark. Meat, water, salt. No Yara. No visible watcher. A better trap than the first because it lets me pretend no one sees.

I crouch over the food.

Not eating. Reading.

Knife cuts on bark. Smoke from their fire. Salt rubbed into the meat. Fat cooled white at the edge. The water smells clean at first, then bitter underneath. A sleeping draught, or something like it. Enough to slow limbs, blur fear, make rope easier.

Drugged.

I should leave it.

Instead I eat.

The drug comes into me as a soft weight behind the eyes. For three breaths the world blurs at the edges. Then the warmth under my ribs turns, quiet and deep. My blood takes the poison, breaks it, throws it aside as it has thrown aside fever, venom, rot-water, and every sickness the island has tried to plant in me.

My sight clears.

My hands stay steady.

The captain thinks he is making me heavy.

He is wrong about my blood.

He is right about the hunger.

I eat every offering after that.

I tell myself it is strategy. Let him think the drug works slowly. Let him waste patience. Let him feed me while I learn his watches, his paths, his sickness, his number of nets and hooks. Let him believe the marked creature grows tame at the mud line.

Those reasons are true.

They are not the whole truth.

The whole truth is uglier.

I like the shape of welcome.

Warm meat set where I can reach it. Clean water. Salt. Once a strip of dried fruit so sweet it hurts my jaw. No blow. No command. No hand on the neck. No one saying mine.

Only a gift.

Only the shape of welcome.

The hook is there. I smell it. I see the line. Some starving part of me leans anyway.

Each night I return later to the high stones.

The tribe notices.

Voss-Marr notices first because hatred is a kind of attention. He gathers his young strong ones on the far side of the fire and watches me eat with human smoke in my hair. His lip curls when I pass. Once he makes the sign for rotten meat. Another male answers with a low laugh.

I could silence them.

That is the new danger of the lip. Not that I lack power. That I have enough to spend badly.

I let the insult pass.

It does not pass. It gathers.

The discarded ones stay near me. Torn-Ear. Stub. Grist. The scarred female whose name I still cannot shape. They know what it is to be half outside the circle. They do not ask why I go down. Perhaps they do not want to hear an answer that sounds too much like their own old hunger.

The center watches them watching me.

That is how a tribe cracks. Not all at once. A glance withheld. A haunch turned away from the shared fire. A child pulled closer when the human king comes near with camp smoke in his hair. Voss-Marr does not need to strike. He only needs to let every doubt find the right place to stand.

He is cleverer than I wanted him to be.

Kala asks without words.

She waits awake when I climb back from the coast. Her hand comes to my shoulder, my hair, my chest where the mark rests. Then she smells my mouth. Salt. Cooked meat. Camp smoke. Human kindness made poisonous by human intent.

Her eyes darken.

She does not push me away.

That would be easier.

She draws me close and lets the question stay between us.

The seer grows less gentle.

On the ninth dusk he bars my way before I leave. Old bones, clouded eye, staff planted across the trail. He touches the eye-and-thorn on my chest, then points down toward the coast, then drags one finger across his own throat.

Danger.

Death.

No farther.

I know enough Vorth to answer badly.

"Need," I say.

The seer goes very still.

It is one of the first words Kala taught me after hunger and pain. Need can mean food. Water. Shelter. A young one crying for its mother. A wound asking for moss.

It can also mean the wound beneath all those hungers.

The seer touches my chest again. Not the mark this time. The breastbone.

Then he points to the high stones under my feet.

Here.

His meaning is plain.

What you need is here.

I want that to be true.

I go down anyway.

Yara comes less often now. Always watched. Always careful. Her mouth gives the camp's words while her fingers give her own.

Drug.

Do not eat.

Captain waits.

Mark wants you.

Once, when the watcher turns to spit, she signs a word I have not learned and points to me. Then to the disc. Then to the captain's black banner.

Same.

No.

Not same.

The mark on me and the mark on them are linked, but not equal. That is all I can gather. It is enough to keep the question burning.

Once she hesitates before smoothing the mud.

The watcher has turned away. She has half a breath. Enough to give another warning. Enough to give me a word. Instead she looks at the bark, at the meat, at the place where I will crouch after she leaves.

Her face hardens.

She smooths away the mark and says the camp's friendly words more clearly.

I understand then that warning me is not the only work she is doing. She is also keeping herself alive. Perhaps she believes both can be done. Perhaps she has been made to choose so often that choice has become a wound she walks on.

Forgiving her would be easy.

Condemning her would be easier.

Reading the fear in her hand is harder than both.

The captain does not appear at the mud line until the eleventh dusk.

He comes alone.

Not truly alone. Men wait behind him in the cut brush. I hear one shift weight too heavily. Smell another's oiled rope. But the captain walks the last stretch by himself and sets the bark down with his own hand.

Then he steps back.

He speaks into the trees.

His voice is low, calm, almost kind.

I understand only fragments.

Marked one.

Hunger.

Answer.

Come.

Behind the words sits the old shape: welcome, welcome, welcome.

I stay hidden.

The captain smiles as if that too is an answer.

Then he coughs.

He turns his head aside before it can bend him, but the sound comes deep and wet. When he straightens, his hand goes to the scar I gave him. Not from pain alone. From fear.

For the first time, I see the man under the method.

Not softer.

Never that.

Only mortal.

He knows the island is in him. He knows his men are watching. He knows the marked creature in the trees may be watching too. His back stays straight because command is another kind of cage, and he is locked inside it as surely as the Mure drying under shade.

He says another word.

Home.

I know it from Yara. Not fully. Enough.

He points to himself. Then the boats. Then the sea beyond them.

Home.

For one hateful moment I understand the bait inside him too. He has somewhere he means to return. Someone, maybe. A city. A child. A debt. A patron with cleaner hands and sharper teeth. He is not cruelty born from mud. He is a man who has chosen cruelty as a road to something else.

That does not forgive him.

It makes him more dangerous.

Pure monsters are simple.

Men with reasons build ships.

He sets one more object on the bark.

Not food.

A little carved figure of dark wood, no longer than my thumb. A child might hold it. A woman maybe. The face is worn smooth by fingers. He places it beside the meat and speaks again.

Home.

Then he leaves it there and walks back toward the camp without looking behind him.

I leave the figure where it is.

I think about it until dawn.

He touches the eye-and-thorn on his chest, then points to the food, then to the place where he knows I am not quite lying.

The gesture is simple enough for any creature.

We are the same mark.

Come eat from my hand.

When he leaves, Marrow makes a sound behind me that is almost a whimper and almost a curse.

I wait until the camp is quiet to eat.

That is what I tell myself, as if waiting changes the shape of the act.

At dawn I return to the high stones with salt on my tongue and poison broken in my blood.

Kala is not asleep.

The seer is not asleep.

Voss-Marr watches from the far fire, eyes bright with the first clean hope I have seen in him since I took the lip.

The human king is taking human bait.

Let him.

The thought moves through the tribe like wind through grass.

I feel it and cannot deny it.

The captain has found the one poison my blood cannot throw off.

A gift.
