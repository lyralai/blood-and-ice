# Chapter 7: The Wreck

Three days after the swine, the river brings me the smell of men.

Tar first. Then old wood. Rust. Salt caught in places salt should not be. Under all of it, bone.

I am hunting the high bank with the spear in my hand when the wind shifts. The scent touches my face and the whole past opens its eyes.

Ship.

I should go back to the den.

Instead I follow.

The river runs brown between walls of root and hanging vine. I keep to the bank where I can, cross where I must, and move slowly when the smell thickens. Strange scent means strange danger. I know that. But this one has hooks in me.

By evening the channel opens onto a wide mud reach, and there it is.

A ship lies broken on the bar.

Not the ship that carried me. Smaller. Older. Dead longer. Its hull is split. Vines climb through the ribs. A tree has grown out of the deck and lifted part of it toward the sky. The mast lies in the mud like a fallen bone, cloth rotted to strips.

I stand in the shallows and feel the world before the island pressing close.

Men made this.

Hands cut the wood. Hands bent iron into nails. Hands tied rope and raised cloth and built a hollow belly to carry other hands across the water.

Hands also locked chains.

I stay in the shallows at first.

I read the bar first.

The bones are everywhere.

At first the green hides them. Then my eyes learn the pattern and cannot stop seeing it. A rib cage full of leaves. A skull half-sunk in mud. A leg bone washed white beside a root. More up the bank. More under the broken hull.

Many men died here.

Not torn. Not butchered. Their bones do not show the great tooth or claw. They seem to have lain down and let the island take them, one after another, until the bar was a field of what they used to be.

They tried to make order before the end.

A ring of stones surrounds a blackened fire pit near the bank. Three poles lean together above it, still tied at the top with rope gone soft as moss. A pot hangs from the poles, empty except for rainwater and two drowned beetles. Beside it lies a row of cups, set mouth-down on a plank as if someone meant to keep them clean for morning.

Morning came.

No hands lifted them.

Farther up the bar, under a roof of torn sailcloth, I find marks cut into the ship's broken ribs. Lines grouped in fives. Then more. Then more. A count kept by men who still believed counting would save them.

The last groups are crooked.

The knife that made them must have shaken.

Beside the count, another mark has been cut again and again by a different hand. A circle. A line through it. A thorn shape beyond me. The first is careful. The last is hacked so deep the rib has split around it.

Not counting.

Begging, maybe.

Or warning.

On the ground below the count marks lies a little wooden horse, one leg snapped off, its painted eye almost gone. A child toy. Or a father's memory of one. I pick it up and feel foolish grief for a person whose name I will never know.

The island killed men with plans, cups, toys, counts, and maps.

It did not only kill bodies.

It waited until hope became a smaller and smaller fire, then breathed on it.

I look at the scummed water. The fever-haze over the mud. The insects darkening the air. The rot that never sleeps.

The green killed them.

The same air I breathe. The same water I have drunk. The same biting clouds that will not touch me.

All these men died of the place I live in.

I cannot understand what that makes me.

The wreck creaks when I climb into it.

Inside, the smell is worse. Wet wood, old death, rust. Light falls green through the broken deck. Bones lie in the hold where men crawled away from rain and did not crawl out again.

I hate them a little for being dead.

I want one of them to lift his head. To curse. To spit. To look at me and prove that the world beyond the island still has human mouths in it.

Bones only grin.

I search anyway.

The green makes round and twisted shapes. Men make straight ones. I find those first: a rusted pot, a hook, a hinge, a buckle. A chain curled in the muck.

The chain stops me.

My hand is already on it before memory strikes. Iron at the wrist. Iron at the neck. The dark hold moving under my feet. A slaver's hand closing at the back of my neck with the calm of a man lifting property.

I drop the chain.

For a moment I am below another deck with bodies packed close and no room to stand straight. Piss, fear, bilge, sickness. The command-sound they used for me.

Then the tree above me drips water onto my shoulder, and I come back.

The chain lies in the mud.

The men who carried it are bones.

That helps.

Not enough.

Under the canted deck I find a chest soft with rot. It collapses when I pull it loose, spilling its hoard into the slime.

Skins, worked thin and marked in dark lines.

Marks that speak. Rows and rows of them. I touch one line with my thumb and wait for the meaning to rise.

Nothing.

There was a time before the hold when marks might have opened for me. I feel the outline of that knowing, like a door behind a wall. I cannot reach the latch.

I roll the skins carefully.

A knife lies beneath them.

Real iron. Rusted, but not ruined. Wrapped well enough that the edge remains under the stain. I work it against wet plank until the metal shows clean. When I hold it, my hand closes around the grip as if remembering a lesson my head has lost.

A knife is a tooth made by men.

I keep it.

With the knife in my hand, I can open the ship further.

I cut a curtain of vine from the broken companionway and find the stairs below. The first step gives under my foot. The second holds. Air breathes up from beneath, thick with wet timber and a sweet mineral stink that coats my tongue.

The lower deck has drowned and emptied a hundred times with the tide. Mud lies in waves across the planks. Little white crabs move through the ribs of a dead man and vanish into his coat when my shadow falls over them.

I make myself go down.

A ship is a body. I learned that before I learned many kinder lessons. It has ribs, spine, belly, mouth, lungs of sail. Men crawl inside it and trust wood to keep the sea out. Men also build cages into bellies and call them cargo space.

This one has cages.

Not many. Three, built against the inner hull with iron bars red from age. The doors stand open. One has been bent outward by a force that twisted the hinges like soft root. Inside, the floor is scratched white beneath the mud.

Fingernails.

My own fingers curl around the knife so tightly the grip bites.

There are marks cut into the wall above the first cage. Not counting lines. Words, maybe. A hand tried to carve them deep and failed before the end. I trace the first shape. Nothing opens in me. The marks remain marks.

In the second cage I find a bowl nailed to the floor.

That makes my throat close.

A bowl loose on a floor means a man can choose where to sit while he eats. A bowl nailed down means someone has decided how far the neck may move.

I back out and stand in the passage until the ship stops leaning around me.

There are no living slavers here. Only their iron. Only their design.

That should matter.

It does not matter enough.

Behind the cages, half under fallen planks, I find a narrow locker with a ring set in the lid. It takes both hands and a curse to open. Inside are bundles wrapped in waxed cloth, better kept than anything else in the wreck.

More skins.

A little brass tube green with age.

Three glass beads, blue as deep water.

And a child's shoe.

Not rotten. Not whole either. Small. Stiff. One side cut open, maybe to free a swollen foot. The leather has been rubbed soft by use before the sea ever touched it.

I set it down carefully.

The ship creaks above me.

No. Not hear. Remember. Or fear. The two are close in places like this.

I lift the brass tube and shake it. Something taps inside. The end cap resists, then gives with a squeal. A scrap of skin slides into my palm, rolled tight around a sliver of black wood.

Marks cover it in tiny rows.

At the bottom, stamped in dark red wax, is the eye-and-thorn.

My teeth ache.

The mark on the wax is not drawn by a shaking sailor. It is clean. Official. A seal for men who expect doors to open.

That last certainty is not born in this wreck.

A room with red tile.

Rain against shutters.

A woman's hand pushing my head down behind a chest.

Quiet now.

The memory vanishes so fast I gasp after it.

The knife point trembles in my hand.

Above me, water drips through the deck one drop at a time.

I wait for the woman's face to come back.

It does not.

Only my name remains behind my teeth, and a certainty I hate: the mark knew me before the island did.

The knife changes the wreck around me.

A moment ago the dark corners were mouths. Now they are corners. The vines across the deck were ropes waiting for my ankles. Now they are vines I can cut if they take hold. Iron does not make me brave. It only gives my fear a point to gather behind.

I test the edge on a strip of rotten sail.

The cloth opens.

I grin before I mean to. "Good tooth," I whisper.

I have said worse to roots.

The last find is small enough to hide in my fist.

A dark metal disc on a cord gone nearly to dust. I rub the mud away and see the mark worked into it.

An open eye.

Through the eye, a thorn.

The breath leaves me.

The mark is not alone.

When I wipe more mud from the chest's broken lid, I find it burned there too, larger, half-eaten by rot. On a strip of leather. On the handle of the dead men's seal. Cut small into the back of the wooden horse, where a child's hand would cover it without knowing.

The mark was on everything they meant to keep.

Or everything that meant to keep them.

Not like I know chain. Not like I know knife. Deeper than either. The eye and thorn live somewhere under the floor of me, below the ship, below the hold, near the place where my mother's voice keeps my name.

I grip the disc until its edge bites my palm.

Where did I see you?

The wreck gives no answer.

I lift the disc to my face. Eye. Thorn. Looking and pierced. I feel meaning there, close enough to hurt, but the words will not come. The memory will not break surface.

It is mine.

That much I know.

I tie the cord around my neck and tuck the disc against my chest. The metal is cold at first. Then it takes my warmth.

I should leave then.

The carved eye on the cup catches the little light and pins me there. Same mark. Same thorn. Same wrong pull under my ribs. I should leave.

Instead I step deeper.

The captain's cabin has collapsed on one side. A tree root spears through the roof and into the desk. Shelves lean drunkenly. A map has rotted into lace, coastlines eaten into bruises.

On the desk lies a cup. Mouth-down. Clean. No mud, no insect eggs, no green mold fur. Everything else has surrendered to the island. The cup has not.

The eye-and-thorn is carved into its base.

Small. Hidden. A mark for the hand that lifts it, not for guests.

I touch the cup with the knife point.

It tips over.

Dry red dust spills out.

Not tea. Not rust.

Powdered blood.

It spreads across the desk in a thin fan, and every biting insect in the cabin lifts at once. Mosquitoes from the wall. Ants from a seam. Little pale flies from a skull under the chair. They rise toward the dust, then stop as the disc on my chest warms against my skin.

The swarm hangs between me and the desk, trembling.

The Red answers under my ribs.

Not the same as when the swine died. Not hungry. Older. Listening. The disc burns cold against my skin, and for a breath I cannot remember the sound of my mother's cup on the table.

I back away from the cabin.

The swarm does not follow.

Neither does the memory.

When I leave the wreck, the sun is low and the bar of bones shines pale in the green light. I carry the knife, the marked skins, and the eye-and-thorn against my breastbone.

Men came here with iron, rope, marks, and chains.

The island killed them.

It has not killed me.

I walk back upriver in the dark with the dead men's knife at my hip and their unanswered mark on my chest.

Behind my teeth, I hold my own name tight.

My name.

The name does not explain the mark.

But it reminds me there are still pieces of me the world has not taken.

As I leave, something knocks once inside the wreck.

Not wood settling.

Too clean.

I turn with the knife in my hand.

The broken hold gapes black beneath the tree-grown deck. Water drips. A fly moves over a skull and lifts away. Nothing else stirs.

Then the knock comes again.

From below.

Slow. Patient. Almost polite.

I stay out of the hold.

Old ships make old sounds. Water moves trapped wood. I tell myself true things and walk away faster than I came.

Behind me, the wreck keeps its dark mouth open.
