# Chapter 13: Belonging

The rains break in the night.

I wake because the world has gone quiet.

For a long moment I lie against Kala's side and listen to the absence. No hammering leaves. No water pouring down stone. No endless hiss through the green. Only drips, breath, the low fire, and the sleeping noises of the tribe.

Above the broken canopy, stars show.

I used to count lights when I was alone. One light for each night survived. One light for each distance between me and whatever I wanted but could not reach.

This night, I leave them uncounted.

There is no between.

The old part of me notices and reaches for a knife that is not there.

When I was alone, distance kept me alive. Every want had space around it. Every sound could be tested before it reached me. Here, Kala's breath warms the back of my neck, Marrow's toes are wedged under my ribs, and some sleeping child has one hand tangled in my hair. I could not run without waking half the tribe.

For a moment the old part of me counts exits.

Then Marrow kicks in his sleep and mutters my chosen name for him, badly shaped but clear enough.

I stop counting.

At dawn the Vorth pour down from the high stones to take what the lifted rain has left. Fruit knocked loose. Shells in new pools. Fat animals driven from flooded dens. The whole tribe smells change and moves with it.

I go with them.

Not behind them.

That is new.

An old male with a torn ear waits at the head of a slope where rot ground begins. He is massive, scarred, strong enough to break a swine's spine with both hands. But the rot will not hold him. He looks down at the wet thicket, then back to me, and makes the asking sound.

Where?

I step past him onto the green skin of the ground.

He follows in my footprints.

I read the safe line by pressure, color, smell, the trembling of roots under the mud. The old male sets his huge feet exactly where mine were. I become the eye. He becomes the hand. Together we bring down the wallowing beast he could smell but not reach.

When we return, he gives me part of the liver before the others.

Not charity.

Share.

Marrow sees it and struts for both of us.

He is mine now in the way any living creature can belong without being owned. The tribe knows it. When larger young ones shove him too hard, he comes to me. When he finds a new burrow-run, he pulls me by the wrist to show it. At night he sleeps wherever my warmth is, unless Kala steals him into the curve of her arm.

This causes trouble.

Two older young ones take his food one morning and toss it between them, high over his head. Not enough to maim. Enough to remind. Marrow's high shoulder drops in that quiet no of his, but his hand stays empty.

I step between them.

The fire shelf goes still in small degrees. First the little ones. Then the nearest adults. Then Voss-Marr, who is sitting above us with one knee up and both hands loose, as though he has been waiting for a lesson someone else would start.

I hold the strike back. I take one of my own strips of liver and put it in Marrow's curled hand. Then I look at the two older ones and eat the stolen piece they meant to throw next.

Marrow's mouth falls open.

The small ones make delighted scandal sounds.

The older young ones stare at me, insulted and uncertain. They expected anger. They do not know what to do with manners used as a knife.

Kala makes no sound at all, which means she approves too much to show it.

One evening he brings me a gift.

A frog.

It is fat, green, alive, and offended.

Marrow lays it at my feet with solemn pride. I look at the frog. The frog looks back. Its throat pulses. Then it launches itself between us and vanishes into the dark.

A sound bursts out of me before I can stop it.

Laughter again.

Marrow startles, then copies it badly, which makes the sound come harder from me. The tribe turns to stare. Marrow laughs louder because they are staring. For a few breaths we are both useless with it, bent over empty hands while the best gift he owned escapes uncooked.

Kala watches us from beside the fire.

Her eyes are sad and warm.

The next morning, she takes my hand and leads me to the shelf of the dead.

I resist at the first step. That ground is not mine. The dead lie there squared to the sun, faces lifted, gifts arranged beside them. Only Kala and the seer tend that place. But Kala does not let go.

A small one died in the night, the wet still in its chest.

Kala carries it up wrapped in leaves. She kneels on the clean stone and sets my hands beneath hers. Together we straighten the little limbs. Turn the face toward morning. Lay a shell beside the hand. Smooth the fur with wet fingers.

My hands shake.

I have left the dead to rot. I have robbed them. I have slept near them. I have never helped make one kept.

When the small one is arranged, Kala guides my hand to an empty place beside the row.

She presses my palm flat to the stone.

Then hers beside it.

She makes the meaning-sound.

Kept.

It takes me too long to understand.

She is showing me where I will go when I die.

Not into the channel. Not into a pit. Not over the side of a ship when my flesh stops being useful. Here. On clean stone. Faced to the sun. Among the Vorth dead, with hands that remember the shape of me.

There are gifts that fill hunger.

There are gifts that make hunger irrelevant.

I kneel there with Kala's hand over mine and cannot breathe.

It is too much.

The hand in the hold promised I would be used until I was empty. Kala's hand promises I will be remembered when I am empty of everything. I have no practice standing under that kind of mercy. It is heavier than fear.

Gull-Marr comes up onto the death shelf while my hand is still on the stone.

Every Vorth goes still.

Kala does not remove her hand.

The king stands above us, immense and old, the gray in his hide bright under the new sun. This is his tribe. His stone. His dead. I am a soft-skinned stranger with an unexplained mark, kneeling where his people promise to remember their own.

His eyes move from me to Kala, from Kala to the empty place, from the empty place to the eye-and-thorn on my chest.

The seer has come behind him. The old one makes the meaning-sound once, low.

Gull-Marr looks at me a long while.

Then he gives a short grunt.

Allowed.

No warmth. No welcome. But no refusal.

Voss-Marr is there when it happens.

He stands below the shelf with the young strong ones behind him, and I understand by the stillness in their shoulders that he has brought them to see me refused. Or to see Kala corrected. Or to see the king put the soft-skin back where a soft-skin belongs.

Instead Gull-Marr leaves my hand on the death stone.

Voss-Marr's face does not change.

That is how I know the change is deep.

He turns and goes down.

The tribe breathes again.

Kala's hand remains over mine until he is gone.

That night, under the returned stars, Marrow sleeps against my chest and Kala's heartbeat moves slow against my back. I touch the hidden name behind my teeth.

My name.

Still mine.

But now there is a place where that name could be laid down, if the day ever comes when I choose to give it.

That is belonging.

Not being taken.

Having somewhere to be placed when all taking is done.

It is also debt.

I understand that before sleep. If I belong here, then this place can be hurt through me. If I am placed among their dead, then I can shame their living. Home is not a shelter only. It is a skin you can be cut through.

The next day the tribe eats too much fruit and becomes foolish.

The rains have shaken loose the yellow clusters that hang high over the east slope. They split when they strike stone, spilling sweet pulp that makes the small ones shriek and the adults pretend not to hurry. Even Gull-Marr takes three and sits with juice in his gray muzzle like a king too dignified to notice he is sticky.

Marrow steals one from under Torn-Ear's hand.

This is a mistake.

Torn-Ear roars and gives chase, slow enough to be insult and fast enough to be danger. Marrow bolts across the fire shelf, leaps over a sleeping child, ducks under Kala's elbow, and throws the fruit to me.

I catch it because my hands are foolish too.

Now Torn-Ear chases me.

The tribe loves this.

I run because there is no way not to run when an old scarred Vorth comes after you with juice on his chin and murder in his shoulders. I cut across the lower stones, over the root where I fell in the game, around the kept fire, up the slope toward the dead shelf and stop just before the forbidden line.

Torn-Ear cannot stop as cleanly.

He skids, arms windmilling, and sits down hard enough to shake dust from the roots.

For one breath I think I have insulted him beyond repair.

Then the old male laughs.

The sound rolls over the stones and breaks the tribe open.

Kala covers her face with one huge hand. The small ones throw peelings. Marrow falls over from delight. Even the seer makes a dry clicking sound that might be laughter if old bones remember how.

I stand holding the stolen fruit, breathless, alive, and not hunted.

For once, running has not ended in hiding.

Torn-Ear reaches up from the dust.

I give the fruit back.

He splits it with one thumbnail and gives me half.

Share.

It is a small gift.

It is not small to me.

Later, when the sun lowers and the fruit-sweet air turns sour with heat, Voss-Marr finds me beside the water jars.

He takes my half-cleaned fiber rope and studies the knots. His hands are strong and clever enough. That surprises me. I expected contempt to make him clumsy. It does not. He understands the snare after one turn, sees the branch-law under it, and tightens the knot better than I did.

Then he tosses it back.

His sound is short.

Useful.

Not praise.

Not insult.

Something worse. A truth neither of us can own.

For a moment I see the shape he might have had if I had not come from the green with a mark on my chest and a seer bowing to my blood. He would have been hard. Proud. Maybe cruel. But also capable. The tribe's future, trained into his bones from birth.

He has learned the knots because he should know all tools that might matter to his rule. He has watched me because a ruler watches anything that changes weight in the hand. There is no stupidity in him to comfort me.

I did not only find a home here.

I stepped into another son's promised place.

The thought sits badly in me.

That night, while Kala sleeps, I take the shell the Skell gave me and set it near the small dead one on the shelf, not among the gifts exactly, but close enough that morning light will touch it.

The shell is from the shallows. The small one never saw the shallows. Now it has something from there.

Maybe that is not Vorth law.

I know Kala sees it at dawn.

She says nothing. She only presses her forehead to mine, carefully, where no one else can make a lesson of it.

The lesson comes anyway.

At first light, the one-clouded female finds the shell.

Her grief-sound is small and sharp. Not for the dead child. For the wrongness beside it. She lifts the shell between two fingers as though it has slime on it and turns toward the fire shelf. Heads rise. Sleep falls away. Kala stands slowly.

The old female holds the shell where everyone can see.

Her words come fast, but enough of their shape is mine now.

Skell gift.

Dead child.

Wrong shelf.

The twisted Skell is nowhere to be seen, but its thin gift has crossed into Vorth morning and become argument.

I step forward.

Kala's hand catches my shoulder. Warning. Not command. She has learned that commands wake old wounds in me, and I have learned that warnings can still be love.

The one-clouded female points the shell at me. Her sound is not cruel. That makes it harder. She is not Voss-Marr, hungry for my fall. She is a keeper of rules because rules have kept something alive longer than any one grief. The dead shelf is not a place for every found token and soft wish. If I can lay Skell gifts beside Vorth dead, what else can cross? Skell bones? Mure signs? Human iron?

The tribe listens because she is right to ask.

Voss-Marr steps from the shadow beneath Gull-Marr's stone.

He says very little.

He does not need much.

His hand points to the high shelf, then down to the scree, then out toward the black channels. One line. Above. Below. Away. His meaning is clear even to the children.

If every border is love, no border remains.

I hate him for the thought because it reaches me.

I have spent my life being kept out. Now that a door has opened, I want all locked places proved cruel. But the Vorth are not wrong to have edges. The dead shelf is clean because hands guard it. The children sleep because someone decides who may come close. A home without borders is only meat waiting for teeth.

The shell feels suddenly heavy in the old female's hand.

Marrow slips beside me. He looks at the shell. Looks at me. Then, before anyone can stop him, he goes down on his knuckles and climbs the first step toward the dead shelf.

The tribe inhales.

Marrow stops before the forbidden line.

Not over it.

Before it.

He takes a fruit seed from his cheek, a disgusting secret he has apparently been saving for no good reason, and sets it on the lower step. Then he points to the shell, points to the same step, and gives a little click.

Not dead shelf.

Memory step.

The audacity of it nearly breaks my face open.

The one-clouded female stares.

Marrow waits with the solemn patience of a creature who has never respected solemnity in his life until this exact breath.

The old seer arrives late, leaning on her staff. She studies Marrow's seed on the lower step. Studies the shell in the old female's hand. Then she takes the shell and places it beside the seed.

Not among the dead.

Not thrown away.

Between.

Her staff taps once.

Allowed.

The word goes through the tribe in ripples. Some approve. Some do not. Voss-Marr's mouth tightens, but he cannot call the seer soft in front of the dead, not when she has kept their names longer than he has been alive.

Marrow climbs down and tries to look as if he planned every breath.

I touch his crooked hand.

He bumps my fingers away, embarrassed by gratitude.

By evening, three more small gifts rest on the memory step: a red leaf, a cracked nut, and one tiny fish bone placed there by Nomi for no reason she can explain. The one-clouded female removes none of them. She only sets them in a straighter line.

By evening, no one has moved them.

A law can begin that quietly: not by kindness, but by an argument surviving morning.
