# Chapter 23: The Baited Hunger

There are two roads in me now.

The up road leads to the high stones: Kala's arm, Marrow's quick breath, the little ones tangled in sleep, the dead faced to morning, the lip where I sit and the tribe turns when danger starts.

The down road leads to the camp: smoke, iron, Yara's words, Drevan Sool's mark, warm meat left at the edge of the green.

I walk both.

Each day they pull farther apart.

In the morning I hunt with Marrow and the scarred ones. We take river-pig from the shallows and burrow-runners from the soft flats. I judge quarrels over meat. I watch Voss-Marr test the air around my choices, looking for a place to push. I sit beside Kala while she combs burrs from a young one's hair with two careful claws.

By dusk I am listening for human feet.

Marrow walks the down road with me and hates every step.

At first he waits above the wet line. Then he refuses that too. He follows all the way to the roots and crouches beside me while I take the bait. He watches the camp, the trees, my hands, anything but the food. His whole body has become one long warning.

Come up.

Come away.

Not yours.

He is right.

Rightness does not move me.

The offerings change. Fish one night. Swine fat another. A gourd of watered wine that burns my nose and makes the drug beneath it hard to smell. Once, a folded scrap of cloth with the eye-and-thorn stamped in black, set beside the meat like an answer waiting to be unfolded.

I leave the cloth where it lies.

I eat the meat.

Each time the poison fails. Each time I tell myself I have learned something useful: how long the watchers wait, which man shifts too soon in the brush, how Drevan changes bait when the last one does not work, how much he knows of me and how much he guesses.

The worst truth is that I am learning him too.

He is patient, but not calm. The difference matters. Calm has no need under it. Drevan's patience is need held on a tight rein. The island is killing his men. The cages are filling slowly. The mark in the green has become the largest prize because it keeps refusing to become cargo.

That should please me.

It does.

It also draws me.

Need makes men honest in the wrong places.

Drevan starts cutting rations for the collared men first. I see it in the way they watch the cooking pots, not with hunger only, but with the careful blankness of those who know complaint has a price. Then he cuts the owners' wine. That makes them louder. Then he gives one sick owner his own cup and stands beside him until the man drinks.

The camp sees mercy.

I see command.

He spends kindness the way he spends rope. Measured. Public. Tightened around the neck that needs it most.

Yara still comes when she can. Watched now. Measured. Her ordinary words are for the men behind her. Her fingers speak lower in the mud.

The hand is coming.

The mark has called it.

Do not trust the gift.

Sometimes she teaches while warning. Two words hidden inside one meeting. Rope. Fever. Ship. Wait. Sell. Pit.

Pit is the word that stays.

She draws a circle in the mud, then points down.

Men around it.

Beasts inside.

Blood.

I have no word to ask why men would build a hole to watch blood. I understand anyway. The hold was a hole for owning. The pit is a hole for proving ownership to a crowd.

Yara sees me understand and looks away.

The camp is sickening.

I smell it before the men accept it. Fever in the shelters. Bad water in the gut. Rot creeping into cuts. Men who laughed at swarms now sleep under blankets in heat that should cook them. Owners step around owners fallen near the cage line. Collared men carry bodies to the shore at dawn and return without meeting anyone's eyes.

The green is eating them from inside while Drevan sets food out for me.

He is dying slower than he is hunting.

The scar I gave him has gone dark at one edge. Not enough to stop him. Enough to make him touch his face when he thinks no one sees. Enough to put a drag in his step by evening. He hides both badly from me and well from his men.

That should make me patient.

Instead it makes the ache worse.

The camp changes when sickness enters its bones.

Owned men become less invisible because owners need them too badly to pretend they are furniture. A collared cook tells a marked guard which water to boil and the guard obeys before remembering to scowl. A fevered owner calls for his mother in a tongue Yara does not translate. Two rowers steal salt from a barrel and share it with a boy who can barely stand.

Cruelty remains.

So does need.

That is the trouble with people. They do not stay one shape long enough for hatred to rest.

The boy with the salt is called Nerin.

I learn the sound because Yara says it when she thinks no watcher cares. He is younger than most of the collared men, old enough to carry buckets and young enough that sleep still changes his face into a child's. His collar has rubbed his neck raw. He hides the sore under a strip of cloth, but the cloth is never dry.

He sees me once.

Not clearly. I am only dark among roots, a place where eyes should not shine. Still, he pauses with his bucket hanging from both hands. His mouth opens. Not to shout. To breathe.

Then Yara says his name.

Nerin.

Soft. Warning. Kind.

He lowers his eyes and keeps walking.

The next day he is put to the fever tent.

That is not mercy either. The healthy free men will not carry fevered bodies if a collared boy can do it. Nerin brings water, changes cloths, holds bowls while men vomit black bile and curse him for seeing them weak. By dusk his own steps have begun to drift sideways. By night he is coughing into his shoulder, trying to hide the sound.

I want to hate him for being human.

Then I watch him tear his own salt cloth in half and tie the cleaner strip around the wrist of a sick rower who cannot stop shaking.

The lie speaks again.

Save him.

As if I can step from the green and lay hands on fever like a king lays hands on law. As if my wrong blood is answer enough for every wound men make and every sickness the island gives back.

I stay hidden.

That is the truth beneath every noble thought.

Nerin dies on the third morning.

Not loudly. The camp has no space left for every death to have its own sound. He folds beside the water rack with two empty gourds against his chest, and for a while men step around him because he is still useful as an obstacle to be avoided. Yara reaches him first. She kneels, touches his throat, and goes still.

The rower with the salt takes one step toward her.

A guard lifts a hook.

The rower stops.

Drevan comes when the camp has gathered enough to matter. He looks down at the boy. Fever has hollowed the captain's cheeks, and sweat runs under the edge of his black collar. For one breath I think he will order the body carried away as he orders crates moved, because that is the cheaper shape.

Instead he crouches.

Slowly. Carefully. As if the bend costs him more than he wants anyone to know.

He takes the two empty gourds from Nerin's arms and gives one to Yara, one to the rower who stopped when the hook lifted. Then he says words I only half know.

Work.

Water.

Remember.

He does not say free.

He does not say sorry.

He gives the dead boy a use, because use is the only holiness this camp understands.

Then he points to the fever tent and orders the rowers back to work.

Yara stays kneeling one breath too long.

Only one.

A guard notices. Drevan notices the guard noticing. Before the guard can speak, Drevan says Yara's camp name and tells her to carry the body to the shore.

It sounds like punishment.

It is also permission to grieve where the owners will not watch closely.

That is the knife of him. The part that cuts cleanest. He can make mercy wear the face of command so thoroughly that no one can tell which edge drew blood.

Yara lifts Nerin under the arms. He is light. Too light for a boy who carried water for men twice his size. The rower takes his feet when Drevan looks away at exactly the right time.

I follow through the trees until they reach the shore.

There, with surf worrying the mud, Yara presses Nerin's forehead once and says his name without the camp listening.

Nerin.

Then the rower and she push him into the tide.

No stone. No morning-facing shelf. No old hands keeping account. Only water taking a body because the camp cannot spare wood.

I think of the dead shelf at home and all the small bodies Kala keeps turned toward dawn.

The down road loses its shine.

Only for a while.

One evening I watch Yara carry broth to the caged Skell with the broken ribs. She waits until the nearest guard turns away, then slips a strip of clean cloth under the bars. The Skell snaps at her hand. She does not flinch until after the jaws close on air.

Then she laughs once, very quietly.

Not because it is funny.

Because the alternative is crying in front of men who would count the tears.

The Skell takes the cloth.

I see Marrow in that. The leavings helping leavings. The weak making small roads under the eyes of the strong.

The sight should send me back to the high stones with a cleaner heart.

Instead it knots the roads tighter.

If I wait, the green may take Yara. It may take the Mure who still lifts its head when she clicks softly through the bars. It may take the collared men who look like me and not like me. It may take the words before I learn enough to ask what I am.

This is the lie the hunger tells best:

Go down now, or lose the answer forever.

There is another lie under that one, softer and more dangerous.

Save them.

Not only Yara. Not only the caged Skell. The fevered boy who carries water. The rower with salt hidden in his palm. The owner calling for his mother in a voice made young by fear.

As if wanting to save a person means I know how.

As if the hand that keeps me from choosing home can call itself mercy and be clean.

At the high stones, the tribe senses the split.

Voss-Marr does more than sense it. He uses it.

He starts with distance. His young strong ones sleep farther from my fire. Then sound. A low note when I pass, almost laughter, almost warning. Then signs left where I will see them: rotten meat near the lip, a strip of human cloth taken from some old wreck and laid across a stone, a child's cage made from twigs and tied with vine.

Marrow finds that one before I do.

He crushes it under his foot until the vines are pulp.

Voss-Marr watches from across the shelf with his head tilted. Not challenging. Not yet. He wants me angry in the wrong direction. He wants the tribe to see me spend strength on shame.

I refuse him that.

But the center of the tribe leans away.

The discarded ones still hold near me. Torn-Ear. Stub. Grist. The scarred female. The ones who know that belonging can always be made conditional by those born nearer the center of the fire. They stay because I made room for them and because my fall would teach the tribe their room was never real.

Marrow stays for simpler reasons.

He is mine and I am his, though neither of us has a word clean enough for it.

Kala reads the worst of it.

One night, after I return with camp smoke in my hair and salt on my tongue, she takes me away from the others. Not far. Just to the edge of the dead shelf, where the small bodies face morning and the old stones remember every name the tribe can give them.

She sits heavily. The movement costs her. I see it now because I have learned her strength well enough to read its limits. Old wounds in the hip. Stiffness in the left hand when rain comes. Breath that catches after a hard climb and then smooths before anyone else notices.

She puts her hand on my chest.

The mark.

Then my face.

Then she points down toward the coast.

Where?

I know the sound. I know the shape of the question. I even have pieces of answer now.

Camp.

Cage.

Girl.

Mark.

Captain.

Hand.

Need.

None of them fit into the pain in her eye.

I put my hand over hers.

No answer comes.

The silence hurts her. I see it land. That is worse than any blow because it is pain I make while trying not to make pain.

She draws me against her side anyway.

Forgiveness can cut deeper than anger.

The seer comes after she leaves.

He moves slowly across the dead shelf, staff tapping once, then twice, then once again. He does not touch the mark at first. He touches my forehead. My mouth. The hollow at my throat. Last, the breastbone.

Then he gives the meaning-sound.

Not for the mark.

For me.

I go still.

He points to the coast. Gives the danger-sound. Points to my chest. Then to the high stones. Then he lays one old finger against my heart and presses until it hurts.

Here.

No farther.

He is telling me the mark is a road. He is telling me the road goes toward a hand. He is telling me I am mistaking direction for destiny.

I know.

Still the down road pulls.

That is the war.

Not tribe against men. Not green against iron. Not wild against camp.

The war is inside the breastbone.

One hunger wants the home that saved me. One wants the human road that may explain me. One wants Kala's arm. One wants Yara's word for the mark. One wants Marrow's shoulder at my side. One wants Drevan's welcome to be false in a way I can master.

Both roads are real.

Both roads are mine.

That is what makes choosing feel like cutting off a living part.

Near dawn, I wake in Kala's arm with my name behind my teeth.

My name.

Still hidden.

Still whole.

Still useless if I never give it to anyone who can answer.

Marrow is awake on my other side. He watches me with the flat yellow eye and does not pretend sleep. The little twist of dried moss he gave me days ago is tied around his curled wrist now, a reminder returned to himself.

"I know," I whisper.

He does not soften.

Good.

Someone should refuse to make this easy.

Then he reaches over and steals the last strip of meat from my hand.

He chews it slowly while staring at me.

It is so rude, so perfectly Marrow, that a laugh nearly climbs out of the wound in my chest. I stop it before the sound wakes Kala, but he sees it in my face. His high shoulder drops a little.

Not forgiveness.

Enough to remind me that the world still has room for teeth shown without threat.

At first light, before the tribe fully wakes, I go down again.

Marrow follows.

Of course he does.
