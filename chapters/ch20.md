# Chapter 20: The Slavers Camp

They have built where the dead built.

I know it before I reach the old wreck bar. The green is wrong there. Open. Hurt. Men have hacked the canopy back from the mud and dragged whole roots aside, leaving pale torn wood exposed to the hot air. The jungle closes every wound it can. Vines drop, moss creeps, leaves turn toward any break in the sky.

Only men hold a wound open and call it camp.

Marrow stops at the last drowned root.

His whole body says no.

The high shoulder drops. The curled hand works once against his chest and goes still. He looks toward the clearing, then up the hidden trail toward the high stones, then back at me with the patience of someone trying not to bare his teeth at a fool.

I should listen.

Instead I touch his shoulder and go on without him.

The smell reaches me first.

Tar. Smoke. Iron. Sweat. Piss. Sickness starting under all of it, still faint, the first sour thread in cloth not yet rotted through. Then the hold comes back in one breath: dark planks, bodies too close, water under the floor, a hand on the neck, the sound that meant come, be still, be owned.

I lie under cut brush and make my skin stop crawling.

The camp moves by rules.

That is the first truth I read once the fear settles enough to let my eyes work. Not chaos. Not just many men. Rules. Owners walk slowly, hands mostly empty, as if weight itself is beneath them. Owned men carry crates, poles, water skins, bundles of net, iron hooks wrapped in hide. If an owner turns his head, an owned man moves before the order is finished. If an owned man slows, a lash touches the air near his back. Not always the skin. Often near it.

That is worse in its way. A blow teaches pain. The near blow teaches the body to carry pain before it arrives.

I know that lesson.

There are more men here than Vorth on the high stones. Two score at least, perhaps three when I count the ones moving in and out of the boats. Some wear leather marked with the eye-and-thorn. Some have no mark but carry themselves like they belong to it. Some are collared. Some not collared but owned all the same, by posture, by hunger, by the way they look at food before looking at the man who gives permission to eat it.

Cages stand near the rear fires.

Not finished cages. Working cages. The kind men can take apart and carry over water, then build again wherever the world offers something worth stealing. In one, a Mure lies web-fingered and dull-eyed, its wet skin drying wrong in the open air. In another, a Skell breathes through broken ribs. Its claws scrape the wood in a rhythm that means pain, pain, pain, though no human head turns toward it.

The cages are clever.

That makes me hate them more.

Each corner comes apart with pins. Each bar has leather wrapped where hands must lift. Hooks fold flat for carrying, then bite into the ground when the cage is set down. Men have thought carefully about how to make a prison travel.

The whole camp is built the same way.

Not settled. Ready.

The tents can come down fast. The racks fold. The smoke pots hang from chains that can be lifted and carried still hot. Spears lie in bundles of five, points wrapped in greased cloth. Nets are dried on slanted frames so rot will not take them before the next shore. Even the fire pits have clay plates under the ash, as if men have learned to bring a little ground with them and leave less trace for rain to find.

At the edge of the clearing, an older owned man sits with a slate across his knees. A young owner stands over him and speaks numbers. The older man's fingers move charcoal over the slate, neat, fast, practiced.

Mure. One.

Skell. One.

Hides. Six bundles.

Teeth. Three strings.

Dead. The owner pauses before that count, then gives it with irritation, as if death is a leaky barrel. The older man marks it down.

Two men carry a body past the slate while he writes. The body is wrapped in sailcloth, feet bare and gray where they show. No one sings. No one touches forehead to forehead. The owner only looks at the slate and asks another question.

Cost.

The word has not come to me yet.

I know the shape.

The body becomes a mark in charcoal. The mark becomes part of whether the camp stays or goes. A life has been changed into a number that can argue with profit.

Men do not only cage bodies.

They cage meaning until it stops crying out.

I touch the scar at the back of my neck and remember a different cage, built of planks, rope, stink, and dark. The shape changes. The lesson does not.

Men are caged too.

That makes something in me go colder than fear.

The men inside the bars have the same hands as the men outside. Same mouths. Same eyes that understand what iron means. The difference is not shape. It is only who holds the key.

One collared boy carries water from the boil rack to the marked tents.

He is near my size.

That is what hooks me.

Not pity first. Recognition. His wrists are too thin for the bucket weight. His hair has been cut unevenly close to the skull, and the back of his neck is rubbed raw under the iron ring. When a guard speaks, the boy lowers his head before the words are done. The movement is so familiar that my own neck answers.

For one breath the trees between us vanish.

I could be there.

He could be me.

The difference is only that the sea broke one cage before it drowned me and this cage still knows its key.

He stumbles near the cook fire. Water slops over his bare feet. A guard lifts a hook, not to tear, only to remind. The boy flinches so hard the bucket almost falls. Before the hook lands, a rower with a gray beard steps close and catches the bucket's rim with two fingers.

Small help.

Dangerous help.

The guard sees. The hook lowers. The gray-bearded rower looks at the ground. The boy looks at the ground. Both stand very still while the guard decides how much order costs today.

At last the guard spits and walks on.

The boy breathes.

So do I.

Then he does a foolish, human, impossible act.

He smiles at the gray-bearded rower.

Only for half a heartbeat. Only one corner of the mouth. It is gone before anyone with power can punish it.

It still reaches me.

There. Not all men are the hand on the neck. Not all human mouths laugh at cages. Some catch a bucket. Some share one hidden breath in the middle of owning.

The wanting opens again, worse because it has kindness in it.

I hate the owners.

I want them to see me.

Both truths sit in the same skin, not fighting, which makes them worse.

Near the center of camp, men raise a pole and hang a strip of black cloth from it. The eye-and-thorn is stitched there in pale thread. The symbol lifts in the sea wind, opens, closes, opens again.

My hand finds the disc under my shirt.

The dead men carried that mark. The seer bowed to it. Now it flies above cages and orders.

The answer is here. I can feel it the way I feel rain before the leaves turn.

The captain comes into the cage yard near dusk.

The camp changes around him.

Not loudly. No call, no horn, no scrambling panic. A quieter change. Men straighten. Conversations shorten. Owners who were careless with their hands become careful. The owned go still in the middle of their work and then move faster. Even the caged Skell stops scraping for a breath, animal enough to read what men pretend is invisible.

He is not the largest man in camp. The largest carries an axe and stands near the boats with arms thick as young trees. The captain is lean, dark-haired, his beard cut short against a jaw too still for kindness. A scar cuts through one brow and misses the eye by a finger. He wears the eye-and-thorn on his chest, worked into boiled leather. The same mark hangs at his belt on a metal seal. Another is stamped onto the pommel of his knife.

Some men wear symbols.

This one uses them like teeth.

He stops before the Mure cage. He does not prod it. Does not laugh. That would be easier to hate. He crouches and studies the creature with the patience of a good hunter. Its gills flutter dry. Its webbed hands clutch at the cage floor.

The captain says something.

A smaller man answers too quickly and is slapped without the captain looking at him. Not hard. Hard enough. The answer comes again, corrected.

The captain listens with his eyes on the Mure. When the smaller man finishes, the captain lifts one finger.

Not enough.

The smaller man swallows, looks toward the water, toward the shade, toward Yara where she stands beside a stack of hides. He calls her over.

She comes without hurry. That is brave. Around men like him, hurry gives them a handle.

The captain speaks again. Yara listens to the smaller man's words, then crouches by the cage and makes the wet throat-click I heard from the brown channels. The Mure's black eyes open wider.

The camp falls a little quiet.

Yara asks another sound. The Mure answers with a thin whistle that makes the hairs rise along my arms.

She turns the whistle into human speech.

The captain smiles.

Not because he is pleased with her. Because a locked box has made a noise and he has found the key.

Power, I am learning, does not always need strength. Sometimes it is the shape of the space everyone leaves around one man.

The captain points to the Mure's throat, then to a slate in another man's hand. The man writes. Points to the drying skin. More writing. Points toward the water. An order follows. Two owned men lift the cage and carry it under shade.

He is not only taking.

He is studying what he takes.

That makes him more dangerous.

Before full dark, the camp becomes a market without buyers.

At least, that is how it looks first.

Men bring what the day has stolen from the island and lay it in lines near the black banner. Hesh teeth not from my Hesh, smaller and yellow with age. Skell claws cut from dead hands. Bundles of blue reed that make fever smoke when burned. A netted sack of shellfish clicking against one another. Three jars of dark mud sealed with wax and marked in pale chalk. The older owned man writes each line while the young owner with him names value in a bored voice.

The bored voice is a lie.

I see his eyes linger on the jars.

So does the captain.

He asks for one to be opened. The young owner hesitates because every sealed jar is a promise to someone far away, and promises seem to matter when coin hangs from them. The captain waits. That is all. The young owner breaks the wax.

The smell reaches even my blind.

Sharp. Mineral. Blood under rain.

The captain dips a twig into the mud and touches it to a scrap of pale hide. The hide blackens where the mud sits, not burned, changed. Men lean closer. Even two collared rowers stare until a guard cuffs one back to work.

The captain smiles the way he smiled at the Skell cage.

Not joy.

Use.

The island is not only meat to him. Not only slaves or hides or monsters dragged screaming into cages. It is medicine, poison, dye, story, weapon, charm. Every root and claw and fever has a price somewhere beyond the water. Every price is a road bringing more boats.

I had thought the camp came to steal bodies.

It came to read the island in the language of sale.

That thought makes the high stones feel suddenly smaller behind me. A king can guard a path, a spring, a sleeping hollow. How does a king guard against hunger that lives across the sea and sends men with slates to count the world apart?

The young owner reaches for the opened jar after the captain turns away.

A woman catches his wrist.

She is broad through the shoulders and gray at the temples, with a burn scar roping one side of her neck. Not collared. Not marked with the eye-and-thorn. Her belt carries needles, glass vials, and a short curved blade kept cleaner than any spear in camp. I have seen her dress wounds, cut rot from a man's foot, pour bitter drops into a caged mouth. The owners speak to her with impatience and caution both.

She says something to the young owner.

He laughs.

She presses his hand toward the open jar until one fingertip hovers above the dark mud.

The laugh dies.

She lets him go.

He steps back with anger on his face and fear in his throat.

The woman seals the jar with fresh wax, then marks the top with a symbol of her own. Not eye-and-thorn. Three short cuts crossing a circle. A warning, maybe. Or a claim.

Another power in camp.

Not soft. Not kind. Necessary.

The captain sees the mark when he returns. For a breath they look at each other over the jar. The camp bends around their silence. He could overrule her. She could refuse him in some way that would cost more than pride. Neither chooses the open fight.

He nods once.

She nods back, smaller.

I store her away in my mind beside the captain, Yara, the slate man, the boy with the raw collar. The camp is not one mouth. It is many mouths learning to chew in rhythm.

That is worse.

He walks the camp after that, and the camp becomes a map of his wanting.

At the net racks he tests knots with one hand and rejects three without raising his voice. The net-master's face goes pale. At the boil rack he smells the water, listens to a report, and sends one barrel to the collared men instead of the marked tent waiting for it. The owner beside him protests.

The captain turns his head.

The protest dies.

At the slate table he reads the charcoal marks. His finger stops on the line where the wrapped body became a number. He asks a question. The older owned man answers with his eyes lowered. The captain listens, then takes the charcoal himself and changes one mark.

Not softer.

More exact.

He has the patience to care whether cruelty is counted correctly.

That makes my skin crawl.

A stupid brutal man can be tricked by his own hunger. This one trims hunger into tools.

He asks after rope. After medicine. After the cages. After the waterline. After the paths cut into the green. Every answer becomes movement. Two men sent to gather bitter leaves. Three to raise the rack higher. One guard replaced because he keeps slapping at insects instead of watching the trees.

Then he stops beside the collared boy with the raw neck.

The boy freezes with both hands around an empty bucket.

The captain crouches, takes the bucket, and looks at the rubbed skin under the collar. He says something to a woman with needles at her belt. She brings cloth and grease. The captain points. She wraps the wound while the boy stares at nothing.

Kindness, the ache in me whispers.

No.

Maintenance.

Both may use the same hand.

The boy will carry more water if the collar does not eat him too quickly. The captain knows it. The boy knows it. I know it, and still the sight of human fingers tending human pain goes into me where I hate to be touched.

At the Skell cage he bends close enough to lose fingers if the creature had strength left. It snaps anyway. He smiles then, and the smile has no heat in it.

One day, I think, he will look at me that way.

The thought should send me back to the high stones.

It keeps me where I am.

Then I see her.

She is not owner and not fully owned.

At first I read only the wrongness of her position. She moves among owners without belonging to them. She carries bundles, but no chain links her to the collared men. She eats from a wooden bowl near the marked tents, not with the caged and not at the captain's fire. When an owner speaks to a prisoner, she steps forward and gives the meaning back in another tongue. When the caged Mure clicks wetly through its drying throat, she listens, flinches, and turns the sound into human words.

She carries meaning across.

My breath catches.

No Vorth can do that. Kala can hold me. Marrow can read me better than any living creature. The seer can touch blood and mark and hear old echoes. But this girl stands between two roads of speech and moves sense from one to the other.

She has the bridge I lost.

She is near my age, maybe, though hunger and labor make age a poor map. Her hair is bound back in wet coils. Rope marks scar one shoulder. A bruise darkens the side of her face, old enough to yellow at the edge. She keeps her eyes lowered around the captain, but when his back turns, the eyes lift and count everything.

Not broken.

Careful.

There is a difference.

The captain calls her with two fingers.

She goes at once.

He speaks without looking down. She listens. The stillness in her face is practiced, but her hand betrays her, closing once in the cloth at her side. Then he gestures toward the cages and the green beyond them. She nods.

The words are closed to me.

I know the shape.

He is asking about what the island says.

About what has been seen.

About us.

Yara answers carefully. The words are closed, but a map is plain when a hand makes one. Her fingers sketch shore, mud, high stone. She leaves spaces. Twice the captain's gaze sharpens at a place she does not fill. Twice she fills it with something else before he can set his finger there.

She is lying for someone.

Maybe herself. Maybe the caged. Maybe the monster in the trees she only half believes in.

The captain knows she is trimming the truth. He lets her.

That is worse than catching her. A patient hunter lets the trail run until it leads home.

My mouth goes dry.

The captain turns his head toward the trees.

Again, not directly to where I lie. A little left. A little high. Reading cover, reading distance, reading where eyes might hide. I sink until mud touches my lip. The insects crawl over my neck. I stay still.

The girl looks too.

For one impossible breath I think her eyes find me.

They pass on.

Near dark, the camp eats.

Owners first. Then the uncollared workers. Then the collared. The caged get bowls shoved between bars, thin broth and scraps not fit for dogs. The Mure does not understand the bowl until the girl crouches and clicks softly in its tongue. It lifts its head, weak and furious, and drinks.

The captain watches her do it.

Not kindly. Never that. With calculation.

He knows what she is worth.

So do I.

Low tide pulls the mud flats silver under the moon. The camp settles into watches. Fires burn low. Fever-swarms gather around the men and sing in black knots above the lamps. One owner slaps at his neck and curses. Another laughs at him.

The island is patient.

The girl slips away when the watch changes.

She carries a water skin and a small knife, both openly enough that no one stops her. I follow from above as she walks down the spit to the edge of the mangroves. She stops where dry mud darkens to wet, just short of the first roots.

Her shoulders are raised around her ears.

The posture of a body waiting for a blow.

I name her in my head because I will not call her owned.

Yara.

Maybe it is not her true name. Maybe it is only the sound I make for a bridge still beyond me. But it is better than girl. Better than owned. Better than translator, which is a tool's name.

Yara looks into the dark.

When she speaks, the voice is not command. Not the closing sound. It is soft and careful, a hand set palm-up on the ground.

I know none of the words.

I know the offering.

She points to the shell near her foot and says a sound. Waits. Points again. Says it again. Then the tide. Then the mud. Then herself, hand pressed to her chest.

The sound she gives for herself is not Yara.

I keep my name for her anyway.

I move one hanging frond.

She goes still.

Not like prey. Like someone whose guess has become a door.

Her eyes find the frond. Then the root shadow above it. Then the deeper dark where I hold my breath. She should run. She should call the watch. She should be afraid of the monster in the trees because that is the sensible shape of the moment.

Instead she points to the shell again and repeats the word.

Teaching.

The ache in me opens so hard I nearly answer with the only word that matters.

My name.

I bite it back.

She keeps going. Shell. Tide. Mud. Water. Fire. Camp. Captain.

The last word is different. Harder. Her mouth tightens around it.

Captain.

Then she points back toward the camp and closes her fist slowly.

Hand.

No. More than hand.

Ownership.

Warning.

Both.

She looks at the closed fist for a moment too long.

Then, with two fingers, she opens it again.

Not free. Not safe. Only possible.

The gesture is so small a watchman would see only a girl flexing a cramped hand. I see a door crack in darkness.

The road between us is only a few stones long, but it is a road.

I take every sound she gives and hide it behind my teeth.

A branch cracks far behind me.

Yara's face changes at once. The soft teaching vanishes. The still mask drops over her again. She lifts the water skin as if she came only to fill it from a bad pool no one else wanted, then turns back toward camp.

I wait until she is gone before moving.

Marrow is up the slope exactly where I left him.

No. Not where I left him. Closer. He came down after me and stopped where his courage and his hatred of the camp met and could go no farther. His eye moves from my face to the place where Yara stood, then toward the camp fires.

He does not need words.

Your shape is not your kin.

I know.

I cannot tell.

Both are true, and neither helps.

We climb back through the mangroves in silence. The high stones are dark by the time we reach them. Kala sleeps near the young ones, but her head lifts before my foot touches the shelf. She smells the camp on me again. Smoke. Iron. Human hunger.

This time she does not reach for me at once.

She looks first at Marrow.

He goes to her and presses his head against her arm like a child, though he is no child by Vorth counting. Her hand closes over his high shoulder. Only then does she look at me.

I have stood before Hesh with blood on my hands. Before Gull-Marr with the tribe watching. Before a snake large enough to swallow a boy.

None of that made me feel as small as Kala's silence.

I go to my place near the fire and lie down with Yara's words warm in my mouth, the captain's mark burning against my chest, and Marrow's warning laid beside both.

Above us, smoke from the slavers' camp stains the stars.
