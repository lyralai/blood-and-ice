# Chapter 19: Smoke on the Water

For three days after Gull-Marr yields, the island gives me peace.

Not safety. The green never gives that. Safety is a word men invented for places they have not read closely enough. The island still has teeth under leaves, fever in the mud, water that looks sweet until the gut cramps around it. But the tribe turns when I move. The young ones watch me openly now. The scarred and crooked ones come nearer the fire. Even the strong males who used to look through me have learned to look at the ground first and then at my face.

I sit on the lip where Gull-Marr sat.

It is only stone. Wet in the morning, warm at noon, slick with old ash near the fire. But a place can be more than itself when enough bodies agree to it. On that stone, the tribe's anger comes to me first. Its hunger. Its fear. Its small disputes over meat and sleeping space and which young hunter took more marrow than he earned. I have no words for most of it. I have eyes, hands, stillness, and the lessons the hold beat into me.

Men taught me power by putting a hand on the back of my neck.

The Vorth teach me a different shape. A look can turn a quarrel aside. A piece of meat set down in front of the weakest can shame a stronger hand away from it. A silence can weigh more than a blow if everyone knows the blow is waiting behind it.

I am learning.

Learning costs more than winning.

The Hesh head draws flies for two days before we burn what is left of it. Its teeth become blades and charms. Its hide becomes strips around wrists, spears, carrying poles. The white crab-spiders that lived behind its jaw scatter into the stones, and small ones chase them with shrieks until Kala's look puts every child back on a safer ledge. The tribe laughs at that. Even the torn-lipped old she laughs, a dry sound like bark cracking.

I laugh too, late and quiet.

The sound feels strange in my mouth.

Kala watches me learn with one dark eye and one hand always near enough to close around my shoulder. She does not crowd me. That is the kindness of it. She lets me sit on the lip alone while making the whole tribe know I am not alone at all.

Marrow understands the new order before I do. He has spent his life reading where not to stand. Now he finds the places beside me where no one can push him off without pushing against my shadow too. He does not become proud. Pride is for creatures born straight enough to expect room. Marrow becomes exact. He carries messages I cannot say. He brings the scarred ones forward. He sends bold young males away with one flat look from his yellow eye, as if my crown has given his crooked body a longer reach.

It warms a place I keep hidden.

Sometimes, when no quarrel needs me and no hunger has teeth in it, he climbs the lip and sits with his shoulder against my knee. He pretends to watch the green. Mostly he watches me.

If I sit too straight, he copies me until his crooked back complains and he makes a grave show of dying from kingship. If a young hunter struts below, Marrow struts along the lip on three limbs with such terrible majesty that two small ones fall over laughing.

The first time he does it, I try to look stern.

I fail.

For three days, I am almost young.

On the second evening, the tribe brings me a peace that is not peaceful at all.

It starts with water.

The upper spring runs thin after three rainless days. The lower pool is fuller, but fever blooms there in green threads, and the Hesh's old path still cuts too close to the bank. The mothers want the clean spring for the small ones. The hunters want it for washing wounds before rot finds them. The old want it because old mouths dry quickly and pride dries faster.

Everyone is right.

That is what makes the shouting rise.

Voss-Marr stands with Red-Shoulder and the blind-eyed female near the spring path. He says little. He has learned how little he needs to say. His presence gives shape to the claim: hunters bring meat, meat keeps children alive, therefore the water that keeps hunters whole is not stolen when hunters take it first.

Kala stands with the mothers.

She says nothing either.

Her silence has a different weight. It remembers fever. It remembers me burning under her hand. It remembers every small body she has seen carried to the dead shelf because water looked clean and was not.

The tribe looks to the lip.

I want to run into the green and fight another Hesh.

A mouth with teeth is easier than thirsty people.

Marrow nudges my ankle. He has a river snail in one hand and the expression of someone prepared to solve kingship by eating during it. Then he points with his chin toward the old carrying poles left from the Hesh hide.

Poles.

Skin.

Hands.

The answer is not who owns the water.

The answer is labor.

I climb down from the lip and walk to the spring. No one moves as I take the first bowl. I carry it to the smallest coughing child and set it in his mother's hands. Then I take the second bowl to Bent-Jaw, whose gums are cracked and bleeding. The third goes to a hunter with a red wound at his thigh. After that, I point to the Hesh hide stretched near the fire, then to the carrying poles, then to the lower pool, then to the flat stones where fire can boil water in shell bowls.

No one understands at once.

Marrow does. He always does when there is work in a plan. He scuttles to the hide and drags at it with his teeth until Torn-Ear snorts and rises to help. Soon the hide is slung between two poles, a sagging skin basin. Two hunters carry it to the lower pool with Voss-Marr watching, too proud not to join once Red-Shoulder does. The lower water comes back in the hide. We heat stones in the fire and drop them into shell bowls until steam lifts. The first bowl goes to a child. The second to the wounded hunter. The third to Bent-Jaw.

By the time moonrise silvers the lip, the spring is not empty, the hunters have water for wounds, and the old have stopped baring gums at the young.

No one roars.

No one bows.

I am beginning to understand that good rule often sounds like people no longer shouting.

Then Grist creeps close with a bundle of bitter leaves in his claws.

Every Vorth eye turns.

He freezes. Rain-thin ribs show under his hide. The bundle shakes once. Skell eat rot and bone and soft dead meat. Skell steal from middens. Skell do not walk into Vorth firelight with medicine.

Grist drops the leaves and backs away.

The blind-eyed female hisses. Red-Shoulder starts forward.

I pick up the leaves first.

I know the smell. Kala used them when fever took me, crushed into mud and pressed behind the knees to pull heat. The Skell know them too. Of course they do. A body thrown to the edges learns every bitter mercy the center ignores.

I carry the leaves to the wounded hunter and set them beside his thigh.

There.

Allowed.

The word has no clean Vorth sound yet, but the gesture holds. Torn-Ear grunts. Kala watches Grist with narrowed eyes, then makes room at the outer fire. Not much. A handspan. A kingdom can begin with less.

Grist does not come closer.

Not that night.

But he does not leave either.

Later, after the shouting has become eating and the eating has become the sleepy murmur of safe bellies, Marrow performs Gull-Marr for the children.

He climbs the lip, puffs his narrow chest, and lowers his brows until his whole face becomes a storm. Then he takes one grand step, slips on ash he placed there himself, and falls backward with all four limbs in the air.

The young ones explode.

Even Kala makes a coughing sound that might be laughter if laughter wore a mask. Torn-Ear turns away too late to hide his teeth. The blind-eyed female does not laugh, but her mouth softens at one corner before she remembers herself.

I should stop Marrow. Gull-Marr is alive somewhere in the green, and mockery has teeth that travel.

But Marrow rolls up, sees me looking stern, and immediately becomes me instead.

He sits on the lip with one solemn hand over his heart, stares at an imaginary dispute, and gives the smallest possible nod, as if solving thirst, hunger, succession, and the turning of the moon with the movement of one chin.

Then he ruins it by scratching under his arm.

I laugh so hard my hurt shoulder pulls fire.

For a while the tribe laughs with me.

Not at me.

With me.

The difference is a shore I never thought to reach.

After, when the fire lowers and the small ones sleep in warm knots, Kala brings me a cracked shell cup of boiled lower water. She drinks first, to show the tribe it is clean enough. Then she hands it to me.

No speech. No ceremony.

Only water made safe by many hands and given back through hers.

I drink. It tastes of smoke, stone, and bitter leaf. It tastes like a law that has survived its first night.

Marrow steals the last mouthful and looks deeply pleased with the theft.

I let him have it.

On the fourth morning I climb the seaward stack to read the tide for river-pig.

Marrow comes with me because he comes when I go, high shoulder raised against the wind, curled hand tucked near his chest. Below us the mangrove mouth opens into pale water. The low tide has drawn the flats bare in long shining ribs. White birds stab at pools. Farther out, haze blurs the line between sea and sky until the world looks unfinished.

I almost miss the shapes because I am happy.

That is the danger of happiness. It puts softness over the eye.

Marrow sees first. His body goes still beside me, so completely that the air seems to move around him and not through him. The curled hand opens once, closes, opens again.

I follow his gaze.

Far out on the pale water, long dark shapes hold against the swell.

Not logs. Logs yield. Not tide-beasts. Tide-beasts roll with water and vanish under it. These cut through the sea with intention. Their noses point toward the mangrove mouth. Small upright forms bend along their sides, pull together, rise together, pull again.

The word comes up whole from the drowned place inside me.

Boats.

My throat closes around it.

For a moment the island falls away. The stack, the birds, Marrow's quick breath, the lip waiting behind me, Kala's arm. All of it goes thin as mist, and I am back under wet planks with bodies pressed on every side and a hand teaching my neck the shape of ownership.

Boats mean hands.

Hands mean the hold.

Marrow makes a low sound. Not fear exactly. Something wiser than fear. He looks from the water to me, then back toward the high stones.

Go back, his body says.

Wake them.

Hide.

He is right.

I should go to Kala. Move the tribe into the broken ridges above the fever pockets. Set watchers on the rotten channels. Let the green do what the green does to men who cross it without permission. I know the island's tolls. Bad water. Swarm fever. Mud that drinks a boot and keeps the leg. The Mure under warm shallows. The Skell in the scree. Poison vines fine as hair.

The men in the boats know none of it.

They are already dying.

I know that before they reach shore, and still I stay turned toward the water.

That shame is its own wound.

If the boats had carried only hooks and teeth, I could have been wise. I could have run to the high stones and worn my crown like a duty. But they carry voices. They carry the old shape of my hands. They carry the chance, thin and poisoned as a thorn, that somewhere below there is a person who can look at my face and know the word human means me too.

The first boat grounds on the bone-spit where I found the wreck. Men jump into the shallows and haul the hull forward. Their voices carry in broken pieces across the water. Human voices. Not Vorth grunts, not Mure clicks, not Skell chittering from stone.

Words.

Thrown from mouth to mouth and caught easily.

The sound goes into me like water into cracked earth.

Marrow touches my arm with the back of his curled hand.

I look at him.

His face is hard with warning. He has never seen a boat. He does not need to. He reads me reading it, and he knows the danger is not only below.

I start down the stack.

Not toward the high stones.

Toward the coast.

Marrow bars my way for one breath. Small body, high shoulder, teeth showing. The sight of it should shame me. Maybe it does. I step around him anyway, and after a moment he follows, furious and faithful.

We go low through the wet rocks and root cages to the old lookout above the spit. The place smells of salt, rot, and dead history. The wreck lies half-swallowed by mangrove roots below, its ribs black in the mud. Men are building beside it as if bones are not warnings.

There are more of them than I first counted. Three boats. Crates. Hide bundles. Spears with hooks. Nets. Iron cages in pieces, unloaded and dragged above the tide line. Owners and owned, easy to tell once you know the trick. Owners carry nothing heavy. Owned men carry until their shoulders shake. Owners point. Owned men bend. The camp is still only a wound in the green, but already the wound has rules.

I hate them.

I lean toward them.

Both truths live in me without touching.

A man near the center gives orders without raising his voice. He is not the biggest. The biggest men are usually tools for smaller men with better eyes. This one stands still while everyone else moves around him. Even at this distance I can read that. Power has a scent even when the wind is wrong.

He turns once, and sunlight catches worked leather at his chest.

The eye-and-thorn.

My hand closes around the disc hidden under my shirt.

The same mark lay with the dead men. The same mark made the seer bow his old head and touch my blood like a question. Now living men wear it openly while they unload cages onto my shore.

Mine, the mark seems to say against my palm.

Theirs, the camp says back.

Both cannot be true.

Marrow presses his head hard into my shoulder. Not comfort. Command. He points uphill with his chin, and there is no mistaking him now.

Leave.

I should. I know every reason. Kala is behind me. The tribe behind her. The lip. The first home I ever earned instead of stole. I have meat, fire, standing, a place among the dead, and a crooked shadow at my side who would follow me into a pit if I let him.

I was full.

Then human voices come out of the boats and prove there was another emptiness under the full one.

The ache disgusts me. It is not noble. Not wise. It does not care that men put me in the hold. It does not care that men branded sound into my bones. It only knows that those figures below have my shape. Their hands work like mine. Their mouths hold the road I lost. Somewhere among them may be the word for the mark, the name of what I am, the answer to why the island failed to kill me when it killed every other human who came before.

Behind my broken teeth, my true name waits.

My name.

I could throw it down to them.

I could hear a human mouth catch it.

The want is so sharp I almost make a sound.

Below, a caged Mure is dragged from the second boat. Its webbed hands flex weakly in the ropes. Men laugh when it hisses. One prods it with a hook to make it move faster. The laugh cleans the want out of me for a breath.

Only for a breath.

That is the ugly truth. Cruelty should make the whole wanting die. It does not. It only teaches the wanting to hide from my judgment. I can hate the laugh and still lean toward the mouths that made it because those mouths know roads mine forgot.

Not kin, Marrow's body says beside me.

He is right again.

The captain turns toward the jungle as if he has heard us thinking.

We flatten into the wet green. My cheek presses mud. Marrow's breath stops. For three long counts the man with the eye-and-thorn studies the line of trees above the spit.

He cannot see us.

Still, I know when a hunter is reading cover.

At last he looks away.

I breathe.

Marrow does not move until I do. When I start downhill for a closer line, he catches my wrist. His grip is small and hard.

No.

I pull free.

The hurt in his face lands worse than teeth.

I tell myself I only need to read the camp. A king must know what comes to his shore. A hunter must know the trap before the trap closes. All true.

Truth is useful that way. It makes a good roof for shame.

By dusk the men have fires burning where no human fire should hold. Smoke climbs into the hot still air and stands over the water like a dark thread stitched between the camp and the sky.

Smoke on the water.

A sign any creature can read.

We came. We burn. We are not afraid.

The island hears.

So do I.

When the camp settles, Marrow finally stops trying to pull me home. He lies beside me with his curled hand over his eyes, as if he cannot bear to watch me watching them.

I stay until the first fever-swarms rise from the mud and pour toward the human fires.

Only then do I turn back.

Kala is waiting at the high stones.

She smells the smoke on me before I reach the fire. Her nostrils widen. Her hand closes once in the air, empty, then opens. She looks past me for Marrow. He comes out of the dark at my heel, shoulders tight with anger and fear.

Kala touches his head first.

Then mine.

The order of it wounds me because it is fair.

The seer comes from the dead shelf and lays two fingers on the disc beneath my shirt. He gives the danger-sound toward the coast, low and sharp enough that the nearest young ones turn.

Danger.

There.

Yes, I think.

And something else.

But I have no words for the else. Not in Vorth. Not in the broken human scraps left to me. So I say nothing while the smoke from the water clings to my skin, and the human voices keep moving inside my head long after the camp has gone quiet.
