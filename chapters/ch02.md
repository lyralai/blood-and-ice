# Chapter 2: The Shore

Heat wakes me.

Not sunlight. Not fire. Heat with weight. It lies over my face and chest like a wet hand, presses the breath back into me, and fills my mouth with the taste of rot.

For a while I lie still.

Mud holds my back. Roots press through my shirt. Something small crawls over my wrist, stops at the blood there, and begins to feed.

I open my eyes.

Green.

It hangs above me in layers so thick the sky is only a white glare behind leaves. Vines cross the branches like ropes on a ship. Water drips everywhere, slow and patient, though no rain is falling. The whole place breathes. I can hear it. Insects whining. Leaves ticking. Far off, something coughs once and goes quiet.

I remember a deck.

I remember hands.

I remember the sound men make when they do not care whether a boy lives, only whether he can still be sold.

Then the memories break apart. The sea has taken some. Pain has taken the rest.

I make an inventory because inventory keeps fear from owning the whole room.

Right hand: slow, but it closes.

Left hand: better.

Teeth: three gone.

Mouth: blood.

Ribs: bruised, maybe cracked.

Knife: none.

That matters most.

I roll onto my side and vomit seawater into the mud. It comes up bitter and warm. Ants find it before I am done spitting. They come in a black thread from under a leaf, quick and certain, and take the red from the mud as if the jungle has sent them to collect what I owe.

I push myself to my knees.

The world swings. I wait it out with one hand locked around a root. The root is slick as an eel and strong as bone. When my sight clears, I see water below me.

Not sea. A brown channel, still on top, moving underneath. Roots reach down into it like fingers. Something slides just below the surface and leaves a line that opens and closes without a ripple.

I keep away from it.

Thirst scrapes the back of my throat. It wants the brown water. My empty body makes stupid arguments. I have learned to listen and then decide whether listening will kill me.

Rainwater first. Clear water if I can find it. Any fruit that does not burn the tongue. No standing brown water unless dying has already become the only other choice.

Those are not thoughts in words. Not at first. They are old lessons stored in scars and hunger.

I stand.

The heat hits harder upright. It fills my shirt, runs down my spine, gathers under my arms. I breathe through my mouth and taste flowers, mold, and something dead nearby. Too much life packed too close becomes another kind of death.

I climb away from the channel, one hand on roots, one foot testing before weight. The mud wants my boots. Twice it takes one and I have to drag it free with both hands. A fly as long as my thumb lands on my cheek. I slap it and my palm comes away red. Mine or its. I cannot tell.

The jungle watches without eyes.

I find the first body under a fan of broad leaves.

He lies face-down, half in the mud, one arm twisted under him. I know him. Not his name. The ship had not left us names. Names are wasted on cargo. But I know the shape of his shoulders and the scar on the back of his neck where a collar rubbed him raw.

He was older than me. Stronger. He had cursed the slavers once in a language I did not know and lost two teeth for it.

Now the jungle has him.

White mushrooms grow from the corner of his mouth.

One hand is closed around mud. I pry the fingers open because hunger makes even the dead into questions. There is nothing there but a bit of leaf crushed to pulp. His nails are packed black. He died holding the island, and the island did not care enough to hold him back.

I know better than to speak over him. The hold taught silence around the dead. A man dies, the slaver kicks his feet apart, and everyone else looks small so death will not be counted as laziness. Still, I find myself wanting a word.

Not a prayer. I have no god that ever answered.

Only his name.

I have no name for him.

That feels like another theft.

I crouch beside him and look for a knife. Belt first. Boots. Hands. The places men hide small blades and charms when they are afraid.

Nothing.

His pouch has a strip of dried meat wrapped in cloth so filthy I almost miss it. My mouth fills before my hand closes.

I eat too fast.

Halfway through the second bite, my stomach heaves. I force the meat down with both hands over my mouth because losing food is a sin the body does not forgive.

When the meat is gone, I search him again. Still no knife. No flint. No cord worth taking. Only a copper token on a string around his neck, green with sweat and salt.

I take it because the dead do not need luck and I have none.

Something calls above me.

Not a bird. Too low. Too wet.

I freeze.

The sound comes again from the trees, a soft clacking that moves without moving. I look up and see nothing but leaves, shadow, and ropes of vine. Then one vine opens an eye.

Not a vine.

A snake, thick as my thigh, patterned green and black. Its head rests on a branch above the dead man's back. Its tongue tastes the air between us.

I step back very slowly.

The snake does not strike. It watches the body. It was here first, maybe. Or it was waiting for the jungle to finish softening him.

I leave it the man.

Mercy has nothing to do with it. A boy with no knife does not argue over meat with a snake that size.

By the time I find water, my tongue feels too large for my mouth.

A thread runs down stone where a tree has cracked the rock open, gathering in a cup no wider than my hand.

Clear.

I smell it. Touch it to my lip. Wait.

No burn. No bitter oil. No swamp skin.

I drink.

The first swallow hurts. The second becomes everything. I drink until the cup is empty, wait, drink again until my stomach cramps.

Only then do I sit back.

The rock is warm behind me. The jungle steams. My shirt clings to the cuts on my back.

I should be shaking by now.

I have seen swamp air take men apart: glassy eyes, cold sweat, hands shaking too hard to hold a spoon. Men stronger than me have died before morning.

But my hands are steady.

The bites on my arms swell, fade, then leave a deep itch under the skin. The cuts from the wreck sting, then tighten. The rot air goes into me and comes out again, and whatever poison rides in it does not find a place to sit. By the time my hands stop trembling, hunger has come back sharp enough to shame me.

That frightens me more than fever would have.

Fever I understand.

This has no shape I know.

I touch the copper token at my neck. It is warm from my hand, stamped with a mark I cannot read. The dead boy kept it hidden under his shirt. Maybe it was his god. Maybe his mother. Maybe only a scrap he could call his own.

The mark might be a two-headed bird, a winged cup, some king's joke. I turn it three ways and it becomes three lies. Maybe the token only reminded him he had once been more than a price.

I tie it under my shirt anyway.

It knocks once against my chest when I move. A small sound. Almost company.

I have one piece like that.

A name.

I hold it where the slavers never looked. Behind the teeth they broke. Under the tongue they told me to keep still.

My name.

My mother gave it to me. Her face is blurred now, and that scares me in a deep place I rarely touch. I remember warmth. I remember a low voice. I remember being lifted once, before hands meant pain.

But I remember the name.

The slavers had another sound for me. A short ugly sound made in the back of the throat. It meant come here. It meant property. It meant a hand on my neck and a boot near my ribs.

I never let it become my name.

My name stays hidden. Small, hard, alive.

I keep it silent. The air here feels hungry enough to steal it.

The seep gives me enough water to think farther than my tongue.

I climb above it because height is the only wealth I have. The rock rises through the green in a broken spine, slick in the shade and hot where the sun reaches. I go slowly. My body complains with every pull. At the top, the trees thin for ten steps and the island opens.

Sea on one side.

Jungle on the other.

Between them, channels cut the land into strips of mud and root and pale stone. The water shines brown under the trees and blue where it finds the sky. Farther out, beyond the reef, the sea breaks white over black teeth. Wreckage turns in the surf there. Not much. A plank. A barrel. A torn length of sailcloth that lifts and falls like a hand too tired to wave.

The sight of open water should mean escape.

It does not.

The reef chews the sea to foam. Between the black rocks, currents pull at each other until the water knots. Even from the height I can see places where the surface dimples and spins without wind. A man with oars might die there. A boy with no boat would be gone before he had time to regret hope.

I look anyway.

No sail.

No smoke.

No human voice carrying across the water.

The island answers with a bird landing on the stone beside me.

It is no bigger than my fist, green-backed, red-throated. It studies me with a black bead of an eye, a silver beetle kicking in its beak.

"You have more than I do," I whisper.

The bird tilts its head as if weighing the fairness of this claim. Then it swallows the beetle whole and drops a white streak of filth onto the rock beside my knee.

For half a breath I only stare.

Then I laugh.

The laugh comes easier than the first one. Still ugly. Still cracked. But it belongs to me. The bird decides my joy is suspicious and flits away.

I wipe the mess with leaves and keep one eye on the sky. Small disrespect is safer than large mercy. I almost thank the bird for reminding me of that.

On the far side of the rock, I find shells caught in a rain hollow. Not sea shells. These are thin as fingernails and blue inside, each one no wider than a coin. They lie in a crescent on dry stone with no water near them. Something brought them here to crack and eat.

I crouch.

The shells are split at the same place, neat as if cut by a tiny knife. No bird beak made those lines. No crab claw either. I pick one up and see a black thorn caught in the crack.

Not a thorn.

A tooth.

Little. Curved. Sharp.

The stone under my foot shifts.

I leap back as the whole shell crescent rises.

Not stone. A flat head lifts from a seam I missed. Two eyes open sideways. Its mouth spreads, rowed with small black teeth.

It snaps where my ankle was.

The sound is soft.

That softness frightens me more than a roar.

I scramble down the spine of rock, skinning one elbow, and land hard in wet leaves. The shell-backed creature does not chase. It settles again, folds its eyes away, and becomes part of the stone.

The island laughs without making a sound.

I stand with my heart hammering and my elbow bleeding, and I learn another law.

Not every trap hides in mud.

Some wait in beautiful places.

When the rain comes, it comes like a roof collapsing.

One breath the jungle is dripping. The next it is roaring. Water hammers the leaves, the mud, my head, the stone beside me. I open my mouth and drink what falls clean from the sky. It tastes of leaf and heat and a green bitterness that does not matter because it is water.

Then the rain stops.

Just stops.

The roar becomes dripping again. Steam rises from the ground. In the sudden quiet, I hear something moving through the undergrowth below the rock.

Men would snap branches.

This does not.

I crouch behind the stone and look down through a curtain of wet leaves.

A shape steps into the clearing.

It is not a man.

It walks on two legs, but its arms are too long and its shoulders too high. Black hair hangs wet over a chest broad enough to make a door. Its face is flat and heavy-browed, with pale scars crossing one cheek. In one hand it carries a club made from a root ball hardened by age.

It stops beside the dead boy.

The snake above him lifts its head.

For a moment nothing moves.

Then the creature bares its teeth and makes a sound so low I feel it in the stone under my hand.

The snake slides backward into leaves.

The creature crouches by the body. It sniffs the dead boy's neck, his hands, the torn cloth where I took the meat. Then its head turns.

Toward me.

I hold still.

The creature's nostrils flare.

It knows I am here.

My hand closes around a stone. Smooth, wet, too small. Not a weapon. But anything is a weapon if the enemy does not expect it.

The creature rises.

Behind it, the jungle shifts.

Two smaller shapes appear among the leaves. Then another. Watching. Waiting. Not animals, not men, but a people of some kind. A family, maybe. A hunting party.

The large one looks up the slope, straight through the rain-bright leaves, and meets my eyes.

Fear moves through me.

Not panic. Panic spends too fast.

The large one makes a sound.

Not a roar. Not a word I know.

The shape of it is almost a question.

I show my teeth.

The creature's face changes. The mouth pulls back, not quite a smile. Not kindness. Interest.

Kindness can be begged from, sometimes. Cruelty can be predicted. Interest does experiments.

I lift the stone.

My arm shakes now. Not fever. Exhaustion. The creature sees it. Of course it does.

It takes one step uphill.

I throw.

The stone strikes its shoulder and falls away.

The smaller ones behind it flinch. The large one looks at the place where the stone hit, then back at me.

It laughs.

The sound is enormous.

The jungle goes quiet around it.

I run.

Not down. Never toward the water unless the land gives no other choice. I run along the ridge, through dripping leaves, over roots slick as guts. Pain tears through my ribs. My lungs burn. Something behind me crashes once through brush, then stops.

It is not chasing.

That makes me more afraid.

Creatures chase when they fear losing you.

Creatures that know the land let the land chase for them.

The ridge narrows. Ahead, a fallen tree bridges a black cut in the earth where water runs far below. The trunk is wet and furred with moss. Too narrow. Too slick.

Behind me, the leaves move.

I step onto the trunk.

Halfway across, my boot slides.

For one breath I am back on the ship. Floor tilting. Hands grabbing. A mouth laughing above me.

No.

I drop to my knees, hug the trunk, and pull myself forward. Bark rips skin from my palms. The cut below breathes cold up at me, the only cold in this whole green world.

I reach the far side and roll into mud.

The large creature stands at the other end of the trunk.

It could follow.

It does not.

It watches me with those dark, unreadable eyes. Then it touches its own chest with one huge hand and makes a sound.

Slow. Deliberate.

Not for the others.

For me.

I cannot understand it.

The creature touches its chest again and repeats the sound.

A name, maybe.

Names are dangerous. Names are hooks. Names are proof that a life exists outside your fear of it.

I keep mine behind my teeth.

The creature waits.

Rainwater runs down its scarred face. The smaller shapes gather behind it, restless, murmuring in throats too deep for their bodies.

I should run again.

I stay.

The creature does not cross. It could have killed me and did not. Behind me the jungle goes on forever, and I am a boy with no knife, no fire, no tribe, and one hidden name.

The large creature turns away first.

It gives a short command to the others. They melt back into the green, carrying the dead boy between them. Food, maybe. Warning, maybe. Law.

The large one looks at me once more before the leaves take it.

This time the sound it makes is lower.

I hear no threat in it.

That does not mean there is none.

When it is gone, I stay on my knees in the mud with my bleeding hands under me and my name locked behind my teeth.

The jungle drips.

Somewhere below, brown water closes over something struggling.

I have survived the first day, or part of it.

The place has noticed.

Alone was simple. Now there are tracks in the mud that turn where I turn, and the stone I threw is still missing from my hand.
